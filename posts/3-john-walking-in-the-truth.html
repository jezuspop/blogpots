3 John: Walking in the Truth

Gaius, Diotrephes, and Demetrius show how truth shapes hospitality, influence, and the way a church treats its people.

Passage and Book Study

Read alongside: 3 John

Big idea

Walk in the truth by supporting faithful workers, practicing hospitable love, and refusing the pride that seeks control.

Third John shows that truth is not merely something Christians confess. Truth becomes visible in how we live, whom we welcome, and how we use influence in the church.

Snapshot

  • Author: “The elder,” traditionally identified as the apostle John
  • Recipient: Gaius, a beloved Christian known for faithful hospitality
  • Setting: Traveling Christian workers needed lodging, provision, and trustworthy local partners. Diotrephes resisted John’s authority and expelled believers who welcomed them.
  • Purpose: Commend Gaius, confront the pattern represented by Diotrephes, and recommend Demetrius
  • Key words: truth, love, faithful, brothers and sisters, testimony, welcome, good, evil
  • Key verse: The elder urges believers to imitate good, not evil; conduct reveals whether people know God (v. 11).

Simple outline

  1. A life aligned with truth (vv. 1–4)
  2. Hospitality that partners with truth (vv. 5–8)
  3. Pride that obstructs truth (vv. 9–10)
  4. Examples reveal whom to imitate (vv. 11–12)
  5. Truth seeks face-to-face fellowship (vv. 13–15)

The three men

Gaius: truth embodied through hospitality

  • John loves him “in truth” (v. 1).
  • His soul is flourishing, and his conduct confirms his confession (vv. 2–4).
  • He faithfully welcomes traveling believers, even though they were strangers to him (vv. 5–6).
  • By sending them onward “in a manner worthy of God,” he becomes a coworker with the truth (vv. 6–8).

Lesson: Supporting faithful ministry is participation in faithful ministry.

Diotrephes: pride embodied through control

  • He “loves to have first place” (v. 9).
  • He refuses John’s authority and attacks him with malicious words (vv. 9–10).
  • He refuses to welcome the brothers, stops others from doing so, and expels them from the church (v. 10).

Lesson: The decisive problem is not merely a difficult personality. His desire for preeminence damages truth, fellowship, mission, and the church’s treatment of faithful people.

Demetrius: goodness confirmed by testimony

  • Everyone speaks well of him (v. 12).
  • “The truth itself” bears witness to the integrity of his life (v. 12).
  • John adds his own trustworthy testimony (v. 12).

Lesson: Christian character should withstand examination from the community, the truth, and mature spiritual witnesses.

Major themes

1

Truth must be walked

John rejoices that his children are “walking in truth” (vv. 3–4). Truth is both the apostolic message and the life shaped by that message. Compare John 14:6; 17:17; 1 John 2:3–6.

2

Hospitality can be gospel partnership

The traveling workers went out “for the sake of the Name” and did not depend on pagan patrons (v. 7). Supporting such workers makes believers “coworkers with the truth” (v. 8). Compare Matthew 10:40–42; Romans 12:13; Hebrews 13:2.

Hospitality here is not indiscriminate endorsement. Second John warns against supporting deceivers; Third John commands support for faithful workers. Christian love welcomes with discernment.

3

Leadership is revealed by its use of power

Diotrephes uses influence to elevate himself, silence correction, block hospitality, and exclude dissenters. Compare Mark 10:42–45; 1 Peter 5:2–3.

4

Imitation is spiritually consequential

Verse 11 places the conflict inside the larger moral contrast between good and evil. John does not tell Gaius merely to recognize the difference, but to imitate the good. Compare 1 Corinthians 11:1; Philippians 3:17; Hebrews 13:7.

5

Testimony matters

The letter repeatedly appeals to observable witness: reports about Gaius, Diotrephes’s conduct, and the testimony concerning Demetrius. Claims of faith are tested by patterns of life. Compare Matthew 7:16–20; 1 Timothy 3:7.

Interpretive cautions

  • “I pray that you may prosper” (v. 2) is a conventional personal greeting, not a promise that every faithful Christian will be wealthy or physically healthy.
  • Diotrephes’s precise office is not stated. He clearly holds influence, but calling him an elder, preacher, or bishop goes beyond the text.
  • The travelers’ exact role is debated. They were likely itinerant Christian missionaries or teachers, but John does not give a formal title.
  • Demetrius may have carried the letter, as many commentators infer from John’s recommendation, but the text does not explicitly say so.
  • “Has not seen God” (v. 11) describes a life fundamentally unacquainted with God, not sinless perfection as the proof of conversion.

Reflection Questions

  • Does my public confession of truth match my private and relational conduct?
  • Do I treat hospitality and material support as real participation in gospel work?
  • When correction comes, do I listen or protect my position?
  • Does my leadership create room for faithful people, or make everything orbit me?
  • Whose character am I imitating?
  • If the truth itself testified about my life, what would it say?

One-sentence takeaway

Third John teaches that truth becomes visible through humble character, discerning hospitality, gospel partnership, and the refusal to let pride rule Christ’s church.
