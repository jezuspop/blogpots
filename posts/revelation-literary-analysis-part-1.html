Lecture-Based Bible Study Cheat Sheet

Key Thought Revelation must be read as a genuine literary composition, a Christian apocalypse, a prophetic word, and a pastoral letter at the same time. John uses the symbolic world of Israel’s Scriptures, but he reshapes Jewish apocalyptic around the decisive claim that the Messiah has already come, has been raised, and has begun God’s promised reign. The book therefore summons Christians to faithful endurance in a hostile world rather than merely giving them a timetable of future events.

Introduction

Dr. Robert Mulholland’s second lecture moves from the four broad approaches to Revelation into the book’s literary and historical world. His concern is methodological: interpreters should use literary analysis and comparison with ancient writings, but neither method should reduce Revelation to a patchwork of sources or dismiss John’s claim to visionary experience.

The lecture develops five major questions:

  1. How should literary and comparative analysis serve the interpretation of Revelation?
  2. What is an apocalyptic worldview, and where did Jewish apocalyptic arise?
  3. How does Christian apocalypse differ from earlier Jewish apocalyptic expectation?
  4. How does Revelation 1:1–9 construct the narrative world of the whole book?
  5. What can responsibly be said about authorship and date?

Mulholland’s pastoral destination is expressed in his memorable summary:

Revelation is a call to radical discipleship as faithful citizens of God’s New Jerusalem in the midst of a fallen Babylon world.

That sentence is the interpretive center of the lecture.

Mulholland’s Argument in Seven Movements

  1. Literary analysis is necessary but limited. John produced a crafted text, yet literary theories should not automatically erase the visionary experience behind it.
  2. Comparison must honor Revelation’s dominant symbolic world. Hellenistic parallels can illuminate words and customs, but Revelation’s controlling images arise overwhelmingly from Israel’s Scriptures and Jewish apocalyptic.
  3. Apocalyptic unveils the spiritual dimension of history. Events in the visible world participate in a larger conflict and purpose located in God’s realm.
  4. Jewish apocalyptic grew among subjected and marginalized people. It expressed hope that God would intervene, overthrow oppressive powers, restore his covenant people, and inaugurate the age to come.
  5. Christian apocalyptic introduces an already/not-yet transformation. In Jesus, the age to come has already invaded the present age, though the final consummation remains future.
  6. Revelation 1:1–9 supplies the narrative grammar of the book. Messiah, servants, witness, resurrection, kingdom, priesthood, tribulation, and endurance establish the world in which every later vision must be heard.
  7. Theology must become discipleship. Those who belong to the kingdom will experience pressure from Babylon and must answer it with patient, Christlike witness.

Literary Analysis

Its legitimate role

Revelation is not a random transcript. John selects, arranges, repeats, and interprets images for an audience. Literary analysis can help readers notice:

  • introductions and conclusions;
  • repeated phrases and changing formulas;
  • patterns of sevens;
  • contrasts such as New Jerusalem and Babylon;
  • characterization of God, the Lamb, the dragon, beasts, saints, and nations;
  • allusions to earlier Scripture;
  • the interaction of apocalypse, prophecy, and letter.

Mulholland’s warning

Mulholland rejects an extreme literary approach that treats the book merely as separately composed fragments later assembled by an editor. He names R. H. Charles as a prominent historical example. The problem is not source analysis itself, but reductionism: a method becomes distorting when it assumes beforehand that no visionary experience occurred.

A balanced principle

The choice is not between inspiration and literary artistry. A visionary experience still had to be communicated through human language. John’s theological understanding, scriptural memory, rhetorical choices, and pastoral purpose shape the written form.

Interpretive Rule Study Revelation as crafted literature without treating literary craft as evidence against genuine revelation.

Comparative Studies

Comparative study asks how Revelation resembles other documents in its ancient environment. Mulholland distinguishes two possible worlds of comparison:

  • Roman-Hellenistic culture: useful for imperial ideology, civic religion, economics, rhetoric, and local customs.
  • Jewish Scripture and apocalyptic: the principal reservoir for Revelation’s genres, symbols, visions, angels, beasts, throne scenes, covenant language, and hopes.

Mulholland argues that extended parallels to large sections of Revelation are much easier to find in Jewish literature than in Greco-Roman literature. A Roman phrase or custom may clarify a detail, but Israel’s scriptural imagination governs the book as a whole.

Teaching caution

This should not be turned into an either/or choice. Revelation’s imagery is predominantly scriptural and Jewish, yet the seven churches lived within Roman Asia. John uses biblical symbols to unveil the theological character of Roman power. Babylon is a scriptural name deployed against a present imperial reality.

What Is Apocalyptic?

Mulholland distinguishes apocalypticism, a worldview, from an apocalypse, a literary disclosure.

Apocalyptic worldview

The visible order exists within a larger spiritual reality. What occurs in God’s heavenly realm bears upon earthly history. Apocalyptic refuses the claim that empire, violence, wealth, and death exhaust what is real.

Apocalypse as disclosure

The Greek word apokalypsis means an unveiling or disclosure. God reveals what human beings could not discover merely by observing surface events. The disclosure commonly includes:

  • a human recipient;
  • a vision or heavenly journey;
  • an angelic interpreter or messenger;
  • symbolic images;
  • conflict between the present order and God’s purpose;
  • divine judgment and deliverance;
  • assurance to a pressured community.

Social location

Apocalyptic often emerges among people who are oppressed or marginalized. Jewish apocalyptic developed through extended subjection to Babylonian, Persian, Greek, Seleucid, and Roman power. Some faithful Jews also felt marginalized by Jewish elites who cooperated with imperial rulers.

Apocalyptic is therefore not escapist fantasy. It contests the oppressor’s account of reality. The empire says, “We rule.” Apocalypse answers, “God reigns, judges, and will vindicate faithful witness.”

Origins of Jewish Apocalyptic

Persian influence

Mulholland allows for some influence from Persian religion, especially Zoroastrianism. He nevertheless stresses that Jewish apocalyptic does not embrace full metaphysical dualism. God and Satan are not equal and eternal rivals, and matter is not intrinsically evil.

Prophetic roots

His primary explanation is that apocalyptic extends Israel’s prophetic tradition.

Prophetic emphasis Apocalyptic emphasis
Calls God’s people to covenant faithfulness in the present Encourages God’s people under prolonged domination
Sees God working within ongoing history Anticipates a decisive divine intervention
Warns of historical consequences for sin Portrays the end of the present evil order
Envisions restoration through God’s historical action Contrasts this age with the age to come

This contrast is useful but should not be made absolute. The prophets also envision cosmic intervention, and apocalypses also summon present faithfulness. Daniel itself is both prophetic and apocalyptic.

The Christian Twist

Jewish apocalyptic generally looked forward to the restoration of Israel’s kingdom. Christian apocalypse announces that the decisive event has already occurred in Jesus’ death and resurrection.

Jewish expectation

The typical pattern is:

present evil age → divine intervention/Messiah → restored kingdom or age to come

Jesus and the early church

Jesus announces that God’s kingdom is already present while evil still operates. The wheat and weeds grow together until the harvest (Matthew 13:24–30, 36–43). The New Testament therefore presents an overlapping of the ages:

present age + inaugurated age to come → final consummation

Revelation’s transformation

John does not merely repeat older apocalyptic expectations:

  • the Messiah has come;
  • the faithful martyr has conquered through death;
  • resurrection has begun in the “firstborn from the dead”;
  • God’s people have already been made a kingdom and priests;
  • tribulation and kingdom coexist;
  • final victory remains future.

Key Point Revelation looks forward from the resurrection, not merely forward to a Messiah. Daniel and Revelation share symbolic territory, but they do not stand at the same point in redemptive history.

Daniel and Revelation

Mulholland strongly warns against making Daniel a simple decoding key for Revelation. Daniel anticipates the coming kingdom; John proclaims that its inauguration has occurred in Christ.

This warning is valuable if it prevents interpreters from flattening both books into one chronological chart. It needs one qualification: Revelation intentionally reuses Daniel’s visions. Daniel is indispensable as a source text, but its images are reread through the Lamb’s death, resurrection, and reign.

The right sequence is:

  1. Understand Daniel’s image in its own context.
  2. Observe how John adopts and modifies it.
  3. Interpret that modification through Christ’s victory.
  4. Ask what allegiance it requires from John’s churches.

The Narrative World of Revelation 1:1–9

Narrative criticism asks what world a text creates for its readers. Revelation opens with three introductions:

  1. Apocalyptic/prohetic introduction — 1:1–3
  2. Epistolary introduction — 1:4–8
  3. Personal introduction — 1:9

Before the first major vision begins, John has already established the identities, conflicts, and hopes that govern the book.

Anchor Text 1: Revelation 1:1–3

“The revelation of Jesus Christ”

Apokalypsis Iēsou Christou can grammatically mean a revelation from Jesus, about Jesus, or both. In the book it is both: Jesus mediates the revelation and is its center.

“Christ” means Messiah, locating the narrative in Israel’s hope. John identifies Jesus as the promised anointed king and immediately gives Jewish expectation a Christian center.

“Which God gave him”

The chain of communication runs from God through Jesus and the angel to John and the servants. This resembles apocalyptic mediation while grounding the message in God’s authority.

“To show his servants”

“Servants” recalls Israel as Yahweh’s covenant people. Revelation applies covenant vocation to the multinational people who follow the Lamb.

“What must soon take place”

Mulholland emphasizes divine necessity: history is not ultimately controlled by Babylon. He proposes that en tachei may emphasize events occurring in their proper order rather than temporal nearness.

That lexical proposal should be treated cautiously. Most lexicons and translations understand en tachei as “soon,” “quickly,” or “without delay.” The stronger interpretive point is that Revelation combines urgency with God’s sovereign necessity; its first readers were to hear the book as relevant to their situation, not as information sealed off for a remote age.

“Blessed … hear … keep”

Revelation aims at obedient hearing. The blessing is not promised to the person who solves every symbol but to the congregation that receives and keeps the prophetic word.

Anchor Text 2: Revelation 1:4–8

A real letter

“John to the seven churches that are in Asia” keeps apocalypse from floating above history. The visions address concrete congregations facing compromise, fear, seduction, and pressure.

The one who is, was, and is coming

John’s unusual Greek may preserve the divine name’s form and force. Mulholland suggests the grammatical roughness is deliberate and alerts readers to watch the formula. Later, “who is to come” disappears (11:17; 16:5 in the critical text), signaling that God’s coming reign has moved from expectation into visionary realization.

Jesus’ titles

Revelation 1:5 calls Jesus:

  • the faithful witness/martyr — he reveals God through costly obedience;
  • the firstborn from the dead — his resurrection inaugurates the promised restoration;
  • the ruler of the kings of the earth — Caesar’s rule is relativized beneath the Messiah.

Freed from sins by his blood

In Jewish restoration hope, the people’s sin must be addressed. John announces that Jesus has dealt with sin through his sacrificial death.

A kingdom and priests

Revelation 1:6 echoes Exodus 19:5–6. God’s covenant purpose is being fulfilled in the Lamb’s people. Priesthood here is a vocation: the church mediates God’s presence and bears faithful witness among the nations.

Coming with the clouds

The cloud-coming image draws on Daniel 7:13–14. John holds inauguration and consummation together: the Son of Man has received royal authority, yet his reign will be universally manifested.

Anchor Text 3: Revelation 1:9

John identifies himself as their brother and fellow participant in three realities:

  1. Tribulation (thlipsis)
  2. Kingdom (basileia)
  3. Patient endurance (hypomonē)

The kingdom at the center

The order matters pastorally. The kingdom is not postponed until after tribulation. John and his readers already share it. Because they belong to God’s kingdom, Babylon pressures them; because they suffer that pressure, they need endurance.

Kingdom allegiance → tribulation from Babylon → patient endurance in Jesus

Tribulation in Revelation

Mulholland’s observation is crucial: John speaks of tribulation as a present shared reality, not only as a short end-time period. Revelation may include final crisis, but its theology of tribulation first concerns the cost of faithful discipleship in every imperial setting.

Patmos and witness

John is on Patmos “because of the word of God and the testimony of Jesus.” Whether this means banishment or missionary presence is debated, but Mulholland understands it as exile resulting from faithful witness.

Word of God and Witness of Jesus

This phrase recurs as a description of faithful believers. Mulholland interprets its two halves as being and doing:

  • Word of God: God’s self-communication shaping the inner person in Christ.
  • Witness of Jesus: Christlike life manifested publicly, even at cost.

He connects this with Colossians 1:27 and 3:16: Christ dwelling within forms a life that bears Christ’s character outwardly.

The phrase can carry more than one nuance—testimony borne by Jesus, testimony about Jesus, or the prophetic witness Jesus gives through his servants. The theological center remains stable: Revelation forms communities whose identity and conduct testify to the Lamb.

Authorship

The author names himself John but does not explicitly say “John the apostle.” Mulholland surveys three principal proposals:

  • John the apostle;
  • John the elder;
  • another otherwise unknown Christian prophet named John.

Case against apostolic authorship

Revelation’s Greek differs significantly from the Gospel and Letters of John. Its syntax can be rough or unconventional.

Mulholland’s case for apostolic authorship

He argues that some irregularities are intentional or result from language being stretched by visionary reality. He also sees Johannine links:

  • Jesus as Lamb;
  • Jesus associated with a door;
  • dynamic belief “into” Jesus in the Gospel;
  • shared emphasis on indwelling life and witness.

Responsible conclusion

The lecture favors John the apostle, but the evidence is not conclusive. Similar theology and imagery may indicate common authorship, a Johannine circle, or shared early Christian tradition. Interpretation should acknowledge the named author John without making disputed apostolic identity carry more weight than the text can bear.

Date and Occasion

Mulholland favors a date in the late 60s, near Nero’s persecution and the civil turmoil following his death. He argues:

  • Nero initiated official persecution of Christians in Rome;
  • the churches appear threatened, though widespread persecution in Asia has not fully erupted;
  • Revelation 17’s kings may fit first-century emperors around Nero and the year of the four emperors;
  • imagery of a divided city may reflect Roman civil war;
  • the book does not clearly indicate that Jerusalem’s temple has already been destroyed.

The other major view

Many scholars date Revelation around AD 95 during Domitian’s reign, drawing especially on early Christian testimony associated with Irenaeus and the setting of imperial pressure in Asia Minor.

Critical evaluation

Neither date is certain. Several cautions are needed:

  • Emperor-counting in Revelation 17 changes depending on where the count begins.
  • Nero’s persecution was severe but geographically centered in Rome; evidence for a coordinated empire-wide persecution is limited.
  • Domitianic pressure also should not be exaggerated into a uniform empire-wide campaign.
  • Revelation may address social, economic, civic, and religious coercion rather than only formal state executions.
  • Silence about the temple’s destruction cannot by itself establish a pre-70 date because symbolic temple imagery continues after AD 70.

The safest classroom conclusion is that both Neronic and Domitianic settings remain credible. More important than choosing a date is recognizing Revelation’s confrontation with Roman imperial power and assimilation.

Key Greek Words

ἀποκάλυψις (apokalypsis) — Revelation 1:1

Unveiling, disclosure, revelation. The book reveals the divine reality hidden beneath appearances.

Χριστός (Christos) — Revelation 1:1–2

Anointed one, Messiah. This term locates Jesus within Israel’s kingdom hope.

δεῖ (dei) — Revelation 1:1

It is necessary, it must happen. The word expresses divine purpose rather than fatalistic inevitability.

ἐν τάχει (en tachei) — Revelation 1:1

Soon, quickly, without delay. Mulholland proposes an emphasis on ordered necessity; this is creative but lexically disputed.

μαρτυρία / μάρτυς (martyria / martys) — Revelation 1:2, 5, 9

Testimony/witness and witness. The language develops toward costly witness and martyrdom.

θλῖψις (thlipsis) — Revelation 1:9

Pressure, affliction, tribulation. A present reality shared by John and the churches.

βασιλεία (basileia) — Revelation 1:6, 9

Kingdom or reign. God’s people already participate in the reign inaugurated by Jesus.

ὑπομονή (hypomonē) — Revelation 1:9

Steadfastness, endurance. Not passive resignation but durable faithfulness under pressure.

Key Themes

  • Heaven unveils the true meaning of earthly history.
  • Jesus transforms Israel’s restoration hope through the cross and resurrection.
  • The kingdom is already present but not yet consummated.
  • The church is a kingdom of priests called to witness.
  • Babylon’s pressure is answered by endurance, not Babylonian violence.
  • Revelation’s purpose is obedient discipleship, not speculative mastery.
  • Literary artistry and visionary revelation are complementary.
  • Original audience and canonical horizon belong together.

Theological Evaluation

What Mulholland protects well

  1. The supernatural horizon: Revelation cannot be confined to Enlightenment assumptions about what is possible.
  2. Literary responsibility: visionary origin does not excuse careless reading of the final text.
  3. Scriptural context: Revelation’s images are governed principally by Israel’s Scriptures.
  4. Christological transformation: the Lamb’s death and resurrection change how apocalyptic victory is imagined.
  5. Inaugurated eschatology: kingdom, tribulation, and endurance coexist in the church’s present life.
  6. Pastoral purpose: Revelation calls hearers to keep the word, not merely understand it.

Where qualification is needed

  1. Prophecy and apocalypse overlap more than the lecture’s contrast suggests. Both address present covenant faithfulness and future divine action.
  2. Persian influence is debated. Similarity does not establish direct borrowing, and developments may arise internally from Israel’s traditions.
  3. Roman-Hellenistic context matters substantially. Jewish symbols are used to critique Roman imperial worship, commerce, violence, and civic identity.
  4. The meaning of en tachei should not be built on the root of tassō. Immediate usage and standard lexical evidence favor “soon/quickly.”
  5. Authorship remains open. The lecture’s apostolic case is plausible but not demonstrable.
  6. The early date is possible, not settled. The late date remains the majority position in much contemporary scholarship.

Class-Ready Summary

Mulholland’s central claim is that Revelation belongs to Jewish apocalyptic but cannot be squeezed into its older expectations. Jewish apocalypse looked forward to God restoring the kingdom; John announces that restoration has already begun through Jesus, the faithful martyr and firstborn from the dead. Revelation 1:1–9 establishes this Christian narrative world before the visions unfold. God’s people are already a kingdom and priests. Their allegiance exposes them to Babylon’s pressure, and their vocation is patient endurance and faithful witness. Literary and historical study help us hear this message, but the final goal is transformed discipleship.

Discussion Questions

  1. What does apocalyptic reveal that ordinary political analysis cannot?
  2. How does the resurrection of Jesus change the shape of Jewish restoration hope?
  3. Why is the kingdom placed between tribulation and endurance in Revelation 1:9?
  4. Where do modern Christians feel pressure to accept Babylon’s account of reality?
  5. How can we use literary analysis without explaining away divine revelation?
  6. What changes when “tribulation” is first understood as John’s present experience?
  7. How does a kingdom of priests conquer differently from an earthly empire?
  8. Does our study of Revelation produce more faithful witness or merely more opinions?

Suggested Class Structure

60-minute lesson

  1. Opening and review — 5 minutes
    Connect the four interpretive approaches from Lesson 1 with the need to identify genre.
  2. Apocalyptic worldview — 10 minutes
    Explain disclosure, spiritual reality, marginalization, and hope.
  3. The Christian twist — 10 minutes
    Draw the “this age/age to come” diagrams and show the overlap inaugurated by Jesus.
  4. Close reading of Revelation 1:1–8 — 15 minutes
    Trace Messiah, servants, witness, resurrection, kingdom, and priesthood.
  5. The kingdom in the middle — 10 minutes
    Work through tribulation, kingdom, and endurance in 1:9.
  6. Application and discussion — 10 minutes
    Ask what faithful citizenship in New Jerusalem looks like inside today’s Babylonian pressures.
