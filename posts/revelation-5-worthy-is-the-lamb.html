Revelation 5: Worthy Is the Lamb

The Lion is revealed as the slain Lamb. Follow the scroll, the worship in heaven, and what makes Jesus worthy.

Big Idea

The crucified and risen Jesus alone is worthy to carry out God’s purposes. His blood gathers a people for God, and his victory summons all creation to worship.

Lesson sentence, original framing: The future belongs to the Lamb, and so do we.

Central question: Who is worthy to open the scroll, and what makes him worthy?

Central discovery: John hears of a victorious Lion, then sees a slaughtered yet standing Lamb. These are not competing messiahs. The Lamb reveals the character of the Lion’s victory.

1. Context: Before the Seals, See the Savior

Literary context

  • Chapters 1–3: Jesus addresses actual churches, exposing compromise and encouraging endurance. The promises to those who conquer prepare us to ask what conquering means.
  • Chapter 4: God reigns as Creator. Heavenly worship recognizes his worthiness because all things exist by his will (4:11).
  • Chapter 5: The throne-room vision continues. The Lamb is acclaimed as worthy because of his redemptive death (5:9). Creation and redemption belong together.
  • Chapter 6: The Lamb begins opening the seals. The judgments must be read in light of the identity of the one who opens them.
  • Later in Revelation: The saints conquer through the Lamb’s blood and faithful testimony (12:11); the Lamb overcomes hostile kings (17:14); God’s people finally share his reign (22:1–5).

Teaching emphasis: Do not rush past chapter 5 to get to the horsemen. John introduces the one entrusted with the scroll before showing the events associated with its opening.

Historical context

The seven churches lived in Roman Asia amid civic religion, imperial claims, economic pressures, and differing experiences of hostility or accommodation. Chapters 2–3 show that suffering and compromise were both real concerns. Do not assume every church faced identical conditions or that every Christian was under a uniform empire-wide death sentence.

The worship of God and the Lamb challenges every rival claim to ultimate allegiance. Rome supplies the immediate imperial setting; modern applications should expose idolatry without pretending the passage names a contemporary political figure.

Three distinctions to preserve

  1. Text: What John explicitly sees, hears, or explains.
  2. Interpretation: What the imagery most likely means in its literary and biblical setting.
  3. Application: How that meaning calls us to trust, repent, worship, and live differently.

2. Revelation 5:1–4: The Closed Scroll and the Open Wound

What the text says

The enthroned one holds a scroll with writing on both sides, sealed with seven seals. A mighty angel asks who is worthy to open it. No one in heaven, on earth, or under the earth can do so. John weeps intensely.

What is the scroll?

Best working interpretation: It represents God’s sovereign purpose for judgment and salvation, entrusted to the Lamb to disclose and bring to fulfillment. Its precise identification is debated; the chapter does not give it a title.

  • Ezekiel 2:9–10: A scroll written on both sides contains lamentation and judgment. This is the strongest direct background for the physical description.
  • Daniel 12:4, 9: A sealed book concerns the appointed fulfillment of God’s purposes. The connection is thematic, not proof that the two books are identical.
  • Revelation 6–8: Breaking seals is associated with unfolding events, supporting more than the idea of Jesus providing private information.

Other proposals: A testament or inheritance document; a covenant document; a title deed connected with the recovery of creation. These may illuminate aspects of the symbol, but none should be announced as the scroll’s explicit biblical label. In particular, “the title deed to the earth” is an interpretation, not a quotation from Revelation 5.

What do the seals communicate?

They emphasize that the scroll is secured and cannot be opened without the proper authority. Seven commonly communicates completeness in Revelation. Avoid building an elaborate lesson around the exact mechanics of seven wax seals: the vision does not supply enough physical detail for certainty.

Why does John weep?

Text: No one worthy has been found.

Inference: The crisis concerns the fulfillment of God’s purpose, not merely John’s frustration at missing secret information. If no one can open the scroll, where is the hope of justice and redemption?

The angel is mighty, but strength alone does not answer the question of worthiness. The scene dramatizes the inability of created beings to supply what the Lamb alone supplies. It does not portray God as surprised or defeated.

Pastoral application: Some tears arise because evil seems stronger than our ability to confront it. The answer here is not that the pain is imaginary. The answer is the identity and victory of Jesus.

Original teaching line: Our inability to open the future does not mean the future is abandoned.

Questions to Consider

  • What makes John weep, and how is that different from curiosity about the future?
  • Where are we expecting human strength, influence, or knowledge to do what only Christ can do?
  • How can we acknowledge suffering without granting suffering the final word?

3. Revelation 5:5–7: Turn Around and See

The Lion and the Root

The elder announces a conqueror identified by two royal, scriptural titles:

  • Lion of the tribe of Judah: Genesis 49:9–10 joins Judah’s lion imagery to kingship and the scepter.
  • Root of David: Isaiah 11:1, 10 supplies the Davidic hope associated with Jesse’s line. Revelation 22:16 later calls Jesus both David’s root and offspring. Here the immediate point is messianic identity, not a botanical diagram or a complete argument about preexistence.

The elder says the victory has been won. The Lamb’s taking of the scroll is grounded in an accomplished victory, not merely a prediction that he may someday prevail.

John hears Lion, then sees Lamb

The sequence interprets the royal announcement. Jesus is the promised conqueror, but the vision makes his sacrificial death central to understanding his conquest.

“The idea is that the promise of the lion-like ruler from Judah is fulfilled in the promise of the slaughtered Lamb.”

Craig R. Koester, “Commentary on Revelation 5:1–13,” Working Preacher, August 15, 2021.

Why this matters: Do not teach that Jesus abandoned being a Lamb so that he could finally become powerful. The Lamb himself is worthy and victorious. Neither should we erase judgment from his kingship: the Lamb opens the seals, and Revelation 6:16–17 speaks of his wrath.

Slaughtered, yet standing

  • Slaughtered: His death was real and remains essential to his identity. The description does not imply that the crucifixion merely appeared to happen.
  • Standing: The slain one is alive and active. This is resurrection-shaped imagery.
  • At the center of the throne-room scene: He is not a peripheral spectator. He approaches the enthroned one and takes the scroll.

The Passover lamb (Exodus 12), the suffering servant led like a lamb to slaughter (Isaiah 53:7), and the wider sacrificial world of Scripture provide complementary backgrounds. No single detail requires reducing the image to only one Old Testament passage.

“The conquering Lion and the conquered Lamb are one and the same.”

Sarah Hinlicky Wilson, “Commentary on Revelation 5:1–13,” Working Preacher, August 10, 2025.

Qualification: “Conquered” describes the apparent defeat of the slain victim. In John’s vision, that death does not have the final verdict: the Lamb stands, acts, and is worshiped.

Seven horns, seven eyes

Image Strongest interpretive direction Biblical connections
Horns Royal strength and authority; seven emphasizes fullness Daniel 7–8; Psalm 89:17, 24
Eyes Complete sight and worldwide divine agency Zechariah 3:9; 4:10
Seven spirits sent into all the earth Commonly understood as the fullness of God’s Spirit in worldwide activity Revelation 1:4; 3:1; 4:5; Zechariah 4:6–10

Interpretive alternative: Some understand the seven spirits as heavenly spirits or angels. The repeated placement of the seven spirits in Revelation’s divine setting and the Zechariah background favor the sevenfold-Spirit reading, but the identification is debated. Do not teach seven separate Holy Spirits.

The imagery is symbolic without being unreal: it communicates a real Lord’s power, perception, and presence. It is not an anatomical description intended to make Jesus grotesque.

Taking the scroll

The gesture presents the Lamb as authorized to carry out God’s purposes. Daniel 7:9–14 offers a significant parallel: the heavenly court, the approach to the enthroned one, and the bestowal of dominion.

Original teaching line: The marks of sacrifice do not disqualify Jesus from the throne; they reveal the victory heaven celebrates.

Questions to Consider

  • What expectations does “Lion” create, and how does seeing the Lamb reshape them?
  • Where do our methods of winning contradict the Lord we claim to follow?
  • How does a slain yet standing Savior speak to people who feel that faithfulness has cost them dearly?

4. Revelation 5:8–10: Bought for God, Made for Service

Worship and the prayers of the saints

The living creatures and elders fall before the Lamb. Harps and golden bowls of incense accompany the scene. John explicitly identifies the incense with the prayers of the saints.

  • Saints: God’s holy people, not an elite class of unusually impressive Christians.
  • Prayer: The church’s prayers belong in this heavenly scene. Compare Psalm 141:2 and Revelation 8:3–4.
  • Application: What appears weak or unnoticed on earth is not absent from God’s presence. This does not promise an immediate answer on our timetable.

The identities of the twenty-four elders remain debated: representatives of God’s people, heavenly council figures, or imagery combining priestly and representative associations. Do not make a precise identification essential to the lesson.

The new song answers the angel’s question

Verse 9 supplies the reason for the Lamb’s worthiness: he was slain and purchased people for God through his blood. The chapter itself interprets the death as redemptive, not simply an inspiring example of courage.

Follow the sequence:

  1. The cost: His blood, his life given in death.
  2. The belonging: A people purchased for God.
  3. The breadth: People from every tribe, language, people, and nation.
  4. The identity: A kingdom and priests.
  5. The vocation and promise: Service to God and participation in his reign.

The purchase metaphor declares cost and transferred belonging. It does not identify Satan as the recipient of a payment. Do not press the metaphor into details the passage does not supply.

A people from every people

Redemption is not restricted to one ethnicity, culture, language, or political nation. The repeated fourfold description emphasizes worldwide scope; it is not a modern census classification.

Application: Cross-cultural fellowship and mission are not optional decorations on the church’s life. They correspond to the people the Lamb purchases. Ask who is welcomed, heard, discipled, and equipped to serve, not only who appears in a group photograph.

Guardrail: “From every” does not mean every individual is automatically saved. Revelation also speaks of repentance, endurance, and judgment.

Kingdom and priests

Exodus 19:5–6 provides the covenant background. Compare Revelation 1:5–6 and 1 Peter 2:5, 9.

  • This is the identity of the redeemed community, not merely its vocational clergy.
  • Priestly identity means belonging to God and serving him through worship, holiness, intercession, and witness.
  • Shared reign is a gift under the Lamb’s authority, not permission to dominate neighbors or seize religious control of the state.
  • The church already belongs to Christ’s kingdom (1:6, 9), while Revelation also anticipates the fullness of its reign (22:5). The present and future dimensions must both be heard.

Textual note for mixed-translation classes

KJV/NKJV and many modern translations differ in 5:9–10. The older English tradition has first-person wording such as “us” and “we”; modern critical-text translations typically describe redeemed people in the third person. The manuscript tradition also differs between present and future forms of reigning, with modern versions commonly choosing the future. These differences matter when arguing about who the elders are, but they do not change the central claim: the Lamb redeems people for God and gives them royal-priestly identity. See NET notes on Revelation 5, especially notes 27, 31, and 34.

Original teaching line: Jesus does not merely rescue us from something; he gathers us for God and gives us something holy to do.

Questions to Consider

  • What does being purchased for God change about how we use our time, bodies, money, and influence?
  • Which cultural boundaries do we still treat as more important than our shared belonging to Jesus?
  • How would ministry change if every disciple understood himself or herself as part of a priestly people?

5. Revelation 5:11–14: The Song Gets Bigger

Expanding circles of praise

Living creatures and elders → countless angels → every creature.

The vision moves outward until no realm of creation is omitted. The enormous angelic number conveys overwhelming multitude, not an invitation to calculate heaven’s population. Daniel 7:10 supplies an important background.

The sevenfold acclaim in verse 12

Power, riches, wisdom, strength, honor, glory, and blessing express the comprehensive worthiness of the Lamb. The song does not make Jesus powerful by human contribution; it recognizes the honor that is his due.

Teaching exercise: Read the list slowly. Ask which of these our culture most often attaches to celebrity, money, office, or military strength. Then ask what changes when the slain Lamb becomes the center of our admiration.

God and the Lamb receive the praise together

Verse 13 addresses both the enthroned one and the Lamb. They remain distinguishable in the vision, yet share the universal doxology. Read this alongside Revelation 19:10 and 22:8–9, where an angel refuses worship and directs John to God.

Theological conclusion: This is powerful evidence for the divine status of Jesus within Revelation’s worship of the one God. It does not erase the distinction between Father and Son or function as a complete later creedal formulation by itself.

Worship is allegiance

“The clear message is about allegiance: Only God and God’s Lamb Jesus are worthy of our worship”

Barbara Rossing, “Commentary on Revelation 5:11–14,” Working Preacher, April 14, 2013, excerpt.

Rossing foregrounds the challenge to Roman imperial worship. That historical emphasis helps us hear the cost of Christian praise. It need not reduce the chapter to politics: redemption, divine identity, prayer, and the final hope of creation are equally integral.

Does every creature worshiping prove universal salvation? Not by itself. The scene portrays comprehensive acknowledgment and praise. Whether understood as an anticipatory picture of the final outcome or as cosmic heavenly liturgy, it must be read with Revelation’s continuing calls to repentance and its judgment scenes, not against them.

The final response

The living creatures affirm the praise with their Amen; the elders fall down in worship. The chapter’s movement ends with surrender to the one found worthy.

Original teaching line: Heaven’s song asks for more than our voices. It asks for our allegiance.

Questions to Consider

  • Does our worship train us to trust Jesus, or merely help us feel better while our deepest loyalties stay unchanged?
  • What rival claim to our devotion needs a practical refusal this week?
  • What would a congregation shaped by the Lamb’s worthiness look like outside its worship gathering?

Greek Helps Without Word-Study Overreach

  • Axios, worthy: Deserving or fitting to receive the honor and carry out the task. The chapter ties worthiness to the Lamb’s redemptive work.
  • Nikaō, conquer/overcome: Used in 5:5 and in the promises to the churches. Read Christian victory through the Lamb and 12:11.
  • Arnion, lamb: Revelation’s characteristic designation for Jesus. Do not build a doctrine of childishness or weakness merely from the word’s diminutive form.
  • Sphazō, slaughter/slay: Makes the violence of the death visible. The slain Lamb is not merely tired, wounded, or misunderstood.
  • Agorazō, buy/purchase: In 5:9, depicts costly redemption into belonging to God, not human self-improvement.
  • Basileia and hiereis, kingdom and priests: Corporate identity with royal and priestly dimensions. Verse 10 is not merely a promise of individual prestige.

These are contextual lexical helps, not claims that every possible meaning of a Greek word is present here. For technical verification, open Revelation 5 in your Greek text and lexicon in Logos.

Interpretive Perspectives: Where Readers Differ

Scroll and fulfillment

  • First-century-oriented / preterist readings foreground what the vision says to churches confronting ancient imperial power and anticipate judgment in that historical horizon.
  • Futurist readings often connect the scroll and subsequent seals to a concentrated period of end-time judgment.
  • Idealist readings emphasize the Lamb’s sovereignty over the recurring conflict between God’s kingdom and evil throughout the church’s life.
  • Eclectic readings combine the first-century address, ongoing patterns, and final consummation.

These are broad families, not identical systems. Revelation 5 establishes the Lamb’s worthiness more clearly than it settles the calendar of all subsequent visions. Keep the chapter’s explicit confession central even when the class differs about chronology.

Scholarly lenses used here

  • Koester: The Lion’s promise is fulfilled in the Lamb; sacrificial victory and worldwide redemption belong together.
  • Wilson: The apparent contrast between conquest and slaughter must be held within the identity of the same Christ.
  • Rossing: Worship challenges false ultimate allegiances and reaches beyond humanity to the whole creation.

These emphases are complementary, not three competing explanations of every detail. Their linked essays were consulted directly. The synthesis and pastoral applications here remain accountable to the biblical text, not to any commentator’s authority.

Restoration Movement Teaching Connections

Scripture interprets Scripture. Start with Revelation’s own explanations and the Old Testament background rather than a modern prediction chart.

Redemption belongs to Christ. The Lamb’s blood, not our performance, purchases the people of God. Grace creates a life of obedient allegiance; it does not make repentance and discipleship irrelevant.

Baptism is a canonical connection, not an explicit topic in this chapter. If inviting a response to Jesus, connect his death and resurrection with Romans 6:3–4 and the call to repent and be baptized in Acts 2:38. Do not present Revelation 5 itself as a baptism instruction text or suggest that baptism competes with the efficacy of Christ’s blood.

The priesthood belongs to the redeemed community. Equip every disciple for service while honoring the different roles described elsewhere in Scripture.

Heavenly harps and earthly worship: Revelation 5 depicts harps within an apocalyptic throne-room scene and explicitly interprets the incense as prayer. Christians differ on how heavenly worship informs earthly practice. The chapter neither directly commands congregational instruments nor explicitly prohibits them. A broader discussion of worship authority should not be settled by isolating this one symbol.

Common Missteps to Avoid

  1. Treating the scroll’s “title deed” identification as a stated fact.
  2. Turning Lion and Lamb into separate personalities or unrelated phases of Jesus.
  3. Making the Lamb merely gentle while ignoring his authority and judgment.
  4. Treating sacrifice as proof that victims should remain exposed to abuse. Jesus’ redemptive death is unique; protection, truth-telling, and seeking help are not failures of discipleship.
  5. Using heavenly prayers as a promise of immediate relief or as a command to address prayer to the elders.
  6. Building a certain identification of the elders on a disputed pronoun.
  7. Mistaking multinational redemption for either cultural uniformity or automatic universal salvation.
  8. Using shared reign to justify coercive domination.
  9. Replacing exegesis with current-event speculation.
  10. Explaining every symbol while leaving the class with no response of worship or obedience.

A 35–40 Minute Teaching Route

  1. Opening and bridge, 4 minutes: Recap the throne in chapter 4. Ask, “What kind of person would you trust with the future?”
  2. Read 5:1–7, 9 minutes: Follow the tears, announcement, and surprise. Spend the most time on Lion/Lamb and slaughtered/standing.
  3. Read 5:8–10, 9 minutes: Trace cost, belonging, breadth, identity, and vocation. Ask what priestly service means for ordinary disciples.
  4. Read 5:11–14, 7 minutes: Notice the widening chorus. Discuss worship as allegiance, not merely atmosphere.
  5. Response and prayer, 6–11 minutes: Choose one concrete act of trust, repentance, cross-cultural fellowship, or service. Pray in response to the Lamb’s worthiness.

If time is short: Keep the scroll discussion brief. Prioritize the Lion/Lamb reveal, the reason for worthiness in verse 9, and the response in verses 13–14. Save technical variants and interpretive systems for follow-up.

Conclusion and Closing Question

John begins with an unopened scroll and uncontrollable tears. He ends surrounded by praise. The turning point is not that the world becomes easy to explain. It is that the Lamb is present, victorious, and worthy.

Closing question: If the Lamb is worthy of all heaven’s worship, what part of my life am I still withholding from him?
