Compare four major approaches to Revelation and learn to test them against the book’s first audience, genre, and biblical imagery.

Lecture-Based Study

Introduction

Robert Mulholland begins with an interpretive problem familiar to almost everyone who has studied Revelation: there seem to be nearly as many readings of the book as there are interpreters. New wars, political movements, technologies, and cultural crises regularly generate new claims that Revelation has finally been decoded.

Mulholland recalls a book published during the 1991 Gulf War that claimed the conflict marked the beginning of Armageddon. The war ended almost immediately, giving the book what he jokingly calls perhaps the shortest useful life of any publication. The failed prediction points to a deeper problem: visionary imagery can be removed from its original world and made to correspond to almost anything in ours.

John did not invent an empty collection of symbols for later generations to decode. He drew chiefly from the symbolic world of Israel's Scriptures and Jewish apocalyptic tradition. Those images communicated to the seven churches because they shared the cultural and canonical grammar needed to recognize them.

The interpretive task therefore has an order:

  1. Understand what the imagery meant to John and his original readers.
  2. Determine what reality the vision disclosed to them.
  3. Trace that reality through the biblical canon and the book as a whole.
  4. Then ask how the church should hear and embody the message today.

Key Thought

Revelation was written for us, but it was not written directly to us. We hear it faithfully by first learning how the seven churches heard it.

Mulholland's Argument in Five Movements

  1. Visionary experience exceeds ordinary description.
    • Paul, Julian of Norwich, and Hildegard of Bingen testify that visionary realities cannot be reproduced fully through ordinary words or pictures.
    • John nevertheless receives the command to write what he sees and send it to seven churches.
  2. John communicates through a shared symbolic world.
    • His imagery comes primarily from Israel's Scriptures and Jewish apocalyptic tradition.
    • The Psalms, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, and Zechariah are especially important.
    • John's audience could recognize associations that are no longer intuitive to modern readers.
  3. Symbols detached from context become interpretively unstable.
    • Modern interpreters can project current events or prior theological systems onto the images.
    • A coherent explanation can still be completely wrong if its controlling assumption is mistaken.
  4. Four major approaches preserve different dimensions of Revelation.
    • Preterism protects original historical meaning.
    • Historicism protects God's sovereignty over the whole course of history.
    • Futurism protects the expectation of final consummation.
    • Idealism protects the book's continuing spiritual and pastoral relevance.
  5. Faithful interpretation should preserve the strengths and avoid the weaknesses of all four.
    • The original audience provides the starting point.
    • God's activity in history, the future victory of God, and the book's relevance in every age must then remain in view.

Why Visionary Language Uses Images

Mulholland's proposal

Mulholland treats John's vision as more than a literary device. He describes visionary experience as an expansion beyond the normal range of consciousness into a deeper dimension of reality.

His analogy is a room filled with television or radio signals. The signals are genuinely present even though ordinary human senses cannot perceive them without a receiver. In a comparable way, the visionary becomes aware of a dimension of reality that is not ordinarily visible.

Biblical example: 2 Corinthians 12:1-4

  • Paul speaks of being caught up into the “third heaven” and “paradise.”
  • He does not know whether the experience occurred in or out of the body.
  • He hears things that a human being is not permitted or able to express.
  • The experience is real to Paul, but ordinary categories cannot contain it fully.

John's communicative challenge

John is told to write and send what he has seen (Revelation 1:11, 19). He must translate visionary experience into communicable form. He does this through images whose scriptural associations were already present in the minds of his hearers.

Teaching caution

Mulholland's description of “vision consciousness” is a phenomenological model, not a doctrine Revelation explicitly explains. The important exegetical point is firmer: visionary discourse communicates through symbol, analogy, scriptural echo, and literary pattern rather than through flat photographic description.

John's Symbolic World

Primary Old Testament sources

Mulholland highlights:

  • Psalms
  • Isaiah
  • Ezekiel
  • Daniel
  • Zechariah

Revelation contains very few, if any, formal verbatim quotations from the Old Testament. John instead writes through echoes, allusions, images, phrases, and transformed scriptural scenes. He “bleeds Bible”: the scriptural world has become his imaginative world.

Jewish apocalyptic and related literature

Mulholland also mentions imagery found in:

  • 1 Enoch
  • Psalms of Solomon
  • Syriac Jewish material
  • 3 Maccabees
  • Tobit
  • Baruch

These writings do not carry the authority of Scripture, but they can illuminate the symbolic environment in which apocalyptic images were heard.

Cultural-symbol analogy

An American audience can recognize a donkey flattened beneath an elephant as political commentary without explanatory captions. The image works because the audience knows the symbolic associations.

John's first hearers similarly recognized meanings carried by beasts, horns, lampstands, plagues, Babylon, thrones, and a slain Lamb. Modern readers must learn that symbolic grammar rather than replace it with their own.

Key Point

Before asking what an image resembles in our world, ask what it communicated in John's world and in Scripture's world.

The Motel of the Mysteries Illustration

Future archaeologists uncover a twentieth-century American motel but lack the cultural knowledge required to identify it. They interpret the motel as a funerary complex and its ordinary objects as sacred artifacts:

  • a motel room becomes a burial chamber;
  • the bed becomes a ceremonial resting place for the dead;
  • the television becomes an altar;
  • the bathroom becomes an inner sanctuary;
  • the toilet becomes a sacred urn;
  • toilet paper becomes sacred parchment; and
  • the toilet seat becomes a ceremonial collar.

The interpretation is funny because it is internally coherent and completely mistaken. Once the archaeologists classify the motel as a tomb, every object appears to confirm their assumption.

Mulholland's application

Revelation can become our own Motel of the Mysteries when we detach its images from the world shared by John and his readers. Current events, political enemies, and new technologies then become the supposed interpretive key.

What the illustration proves

  • Context constrains meaning.
  • An explanation can account for every detail and still be false.
  • Confidence should not outrun evidence.
  • The initial controlling assumption must be tested, not merely applied.

What the illustration does not prove

  • It does not prove that symbols can mean anything.
  • It does not prove that Revelation is unintelligible.
  • It does not make all interpretations equally plausible.
  • It does not eliminate the need for contemporary application.

Key Point

A coherent system is not necessarily a correct system.

The Four Interpretive Approaches at a Glance

Approach Primary horizon Central contribution Recurring danger
Preterist John's first-century world and the early Roman period Protects original historical meaning Can confine fulfillment and relevance to the past
Historicist The course of church/world history from Christ to the end Protects God's sovereignty over history Can turn the visions into a disputed timeline of later events
Futurist A final period still ahead, especially Revelation 4–22 Protects real future consummation Can make most of the book irrelevant to its first hearers
Idealist Recurring spiritual realities in every age Protects abiding theological and pastoral relevance Can dissolve concrete history into timeless principles

Approach 1: Preterist

The basic claim

Revelation speaks primarily about realities contemporary with John and the seven churches. Depending upon the version, the principal fulfillment may be associated with:

  • the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70;
  • the persecution and political-religious world of the Roman Empire;
  • the vindication of the early church; or
  • the eventual fall of Rome, sometimes extended as late as AD 476.

What the approach protects well

  • Revelation addressed real congregations in real cities (Revelation 1:4, 11).
  • The hearers were expected to understand, keep, and respond to the prophecy (Revelation 1:3; 22:7).
  • Historical setting and literary genre matter.
  • Symbols should be read according to associations available at the time of writing.
  • Revelation engages history rather than offering timeless abstractions detached from lived suffering.

Where the approach can become distorted

  • A thoroughgoing preterism may leave little direct significance for later Christians.
  • The final victory of God can be reduced to an event entirely within ancient history.
  • The cosmic scale of apocalyptic can be narrowed to one political crisis.
  • Future resurrection, judgment, and new creation may be weakened or reinterpreted completely.

Mulholland's necessary distinction

Beginning with the first-century meaning is not the weakness of preterism; it is the proper beginning of all interpretation. The weakness emerges only when the meaning and work of God are imprisoned in that era.

Key Point

Historical exegesis does not trap Revelation in the past. It gives faithful present application a foundation.

Approach 2: Historicist

The basic claim

Revelation portrays the unfolding course of church or world history from Christ's first advent to the end.

Two major forms

Straight-line historicism

  • Revelation's chapters proceed through successive periods of history.
  • The seven churches may be treated as seven eras of church history.
  • Later visions are correlated with events, rulers, empires, movements, or institutions.
  • Interpreters often identify their own generation with a late stage in the sequence.

Recapitulation historicism

  • The seals, trumpets, bowls, and other cycles revisit the same sweep of history.
  • Each cycle portrays the period from Christ to the end from a different perspective.
  • The visions may intensify rather than advance along one simple chronological line.

What the approach protects well

  • God is sovereign over history.
  • God acts within history rather than merely at its beginning and end.
  • History has a goal: the fulfillment of God's purposes.
  • Revelation remains relevant beyond the first century.
  • Recapitulation notices genuine literary repetitions and parallel movements within the book.

Where the approach can become distorted

  • Later events can replace John's original setting as the interpretive center.
  • The seven churches become spectators of a future historical code rather than the prophecy's addressed hearers.
  • Interpreters develop a forecasting mentality and attempt to locate themselves on the timeline.
  • Different historicist schemes show little agreement about which events fulfill which visions.
  • Nearly every generation manages to place itself close to the end.
  • Allegorical identifications become arbitrary when the imagery is removed from its scriptural roots.

Historical example

Some Protestant interpreters during the Reformation identified the papacy with the beast and their own era with Revelation 13. Everything before their location on the timeline was considered fulfilled; everything after it remained future.

Key Point

Revelation unveils God's rule over history, but it is not necessarily a chapter-by-chapter calendar of postbiblical events.

Approach 3: Futurist

The basic claim

Most of Revelation—commonly chapters 4–22—describes events that await fulfillment in a final period of history.

Some futurists treat the seven churches as seven stages of church history. Others regard them as actual first-century congregations but still place the majority of the visions after their time.

Relationship to historicism

Mulholland sees historicism and futurism as overlapping approaches when both understand Revelation as a historical blueprint. The difference is largely one of location:

  • the historicist believes much of the blueprint has already unfolded;
  • the futurist believes most of it still lies ahead.

What the approach protects well

  • Revelation truly anticipates a future victory of God.
  • Evil will not continue forever.
  • Judgment, resurrection, the defeat of death, and new creation cannot be reduced to recurring spiritual principles.
  • Christian hope looks toward a consummation God has not yet brought to completion.

Where the approach can become distorted

  • Most of the book can become irrelevant or incomprehensible to John's churches.
  • God's apocalyptic activity throughout history can be reduced to one brief future crisis.
  • John's images are read through a previously constructed end-times system.
  • Modern nations, weapons, technologies, and political figures are inserted into the imagery.
  • Revelation becomes a cryptogram that supposedly yields a precise sequence once its secret cipher is found.
  • Speculative charts can replace the book's pastoral summons to worship, endurance, holiness, and witness.

Mulholland's diagnostic question

If chapters 4–22 were almost entirely about a distant future, why did John send them to seven first-century churches rather than mark them, “Do not open until the end times”?

Key Point

The future matters in Revelation, but a future interpretation that erases the first audience has misunderstood how prophecy communicates.

Approach 4: Idealist

The basic claim

Revelation unveils recurring spiritual realities that operate throughout every age: the conflict between God's reign and evil, the seduction of empire, the suffering witness of the saints, divine judgment, faithful endurance, and the certainty of God's victory.

The vision has an immediate meaning for John and his readers, but the deeper reality it reveals applies to human existence across history.

What the approach protects well

  • Apocalyptic reveals the larger divine reality within which visible history unfolds.
  • The whole book can address every generation of Christians.
  • Revelation speaks to personal discipleship and the corporate faithfulness of the church.
  • Babylon, beastly power, false worship, compromise, faithful witness, and the Lamb's victory are not exhausted by one ancient referent.
  • The conflict depicted in Revelation can recur in different historical embodiments.

Where the approach can become distorted

  • Concrete history can dissolve into vague timeless principles.
  • A real final consummation can disappear.
  • The symbols can be separated from their original historical and scriptural associations.
  • The interpreter's preferred ideology or theology can be projected back into the vision as its supposed “spiritual meaning.”

Historical background

Mulholland broadly associates the approach with the Alexandrian allegorical tradition, especially Origen, and with Augustine's contrast between the City of God and the earthly city.

Key Point

Revelation discloses realities that recur in every age, but those realities must be derived from John's images rather than supplied by the interpreter.

Mulholland's Constructive Synthesis

Mulholland does not choose one approach and discard the other three. He begins with the interpretive strength of preterism and then preserves the biblical truth protected by each approach.

Begin with the preterist strength

  • What did John communicate to the seven churches?
  • What did the imagery mean in their scriptural, cultural, political, and congregational world?
  • How did the vision call them to respond?

Retain the historicist strength

  • God is sovereignly active throughout history.
  • Human empires and crises exist within God's larger purposes.
  • History moves toward God's appointed goal.

Retain the futurist strength

  • God's final victory is real and still awaits consummation.
  • Death, evil, judgment, resurrection, and new creation cannot be explained away as merely inward or timeless truths.

Retain the idealist strength

  • The realities exposed by Revelation confront the church in every generation.
  • Beastly power, Babylonian seduction, faithful witness, worship, endurance, and allegiance to the Lamb recur in history.

Avoid the shared danger

Every approach becomes unstable when it disconnects Revelation's imagery from the scriptural and historical world of John and his readers.

Interpretive sequence

Original meaning → theological reality → canonical significance → contemporary application

Key Point

Application that bypasses original meaning is not greater relevance. It is uncontrolled association.

Anchor Text 1: Revelation 1:1-4

“The revelation of Jesus Christ”

  • The book is an unveiling, not merely a concealment.
  • Its images disclose reality from heaven's perspective.
  • Jesus Christ is both the giver and central content of the revelation.

“To show his servants”

  • Revelation intends communication.
  • The symbolic form does not mean God has given the church an unintelligible puzzle.
  • Symbols can reveal truth with a depth that straightforward description cannot equal.

“Blessed is the one who reads aloud ... and those who hear”

  • The prophecy was designed for oral reception in gathered churches.
  • The hearers are blessed not merely for decoding it but for keeping what is written.
  • Interpretation must serve obedience.

“To the seven churches that are in Asia”

  • Revelation had a concrete audience.
  • A responsible reading must explain how the visions addressed those congregations.
  • The seven churches are not incidental recipients of a message intended only for distant readers.

Key Point

Revelation expects to be heard, understood sufficiently for obedience, and lived by real congregations.

Anchor Text 2: Revelation 1:9-20

Vision translated through Scripture

John sees “one like a son of man,” imagery rooted in Daniel 7:13-14. The figure's clothing, hair, eyes, feet, voice, and sword draw from Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, and other scriptural scenes.

The book interprets some of its own images

  • The seven stars are the angels or messengers of the seven churches.
  • The seven lampstands are the seven churches.
  • Jesus stands among the lampstands: the risen Lord is present among his congregations.

Hermeneutical significance

  • Symbols are not necessarily literal, but neither are they arbitrary.
  • Revelation itself can supply interpretations.
  • The Old Testament supplies the image's background.
  • The pastoral context supplies its force.

Key Point

Revelation 1 models the method Mulholland advocates: scriptural image, visionary disclosure, textual interpretation, and pastoral application belong together.

Anchor Text 3: Revelation 13 and Daniel 7

The beast from the sea

Revelation 13 combines the lion, bear, leopard, and terrifying beast of Daniel 7 into one monster. John is not asking readers to identify a zoological creature. The composite beast represents imperial power gathering earlier beastly kingdoms into itself.

First-century horizon

  • Roman imperial power supplied an immediate embodiment of the beastly reality.
  • Imperial claims, violence, economic control, and demands for allegiance made the vision pastorally urgent.
  • The number 666 likely functioned within this first-century symbolic environment rather than as a reference to an unknown modern technology.

Continuing horizon

  • Rome does not exhaust the theological reality of beastly rule.
  • Political, economic, and religious systems can repeatedly demand an allegiance that belongs to God.
  • Contemporary application grows from the original pattern: empire becomes beastly when it usurps divine authority and wages war against faithful witness.

Future horizon

  • Revelation anticipates the Lamb's decisive victory over every beastly power.
  • The recurring pattern moves toward final judgment, not an endless cycle without consummation.

Key Point

A balanced reading can be historically rooted, spiritually recurring, and eschatologically future without making the image mean three unrelated things.

Anchor Text 4: Revelation 17-18

Babylon as a scriptural image

Babylon carries the memory of Israel's ancient oppressor and exile. John applies that scriptural identity to the wealthy, violent, idolatrous imperial city of his world.

Historical reference

Rome is the immediate first-century embodiment of Babylon:

  • she dominates kings;
  • intoxicates nations;
  • thrives through luxury and exploitation;
  • persecutes the saints; and
  • presents herself as secure against judgment.

Recurring reality

Babylon is larger than one city because the theological pattern can reappear wherever wealth, violence, idolatry, exploitation, and self-glorification converge.

Future judgment

Every embodiment of Babylon will finally fall beneath God's judgment. The recurring pattern does not eliminate the finality of the promise.

Pastoral application

“Come out of her, my people” (Revelation 18:4) calls the church to disentangle its worship, ethics, desires, and economic life from Babylon's seductions.

Key Point

The symbol moves from Scripture, to Rome, to recurring empire, to final judgment—without losing its textual center.

Key Themes

  • Revelation as Unveiling: the visions disclose reality rather than merely disguising information.
  • Original Audience: the seven churches are essential to meaning, not incidental delivery addresses.
  • Scriptural Imagination: Israel's Scriptures provide the primary grammar of John's symbols.
  • God's Sovereignty in History: empires and persecution do not place history outside God's purposes.
  • Recurring Conflict: beastly power and Babylonian seduction confront the church in multiple ages.
  • Final Consummation: recurring patterns move toward decisive divine victory.
  • Faithful Witness: the purpose of interpretation is loyal endurance under the Lamb's reign.
  • Interpretive Humility: certainty must be calibrated to textual, historical, and canonical evidence.
  • Pastoral Relevance: Revelation forms worship, allegiance, holiness, hope, and courage—not merely end-times curiosity.

Key Greek Words

ἀποκάλυψις (apokalypsis) | Revelation 1:1

  • Basic sense: disclosure, unveiling, or revelation.
  • The term emphasizes something being made known, not merely hidden.
  • Revelation's symbolic form is a mode of disclosure.

σημαίνω (sēmainō) | Revelation 1:1

  • Basic sense: to signify, indicate, make known, or communicate by signs.
  • The verb is related to the noun for “sign” (sēmeion).
  • It alerts the reader that the message is communicated symbolically, though the debated force of the word should not bear the whole argument for symbolic interpretation.

προφητεία (prophēteia) | Revelation 1:3; 22:7, 10, 18-19

  • Basic sense: prophecy or prophetic utterance.
  • Revelation identifies itself as prophecy, placing it in continuity with Israel's prophets.
  • Biblical prophecy calls God's people to faithful response; it is not merely prediction.

μάρτυς (martys) | Revelation 1:5; 2:13; 11:3; 17:6

  • Basic sense: witness or one who testifies.
  • Jesus is “the faithful witness,” and the church is called to share his testimony.
  • The word later becomes closely associated with martyrdom because faithful testimony can lead to death, but “witness” is its controlling sense in the text.

νικάω (nikaō) | Revelation 2–3; 5:5; 12:11; 21:7

  • Basic sense: to conquer, overcome, or be victorious.
  • Revelation radically redefines victory through the slain Lamb.
  • The saints conquer through faithful testimony and willingness to suffer rather than through beastly coercion.

Theological Evaluation

What Mulholland's approach protects well

  • It refuses to make the original audience irrelevant.
  • It recognizes the Old Testament as the indispensable interpretive background.
  • It resists newspaper exegesis and speculative date-setting.
  • It preserves the partial truth present in all four major approaches.
  • It joins historical exegesis with theological and pastoral application.
  • It understands Revelation as forming faithful disciples rather than satisfying curiosity.
  • It calibrates interpretation through context instead of interpretive cleverness.

Questions and qualifications

1. Does original meaning require a preterist conclusion?

No. Beginning with what the text communicated to its first audience is a hermeneutical commitment shared across approaches. It does not by itself determine whether a prophecy also has recurring patterns or a future consummation.

2. Can an image have both a historical referent and continuing significance?

Yes. Rome can embody Babylon in John's world while Babylon also names a recurring theological pattern of idolatrous, exploitative empire. Continuing significance should extend the textual pattern rather than replace its historical meaning.

3. Does recapitulation belong only to historicism?

No. Recapitulation is first a proposal about Revelation's literary structure. Preterist, idealist, eclectic, and modified futurist interpreters can also recognize recapitulating cycles.

4. Is all futurism speculative?

No. Revelation plainly anticipates realities still future: the final defeat of evil, resurrection, judgment, the end of death, and new creation. The problem is not futurity but a futurism that makes the visions meaningless to their first hearers or treats modern correlations as certain.

5. Is the four-view framework exhaustive?

No. Many responsible scholars use an eclectic or redemptive-historical approach, combining a first-century referent, recurring patterns, and final consummation. Mulholland's own proposal is effectively an ordered eclecticism.

Historical cautions

  • Mulholland's account of early Christian millennial interpretation is too compressed. Justin Martyr and Irenaeus generally expected a future earthly millennium; they should not simply be treated as believing that the millennium began with Jesus and would expire after one thousand calendar years.
  • The idealist approach has historical affinities with Alexandrian allegory, but modern idealism is not identical to Origen's allegorical method.
  • Dispensational interpretation is normally classified primarily as futurist, although some dispensational readings treat the seven churches in a historicist manner.
  • Recovering first-century context narrows interpretive possibilities but does not settle every debated date, referent, allusion, or structure automatically.

Honest conclusion

Mulholland's strongest contribution is not the history of the four views but the order he gives interpretation. Original meaning comes first, yet it is not the last word. The historical message unveils theological reality, the canonical setting clarifies that reality, and the church then embodies it in its own time while awaiting God's final victory.

Restoration Movement Connections

Let Scripture's world govern the interpretation

  • Restoration thought is strongest when Scripture judges inherited systems rather than inherited systems judging Scripture.
  • Preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist labels are tools, not authorities.
  • No chart, tradition, or popular prophecy teacher should determine in advance what John's images are allowed to mean.

The text was given to produce obedient disciples

  • Revelation blesses those who hear and keep its message (Revelation 1:3; 22:7).
  • Knowledge that does not produce faithfulness has missed the purpose of the prophecy.
  • The central question is not, “Can I identify every symbol?” but, “Will I remain loyal to the Lamb?”

Allegiance precedes speculation

  • The book repeatedly contrasts worship of God and the Lamb with worship of the beast.
  • Revelation calls believers out of Babylon before it invites them to explain every feature of Babylon.
  • Repentance, endurance, worship, holiness, and witness are clearer than many proposed timelines.

Preserve the whole biblical witness

  • A strictly past reading can weaken future hope.
  • A strictly future reading can weaken first-century meaning and present obedience.
  • A purely timeless reading can weaken concrete history.
  • A chronological system can weaken the book's literary and pastoral design.
  • The faithful goal is not to split the difference mechanically but to let every textual dimension stand.

Unity without interpretive laziness

  • Christians can disagree about the millennium, the structure of the visions, and particular fulfillments while sharing allegiance to Christ.
  • Charity does not require pretending every claim is equally supported.
  • Interpretive humility means distinguishing what the text states, what it strongly implies, what tradition proposes, and what remains speculative.

Application Questions

  1. Starting assumptions: What modern event, political concern, or theological system am I tempted to use as the controlling key to Revelation?
  2. Scriptural imagination: Which Old Testament books do I need to know better in order to hear John's images?
  3. First audience: Would my interpretation have communicated anything meaningful to the seven churches?
  4. Allegiance: What beastly power or Babylonian desire is competing for my worship today?
  5. Hope: Does my reading preserve Revelation's promise of God's real final victory?
  6. Obedience: How does this interpretation help the church hear and keep the prophecy?
  7. Humility: Which conclusions are explicit, strongly inferred, plausible, traditional, or speculative?
  8. Witness: What would it look like for me to conquer in the manner of the Lamb rather than the manner of the beast?

Discussion Questions

  1. Why does visionary literature create unusual opportunities for interpretive projection?
  2. How does Motel of the Mysteries expose the danger of a false controlling assumption?
  3. Why does original context constrain symbols without exhausting their significance?
  4. What legitimate truth does each of the four approaches protect?
  5. Which approach most shapes popular evangelical readings of Revelation today?
  6. Why is Mulholland especially critical of “crypto-chronological” interpretation?
  7. Can Rome be the first-century Babylon while Babylon remains relevant in later ages? Explain.
  8. How does Revelation 1:3 change the goal of interpretation?
  9. What is gained by reading Revelation through Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, and Zechariah rather than through current headlines?
  10. Where should a teacher say, “This reading is possible, but the evidence does not permit certainty”?

Quick Teaching Grid

When studying any major image in Revelation, ask:

  1. Observe: What does the text actually say?
  2. Locate: Where does the image occur in the book's literary movement?
  3. Echo: Which Old Testament texts or Jewish images stand behind it?
