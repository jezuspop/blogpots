Revelation 16–17: When Babylon Falls, the Lamb Still Reigns

Follow the seven bowls and the fall of Babylon, and see how judgment unmasks the loyalties that hold people captive.

The chapters in one sentence God brings the beast’s world under judgment, exposes Babylon’s seductive power, and assures his people that the Lamb will overcome those who oppose him.

Big question: When evil looks powerful and compromise looks profitable, will I remain faithful to the Lamb?

Read first: Revelation 16–17 in full. For the immediate context, revisit 15:1–8. Explanations below are paraphrases and interpretations, not a substitute for reading the text. Short direct Scripture excerpts identify their translation.

Three anchors:

  • God’s judgments are just: 16:5–7.
  • Stay awake and remain faithful: 16:15.
  • The Lamb overcomes: 17:14.

1. Where These Chapters Fit

From worship to judgment

Revelation 14 contrasts the Lamb’s people with the beast’s worshipers. Chapter 15 shows the victorious saints singing and seven angels receiving the final plagues. Chapter 16 pours out the bowls; chapter 17 begins a closer examination of Babylon’s judgment.

  • 14:8: Babylon’s fall is announced.
  • 15:1: The last plagues bring God’s wrath to completion.
  • 16:19: Babylon receives the cup of judgment.
  • 17: Her identity, alliances, violence, and coming destruction are exposed.
  • 18: Her downfall is lamented by those who profited from her.
  • 19:1–10: Heaven celebrates God’s justice and the Lamb’s wedding.

Read the visions as visions. The order in which John sees scenes does not automatically establish a simple calendar of future events. Chapter 17 can enlarge the Babylon judgment already announced in chapter 16. Some interpreters see the seals, trumpets, and bowls as overlapping portrayals with increasing intensity; others see a more sequential progression. The study should not depend on settling every timeline question.

The original audience matters

These visions address the seven churches of Roman Asia named in chapters 1–3. Their situations included persecution, pressure to participate in idolatry, economic vulnerability, and the temptation of comfortable compromise. Do not assume every church faced identical circumstances or that every detail requires a single empire-wide persecution policy.

The pastoral challenge cuts two ways:

  • To suffering believers: the power hurting you will not have the final word.
  • To comfortable believers: the world rewarding you may be drawing you into false worship.

Working approach: Read the imagery through its Old Testament background and first-century setting, then consider its recurring patterns and final horizon. A historical anchor does not exhaust the theological significance; a future fulfillment does not cancel the original audience.

Bowls, trumpets, and Egypt

The first four trumpet and bowl judgments touch the same broad domains: earth, sea, fresh water, and heavenly lights. The bowls intensify the picture. Where the trumpet visions repeatedly speak of a third, the second bowl describes the death of all sea life.

The Exodus echoes are not decorative. Egypt was a kingdom that enslaved God’s people, resisted God’s word, and could not prevent his deliverance. Revelation portrays oppressive power as another Pharaoh-like order facing the Creator.

Symbolic does not mean unreal. Apocalyptic images communicate genuine judgment with theological force. Reading them symbolically is not dismissing their seriousness; reading them literally does not remove the need to interpret their scriptural background. [S1]

3. Revelation 16:1–7 | Justice for Blood That Was Shed

The command comes from the sanctuary

The bowls proceed under divine command, not because evil has wrested control from God. The sanctuary connection joins judgment to God’s holiness. Chapter 15’s praise of his righteous ways prepares us to understand chapter 16.

  • The first bowl explicitly targets allegiance to the beast.
  • The water judgments bring death into places ordinarily associated with life.
  • The angel’s explanation in 16:5–6 identifies the moral issue: the killing of God’s holy people and prophets.
  • The answering voice from the altar recalls the martyrs beneath the altar in 6:9–11. Their cry for justice has not been forgotten.

Is this merely divine anger?

The chapter interprets these acts as righteous judgment, not uncontrolled rage. God’s holiness includes his opposition to the destruction of human life and the persecution of his people.

That does not make the imagery easy. The language is deliberately severe. Read it alongside God’s patience and call to repentance, not apart from them: Romans 2:4–6; 2 Peter 3:9.

A necessary distinction: God’s right to judge does not authorize Christians to avenge themselves. Romans 12:19–21 places vengeance in God’s hands and commands believers to overcome evil with good.

  • We may ask God to stop violence and vindicate the oppressed.
  • We must not make delight in another person’s suffering a measure of faithfulness.
  • This vision is not permission to identify a sick neighbor or disaster victim as a beast worshiper. Compare Jesus’ warning against simplistic explanations of tragedy in Luke 13:1–5.

Questions to Consider

  • Do I believe God has heard the suffering that powerful people have ignored?
  • Where am I tempted to take vengeance rather than pursue justice without hatred?
  • Does my view of God make room for both mercy toward sinners and judgment against evil?

4. Revelation 16:8–11 | Pain Does Not Automatically Produce Repentance

The fourth and fifth bowls include a repeated response: people curse God and refuse to repent. Their suffering is real, but suffering by itself does not generate a humble heart.

The text’s uncomfortable distinction

  • Awareness: They recognize that God has authority over what is happening.
  • Resistance: They refuse the response that his authority deserves.
  • Repentance: The issue includes what they do, not merely how they feel; 16:11 refers to their deeds.

The narrative echoes Pharaoh’s resistance without requiring every detail of Exodus and Revelation to match. It also develops the refusal to repent in 9:20–21.

Original framing: Being upset about the consequences of sin is not the same as turning away from sin.

The beast’s throne goes dark

In 13:2, the dragon gives the beast power, a throne, and authority. In 16:10, that throne becomes a target of judgment. The kingdom offering protection cannot protect itself.

Application: When the thing I trusted fails me, I can either question my false security or cling to it more desperately. Revelation calls me to repent before loyalty hardens into defiance.

Do not apply this as a blanket explanation for illness, grief, depression, or hardship. The passage describes a particular visionary response of resistant worshipers; it does not diagnose every suffering person.

Questions to Consider

  • Am I more concerned about being caught, being uncomfortable, or becoming faithful?
  • What have I repeatedly defended even after recognizing that it is wrong?
  • What concrete change would show that my repentance is more than regret?

5. Revelation 16:12–16 | Armageddon and the Call to Stay Awake

The Euphrates: A boundary is removed

The river dries, preparing a route for kings from the east. Several backgrounds may illuminate the scene:

  • The Euphrates was associated with the eastern horizon of Roman power and threats beyond it. Parthian associations are historically plausible.
  • Isaiah 44:27–45:1 connects drying waters with Cyrus and the overthrow of kingdoms. Babylon’s vulnerability is a relevant scriptural background.
  • Biblical river-crossing and sea-drying scenes also recall God’s power to remove barriers.

These are contextual connections, not a warrant to name a present-day country as the definite fulfillment. Verse 14 widens the gathering to kings throughout the world. [S1]

Three mouths, one deception

The dragon, beast, and false prophet produce unclean spirits pictured as frogs. Compare the frogs in Exodus 8:1–15. Their emergence from mouths draws attention to deceptive influence and proclamation.

  • The dragon is Satan, identified in 12:9.
  • The beast is continuous with the anti-God power of chapter 13.
  • The false prophet corresponds to the sign-working deceiver of 13:11–18, clarified by 19:20.
  • Their signs persuade rulers to gather in opposition to God.

They are often described as an unholy or counterfeit trinity. That is a useful interpretive label, not a phrase John uses. Do not imply equality with the Father, Son, and Spirit. These enemies are creatures facing judgment.

Discernment principle: An impressive sign does not establish that a message comes from God. Compare Deuteronomy 13:1–3; Matthew 24:24. Test the allegiance a message requires, not merely its spectacle.

What does Armageddon mean?

Armageddon / Harmagedon is commonly connected with Hebrew words meaning mountain of Megiddo. Megiddo evokes conflict in Israel’s history: Judges 5:19; 2 Kings 23:29–30; Zechariah 12:11.

The expression’s precise geographical and linguistic explanation remains debated. Megiddo is associated with a tell and a plain, not an obvious great mountain matching the phrase. Some therefore emphasize symbolic geography; others expect a future military gathering connected to the region.

What the text clearly says: The kings gather for confrontation on God’s great day.

What this passage does not provide: A modern military roster, an exact date, or a detailed account of the battle itself. Chapter 16 describes the gathering. Compare the later confrontations in 19:19–21 and 20:7–10; interpreters differ over whether these depict overlapping perspectives or distinct events.

The sentence we must not miss: 16:15

Jesus interrupts the gathering scene with a blessing on the person who remains alert and clothed. His coming like a thief stresses unexpectedness and the need for readiness. It does not provide a timetable or, by itself, a complete doctrine of the rapture.

  • Stay awake: Be spiritually attentive, not constantly speculative.
  • Keep your garments: Remain ready and faithful. Compare 3:3–5, 17–18.
  • Do not be exposed: Refuse the complacency that leaves allegiance unguarded.

The practical command at Armageddon is vigilance, not prediction.

Questions to Consider

  • Do I spend more energy identifying enemies than examining my own allegiance?
  • What persuasive voice has made compromise seem wise or inevitable?
  • If Christ came unexpectedly, what unfinished obedience would I wish I had addressed?

6. Revelation 16:17–21 | God Remembers Babylon

The seventh bowl brings a declaration from the throne, followed by creation-shaking imagery. The earthquake and hail recall earlier judgment scenes and the divine presence.

Completion, not coincidence

The declaration that it is done concerns the judgment unfolding here. Compare 21:6, where completion accompanies new creation. It may resonate theologically with Jesus’ finished work, but John 19:30 uses a different Greek expression and occurs in a different context. Do not treat them as one identical statement.

What does it mean that God remembered?

God has not recovered forgotten information. In biblical narrative, remembering commonly signals decisive covenant action: Genesis 8:1; Exodus 2:24. Here the action is judgment against Babylon.

  • Her power has not placed her beyond accountability.
  • The cup recalls 14:8–10 and anticipates the golden cup in 17:4.
  • The images of fleeing islands and disappearing mountains portray the undoing of what seemed unshakable.
  • The final curse against God in 16:21 continues the chapter’s portrayal of resistance.

The great city in 16:19 is often understood in relation to Babylon/Rome, but its precise relationship to other great-city references is debated. Let chapter 17’s explicit explanation guide the study rather than assuming every city phrase has exactly the same referent.

Application: Delayed judgment is not divine indifference. Apparent permanence is not immunity.

7. Revelation 17:1–6 | The Golden Cup and the Hidden Blood

Babylon’s appeal

The angel takes John into the wilderness and shows him a richly dressed woman riding a scarlet beast. The vision first presents splendor, then reveals what sustains it.

Visible attraction What the vision exposes
Royal colors and jewels Wealth does not establish moral goodness
A golden cup The container is impressive; the contents are corrupt
Relationships with rulers Political access can be purchased through idolatrous loyalty
Influence over many peoples Widespread participation does not make a system righteous
Apparent grandeur The blood of Jesus’ witnesses exposes violent foundations

The woman and the beast are related but not interchangeable. The woman represents the great ruling city in 17:18; the beast carries her and later joins in her destruction. The imagery shows cooperation between seductive splendor and coercive power without promising that their alliance will last.

Sexual imagery and spiritual unfaithfulness

The prophetic background uses prostitution imagery for idolatry, corrupt alliances, and exploitative cities: Isaiah 23:15–17; Nahum 3:4; Ezekiel 16; 23.

This is not a statement that women are uniquely corrupt or that sexual sin is the only issue. Revelation also uses feminine imagery positively for God’s people and the bride. The angel identifies this woman as a city, not an individual woman or all women.

The imagery must not become permission to degrade people involved in prostitution, especially people experiencing coercion or exploitation. The target here is a powerful, murderous order that seduces nations into unfaithfulness.

Reference Guide: Women, Men, Power, and Unfaithfulness as Biblical Images

How to explain the imagery: Biblical writers used familiar relationships, occupations, and political realities to make spiritual truths visible. Cities could be portrayed as women, covenant loyalty as marriage, betrayal as adultery, and rulers as shepherds or beasts. These images address whole communities, including men and women. They are not a code in which female means evil and male means good.

Historical care: These writings come from ancient patriarchal societies, where marriage, household honor, kingship, and conquest carried powerful associations. That helps explain the imagery without making every ancient social assumption an ideal. The sexual humiliation and violence in some prophetic pictures are genuinely disturbing; acknowledging that is compatible with reading their theological message carefully. They must not become a model for treating women or anyone else.

Female imagery used positively
  • Wisdom as a woman: She publicly calls people toward truth and understanding. Proverbs 8:1–11.
  • God’s comfort compared to a mother’s: Maternal care communicates God’s tenderness toward his people. Isaiah 66:13.
  • Israel as a restored wife: Betrothal pictures renewed covenant faithfulness, justice, and compassion. Hosea 2:19–20.
  • The church as a betrothed bride: The congregation, including its men, is called to undivided devotion to Christ. 2 Corinthians 11:2–3.
  • The holy city as a bride: The angel promises to show the Lamb’s bride and shows John the New Jerusalem. Revelation 21:2, 9–10.
Prostitution and adultery as images of betrayal
  • Jerusalem as an unfaithful wife: Gifts from God are redirected into idolatry and foreign entanglements. Ezekiel 16:8, 15–22, 26–29.
  • Two sisters as two cities: Oholah and Oholibah explicitly represent Samaria and Jerusalem, not women in general. Ezekiel 23:4–12.
  • Tyre as a prostitute: International commercial relationships are portrayed through the image of prostitution. Isaiah 23:15–17.
  • Nineveh as a seductive prostitute: The metaphor exposes the empire’s enticement and enslavement of nations. Nahum 3:1–4.
  • Babylon as a prostitute: The angel identifies her as the ruling city; her partners include the kings who share in her corruption. Revelation 17:1–6, 18.
  • Worldliness as adultery: James applies marital-betrayal language to friendship with the world in opposition to God. James 4:4.

Important distinction: Israel’s adultery imagery stresses a broken covenant. Images applied to Tyre, Nineveh, and Babylon also stress corrupt dealings, seduction, and domination. Do not force every occurrence to mean exactly the same thing.

Male figures and images of authority, both good and corrupt
  • God as shepherd: Leadership is pictured as provision, guidance, and protection. Psalm 23:1–4.
  • Israel’s leaders as selfish shepherds: They feed themselves rather than protect the flock. Ezekiel 34:2–4.
  • Israel as God’s son: A male family image represents a whole people, including women. Hosea 11:1.
  • Kings and kingdoms as beasts: Predatory creatures depict destructive imperial power. Daniel 7:17, 23.
  • Horns as kings: An animal’s strength becomes a political image of ruling authority. Daniel 7:24; Revelation 17:12.
  • Jesus as the victorious royal rider: His kingship exposes every rival ruler’s limited authority. Revelation 19:11–16.

Avoid a double standard: Hosea explicitly confronts the men who participate in prostitution rather than singling out women for blame (Hosea 4:14). This is a direct rebuke of conduct, not simply another metaphor. In Revelation 17:2, the male kings are participants in Babylon’s corruption, not innocent bystanders.

Contemporary comparisons for explaining the images

These are original teaching analogies, not predictions or claims about what God would write today. Modern symbols differ across cultures, and marriage imagery still communicates powerfully.

  • Power: A presidential seal, a military convoy, or a corporate boardroom can suggest authority; context tells us whether that authority serves or exploits.
  • Corrupt power: A polished executive signing a profitable deal while concealed footage shows abused workers can illustrate Babylon’s splendor hiding violence.
  • Adultery or broken loyalty: A wedding ring beside a secret dating profile makes the contrast between public commitment and hidden betrayal visible.
  • Selling allegiance: An official secretly accepting a payment to betray the people they swore to serve illustrates loyalty traded for advantage.
  • Faithfulness: Wedding vows honored when keeping them becomes costly can illustrate covenant loyalty rather than mere romantic feeling.

Brief teaching line (original): “John is not telling us what women are like. He is showing us what unfaithfulness looks like. Babylon is a city, the bride is a people, and both pictures ask where our loyalty belongs.”

Why Babylon, and why Rome?

Ancient Babylon supplied Israel with an enduring picture of conquest, arrogance, exile, and idolatrous power. John applies that name to a new setting.

Rome is the strongest first-century identification, supported by the seven hills in 17:9 and the city’s rule over other kings in 17:18. The imperial context also fits the combination of luxury, worship, and violence. This reading is widely held, but it remains an interpretation of the imagery, not the word “Rome” appearing in the verse. [S2]

The symbol reaches beyond a single place when later systems repeat those characteristics. Applying it today requires moral examination, not merely swapping in the name of a nation or party we dislike.

“Revelation 17 and 18 expose the idolatry, economic exploitation, and dehumanization of the empire.”

Dean Flemming, article abstract, “Locating and leaving Babylon,” Missiology 48, no. 2 (2020), first published online December 24, 2019. [S3]

Why this matters: Babylon’s danger is not only direct persecution. People can become invested in an unjust order because they benefit from it. Chapter 18 will make the economic dimension even more explicit.

Questions to Consider

  • What do I excuse because it is wealthy, successful, or socially admired?
  • Whose suffering is hidden behind the comforts I enjoy?
  • Do I test my own group’s conduct as carefully as I test the conduct of my opponents?

8. Revelation 17:7–13 | The Beast, Seven Heads, and Ten Horns

Begin with the angel’s explanations

Image Explanation given in the chapter What remains interpretive
Many waters Peoples, multitudes, nations, languages (17:15) The precise political relationships represented
Woman The great city ruling over kings (17:18) Rome, alternative identifications, and wider application
Seven heads Seven mountains and seven kings (17:9–10) The specific list of rulers or kingdoms
Ten horns Ten kings receiving brief authority (17:12) Which rulers, and whether ten is a literal count
Beast An enemy destined for destruction (17:8, 11) The historical and final form of its reappearance

Interpretive discipline: What the angel explains should control what we infer. Do not substitute a modern codebook for the text’s own clues.

The beast’s counterfeit story

The beast’s description in 17:8 resembles a distorted version of divine permanence and the Lamb’s victory over death. Compare 1:4, 8; 1:18; 13:3.

Its apparent reappearance dazzles people, but John repeatedly supplies its destination: destruction. The ability to return is not the ability to reign forever.

One historical explanation relates this to traditions about Nero’s return after his death. That is a proposed cultural background, not John teaching that Nero truly rose from the dead. Other readings emphasize recurring beastly empire or a future climactic ruler. [S2]

Why not publish a confident emperor list?

Attempts to identify the seven kings depend on choices about where to begin, which short-lived rulers to include, and how the vision relates to the date of writing. Those choices produce different lists.

Some interpreters see Roman emperors; others see a sequence of kingdoms or a symbolic fullness of hostile rule. The eighth belongs to the seven yet proceeds to destruction, suggesting continuity and culmination, but not an effortlessly solvable modern numerical puzzle.

The ten kings recall Daniel 7:7–8, 24. Their single hour emphasizes limited authority in the vision. It should not automatically be converted into sixty clock minutes or identified with a particular modern organization’s membership count.

Secure conclusion: Evil can coordinate power, but its time and outcome remain limited.

9. Revelation 17:14 | The Lamb Will Overcome

“the Lamb will triumph over them because he is Lord of lords and King of kings”

Revelation 17:14, NIV, short excerpt.

This is the theological center of the chapter. The Lamb does not win because his followers correctly name every king. He wins because of who he is.

The title belongs to Jesus

The language expresses supreme rule. Compare Deuteronomy 10:17; 1 Timothy 6:15; Revelation 19:16. Human rulers have real but limited authority; none has Christ’s final claim.

The victorious figure is still the Lamb. Read the battle language alongside 5:5–10 and 12:11: the One who conquers has given his life, and his people overcome through his blood and faithful testimony.

Called, chosen, and faithful

These descriptions hold together divine initiative and persevering allegiance.

  • Called: Their belonging begins with God’s summons, not self-appointment.
  • Chosen: Their identity rests in God’s purpose and grace.
  • Faithful: Their lives continue to express loyalty to Christ under pressure.

The verse does not provide a complete account of election or settle every Calvinist–Arminian debate. Its immediate force is assurance and allegiance: the people with the Lamb belong to him and remain faithful.

Discipleship implication: We are not invited to become a rival beast in Jesus’ name. Our response is obedience, truthful witness, prayer, patient endurance, and love of enemies. See Matthew 5:43–48; Revelation 13:10; 14:12.

Questions to Consider

  • Is my hope anchored in Christ’s lordship or in my ability to control events?
  • What would faithfulness look like in the pressure I am facing this week?
  • Am I trying to defend Jesus using methods that contradict his character?

10. Revelation 17:15–18 | Evil Turns on Its Own Allies

The angel’s final explanations reveal the fragility of Babylon’s arrangement. The powers that supported her turn against her. Her luxury and alliances cannot guarantee her survival.

The relationship becomes destructive

The violence of the imagery echoes the prophetic judgment scenes in Ezekiel 16:37–41; 23:25–29. It describes the ruin of the personified city. It is not an ethical model for how Christians should treat enemies or women.

Original framing: What helps you compromise today may help destroy you tomorrow.

“Many have toyed with evil, thinking that they can control it.”

Ligonier Ministries / Tabletalk, “The Reign of Babylon,” December 7, 2020. [S4]

Why this matters: The apparent usefulness of evil should not be confused with its loyalty. An alliance organized around domination can consume its own partners.

God’s purpose and human responsibility

Verse 17 says God brings his purpose to fulfillment through these rulers’ actions. It is a strong statement of sovereignty, not merely a suggestion that God observes events.

Yet the beast and its allies remain enemies of the Lamb. Their use within God’s judgment does not make their motives righteous or excuse their violence.

Compare Isaiah 10:5–15: Assyria can serve as an instrument of judgment and still answer for its arrogance. Compare also Acts 2:23–24: divine purpose and human responsibility appear together at the cross.

Christians differ about how to explain providence philosophically. The passage’s pastoral assurance is clearer than those debates: evil does not gain the last word, and God’s word will be fulfilled.

Questions to Consider

  • What destructive influence have I kept close because I believe I can manage it?
  • Where have I mistaken temporary usefulness for lasting security?
  • Can I trust God’s sovereignty without excusing wrong behavior, including my own?

11. Major Interpretive Options

Approach Typical emphasis in these chapters Question to keep testing
Historical / preterist Rome, imperial worship, and judgments meaningful to the first hearers; some readings emphasize Jerusalem Does the identification fit all the textual clues and the book’s final horizon?
Idealist / recapitulation Repeated patterns of idolatrous power, seductive prosperity, and God’s ultimate victory Does the reading preserve the concrete historical setting rather than making every symbol generic?
Futurist A final concentration of rebellion, often including future rulers and a climactic confrontation How would the passage have addressed the original churches, and which details are explicit rather than reconstructed?
Historicist Successive stages of church and political history represented in the visions Why prefer one historical correspondence over the many conflicting schemes?

These categories overlap. Christians within each approach differ significantly.

A note on identifying Babylon

  • Rome: Best fits the combined first-century political and geographical clues in the working reading used here.
  • Jerusalem: Some interpreters emphasize covenant-infidelity imagery and responsibility for prophetic blood. They must explain the worldwide ruling-city language and the Rome-like clues.
  • A future city or world order: Common in some futurist readings. This must be argued from the text, not assumed from contemporary headlines.
  • A particular church institution: Historicist readings have often proposed this. Revelation 17 does not name a modern denomination, and the woman’s city/imperial identity must not be ignored.

Disagreement should sharpen study, not license contempt. No ethnic group, nationality, or present-day population can simply be treated as the incarnation of evil.

14. Misconceptions to Avoid

  1. “Every disaster is one of these bowls.” The chapter does not authorize confident labeling of current tragedies or their victims.
  2. “If people suffer enough, they will repent.” The repeated refusal in 16:9 and 11 warns against that assumption.
  3. “Armageddon gives us the date.” The explicit instruction is to stay awake, not calculate a day.
  4. “Seven hills settles every detail.” It strongly supports Rome; it does not automatically solve the kings or every later application.
  5. “Babylon means my political opponents.” Begin by examining the idolatry and compromises from which you benefit.
  6. “God’s sovereignty makes the beast innocent.” The chapter portrays the beast as accountable and destined for destruction.
  7. “Following the winning Lamb guarantees an easy life.” Revelation repeatedly joins victory with costly witness.
  8. “Judgment permits Christian cruelty.” God judges; Christians must remain faithful to the Lamb’s way.

15. A Short Route for Personal or Group Study

  1. Read 16:1–7. Identify how the chapter explains the justice of judgment.
  2. Read 16:8–11. Contrast regret, resentment, and repentance.
  3. Read 16:12–16. Spend at least as much time on Jesus’ command in verse 15 as on the location in verse 16.
  4. Read 17:1–6, 15, 18. Use the angel’s explanations to distinguish the image from its meaning.
  5. Read 17:7–14. Name the details that remain debated, then identify the promise that is explicit.
  6. Read 17:16–18. Discuss the danger of trusting evil alliances.
  7. Choose one response: confess a compromise, refuse a dishonest benefit, reconcile without revenge, or encourage someone bearing the cost of faithfulness.

Conclusion

God’s justice means that suffering and violence are not ignored. The refusal to repent warns us against hardening our hearts. Babylon’s beauty warns us that evil can attract as well as intimidate. The Lamb’s victory gives us a reason to remain faithful without becoming fearful, speculative, or cruel.

Closing question: What loyalty do I need to surrender so that my life more clearly belongs to the Lamb?
