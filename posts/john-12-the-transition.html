John 12: The Transition

From Mary’s anointing to the grain of wheat, the signs give way to the hour Jesus has been speaking about.

Context and source precision

Read the anointing (12:1-11), royal entry (12:12-19), approaching hour and grain of wheat (12:20-36), and closing assessment of public response (12:37-50) as a movement toward Jesus’ death and glorification.

John 12:7: The construction about keeping the perfume is difficult and translated in different ways. The verb tēreō does not by itself mean preserving the memory of Mary’s devotion or establish how fully she understood Jesus’ approaching death. Jesus interprets her act in relation to burial. Matthew 26:13 supplies a separate canonical connection about remembering the woman’s deed; it should not be read back as the lexical meaning of John’s verb.

Commentary Notes

Introduction & Context

John 12 is the hinge chapter of the Fourth Gospel. It marks the final movement of the “Book of Signs” (chs. 1–12) before the transition into the “Book of Glory” (chs. 13–21). If John 11 is the crescendo of Jesus’ public ministry — the raising of Lazarus demonstrating his power over death itself — then John 12 is where that crescendo gives way to something darker, more intimate, and more theological. The chapter is structured around five major movements that progressively narrow the focus from crowds to a small circle of followers, and from public spectacle to private discourse about the meaning of sacrifice and glory.

deSilva (An Introduction to the New Testament: Contexts, Methods & Ministry Formation) situates John 12 as the pivotal transition between two distinct theological movements in John’s Gospel. The Book of Signs has built a cumulative case for Jesus’ identity — he is the Word made flesh, the giver of life, the light of the world. The raising of Lazarus in chapter 11 was the seventh and final sign, the audacious climax to that sequence. But now, in chapter 12, the narrative begins to unfold the cost of that revelation. The coming of the Greeks signals that the hour of glory has arrived, but that glory will be achieved through suffering and death. deSilva notes the theological irony: just as the sign of greatest power (raising the dead) provoked the decision to kill Jesus, so the greatest revelation of Jesus’ significance (the arrival of Gentiles seeking him) triggers the discourse on sacrifice.

Carson (The Gospel According to John, Pillar New Testament Commentary) emphasizes that John 12 is fundamentally eschatological in character. The repeated phrase “the hour” (ἡ ὥρα, hē hōra) in vv. 23, 27, and 31 signals that the moment of fulfillment has arrived. What was promised, what was foreshadowed, what was anticipated in all the signs and discourses now comes to its appointed time. But Carson insists that this “hour” is not simply the moment of crucifixion — it is the entire complex of crucifixion-resurrection-exaltation, the unified event through which Jesus’ glorification is accomplished. To understand John 12, one must grasp that for John, the cross is not tragedy interrupted by resurrection; it is the triumphant display of God’s glory.

Keener (The Gospel of John: A Commentary, IVP Background) provides rich cultural and historical context. The triumphal entry (vv. 12–19) must be read against the backdrop of Jewish nationalism and anti-Roman sentiment in first-century Palestine. The waving of palm branches and the shouting of “Hosanna!” (ὡσαννά, hōsanna, from Hebrew hoshi’a na, “save us”) would have carried loaded political implications. Keener notes that the Feast of Tabernacles was associated with messianic expectation and armed rebellion; by introducing the triumphal entry during Passover, John emphasizes the eschatological significance while simultaneously highlighting the tragic irony that the crowd’s messianic hopes are about to be shattered.

Stern (Jewish New Testament Commentary) reads John 12 through a distinctly Jewish lens, emphasizing the fulfillment of Old Testament prophecy and Jewish messianic expectation. The quotation from Zechariah 9:9 in v. 15 (“Do not fear, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming, sitting on a donkey’s colt”) would have resonated powerfully with Jewish readers who knew that text as a messianic prophecy. Stern also highlights the Jewish significance of the anointing narrative (vv. 1–11), where Mary’s act of anointing Jesus’ feet with expensive nard echoes the anointing of kings and priests in Jewish tradition — an act laden with symbolic weight.

Morris (The Gospel According to John, NICNT) provides close attention to the grain of wheat discourse (v. 24). The image of a grain of wheat falling to the ground and dying in order to produce much fruit is one of the most profound theological statements about the cross in Scripture. Morris argues that this metaphor, more than any explicit explanation of atonement, captures what Jesus’ death accomplishes: it is not merely substitution but transformation, not just redemptive but generative. The cross is the paradoxical act by which weakness becomes strength, death becomes life, and one becomes many.

Wright (The Resurrection of the Son of God and Jesus and the Victory of God) contextualizes John 12 within the broader Jewish narrative of exile and restoration. The coming of the Greeks (vv. 20–26) represents the moment when the exile of gentile separation from God’s people comes to an end. The cross, in Wright’s reading, is the act by which Jesus, as Israel’s representative and the true king, dies the death of exile so that the nations might be grafted in. This is not abstract theological doctrine but the realization of the covenant promise to Abraham.

Lexham Research Commentary emphasizes the Johannine themes that dominate chapter 12: light and darkness, belief and unbelief, glory that comes from God versus glory that comes from man. The final discourse of the chapter (vv. 44–50) is Jesus’ last public teaching, a distillation of his entire mission and the fundamental divide that has characterized his ministry — those who believe and those who reject.

Literary Placement in John’s Narrative Arc

John 12 occupies a uniquely transitional position in the Gospel’s architecture:

  • Before John 12: The Book of Signs has culminated in John 11 with the raising of Lazarus, the seventh and final sign. This miracle provoked the Sanhedrin’s formal decision to kill Jesus (11:53). The religious establishment has now moved from questioning and debating Jesus to plotting his execution.
  • John 12 itself: The chapter traces the final week before the Passion. It moves from private acts (Mary’s anointing at Bethany) to public spectacle (the triumphal entry) to eschatological moment (the Greeks’ arrival and Jesus’ hour) to profound teaching (the grain of wheat and the final public discourse). The chapter ends with Jesus withdrawing from public view (v. 36b), signaling that the time of public signs and miracles has ended.
  • After John 12: Chapter 13 begins the Last Supper and the Farewell Discourse — the final night of private teaching to the disciples. The focus shifts entirely from the crowds to the inner circle. The public phase of Jesus’ ministry is over; the private preparation of the apostles for his departure and their mission begins.

The movement is unmistakable: from public to private, from signs to discourse, from external revelation to internal transformation. John 12 is the bridge between these worlds.

Key Themes

  1. The Hour Has Come — The phrase “the hour” (ἡ ὥρα) appears throughout chapter 12, signaling that the eschatological moment has arrived. What was promised at the beginning (“I must decrease,” 3:30) now comes to fulfillment. The hour is not a moment in time but the culmination of God’s redemptive plan — the crucifixion-resurrection-exaltation as a unified whole.

  2. The Paradox of Glorification Through Death — Jesus declares that when he is “lifted up” (ὑψωθῶ, hypsōthō, v. 32), he will draw all people to himself. The lifting up is simultaneously the crucifixion (lowering to the earth) and exaltation (lifted to heaven). Glory and suffering are not sequential but simultaneous — the cross is the throne.

  3. The Grain of Wheat and Fruitful Death — The metaphor in v. 24 captures the paradox: a grain of wheat must fall to the ground and die to produce much fruit. This is not merely the doctrine of substitution but a principle of transformation. Jesus’ death is not a tragedy that happens to interrupt God’s plan; it is the means by which the plan is accomplished and extended to the whole world.

  4. The Arrival of the Gentiles as Eschatological Sign — When Greeks come seeking Jesus (vv. 20–26), it signals that “the hour has come.” The inclusion of the nations, long promised in the OT and expected as a feature of the messianic age, now begins. The cross will be the instrument by which the gospel extends to all people.

  5. Light and Darkness, Belief and Unbelief — The chapter concludes with explicit statements about division (vv. 37–41). Isaiah 53:1 (“Who has believed our message?”) and Isaiah 6:10 (on hardened hearts) are quoted to explain why, despite all the signs and teaching, many still do not believe. This is not a failure of revelation but a fulfillment of prophetic expectation that the gospel would divide.

  6. The “Glory that Comes from God” Versus “Glory that Comes from Man” — In v. 43, Jesus diagnoses the fundamental problem: “They loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God.” This single sentence captures the spiritual disease of unbelief — not intellectual confusion but spiritual misdirection, the preference for human honor over divine truth.

  7. Voluntary Submission to Death — Unlike the Synoptic accounts where Jesus seems to face death as an approaching necessity, John emphasizes that Jesus chooses to lay down his life (10:17–18). The troubled soul (v. 27) is not despair but the moment of choosing to embrace the Father’s will. This is not martyrdom but obedience, not tragedy but triumph.

  8. The Anointing as Preparation for Burial — Mary’s act (vv. 1–8) is interpreted by Jesus as preparation for his burial (v. 7). It is an act of prophecy, an embodied statement that death is coming. Yet it is also an act of devotion — the most expensive gift for the one she loves most. This tension between eschatological awareness and passionate devotion characterizes true discipleship.

  9. The Living Testimony of Lazarus and the Threat It Poses — The chief priests’ decision to kill Lazarus (vv. 9–11) reveals the desperation of the religious establishment. A man raised from the dead is an unanswerable argument. The authorities understand that as long as Lazarus lives, he testifies to Jesus’ power in a way that cannot be denied, debated, or spiritualized away.

  10. Judgment as Light Shining in Darkness — The final discourse (vv. 44–50) makes clear that Jesus’ words are not merely informational but judgmental. Those who hear them and reject them have already judged themselves. The light of revelation is, simultaneously, the light of judgment — not because God judges harshly but because truth, once made visible, divides those who embrace it from those who reject it.

RS’s Notes: John 12 — “Unless a Grain of Wheat Falls to the Ground”: The Hour of Glorification

Introduction

John 12 is where the crescendo of revelation gives way to the silence of sacrifice. For eleven chapters, Jesus has been revealing his identity through signs — water to wine, the healing of the sick, the feeding of the five thousand, the raising of Lazarus. The multiplication of miracles has been accompanied by the multiplication of opposition. The religious establishment that once debated Jesus now plots his death. The crowds that marveled at his power are increasingly divided. And Jesus, aware that his hour has come, begins the final week of his earthly ministry.

The chapter moves in five major acts: Mary’s anointing (vv. 1–11), a prophetic act that points toward his burial; the triumphal entry (vv. 12–19), where crowds hail him as king even as their political hopes will soon be shattered; the Greeks’ arrival (vv. 20–26), signaling that the hour of glory has come; the troubled soul and the voice from heaven (vv. 27–36a), Jesus’ moment of choosing the Father’s will; and finally the closing discourse on light and darkness, belief and unbelief (vv. 36b–50), where Jesus withdraws from public view and the Book of Signs comes to a close.

Carson frames John 12 as the decisive moment when Jesus consciously moves toward the cross, not as a forced necessity but as a chosen fulfillment. The hour is not imposed upon him; he embraces it. Wright emphasizes the eschatological significance — the arrival of the Greeks signals that the age of gentile exclusion is ending; the cross will be the means by which this barrier is broken down. deSilva highlights the narrative irony: the greatest sign of power (Lazarus’s raising) provokes the decision to kill Jesus, and the greatest revelation (his glorification through death) comes through apparent weakness.

One-sentence summary: In John 12, Jesus moves into the final week of his earthly ministry, accepting the hour of his glorification — a glorification that will be achieved paradoxically through suffering, death, and the drawing of all peoples to himself through the cross.

****Key Thought**** The hour has come not for Jesus to assert his power but to surrender his life. And in that surrender — the grain of wheat falling to the ground and dying — the world will be transformed. What looks like the end of a story is the beginning of the world’s salvation.

1. Mary’s Anointing: Prophecy Through Devotion (vv. 1–11)

> Mary therefore took a pound of expensive ointment made from pure nard, and anointed the feet of Jesus and wiped his feet with her hair. The house was filled with the fragrance of the ointment… Jesus said, “Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial.” — John 12:3, 7 (ESV)

Main Point: Mary’s anointing of Jesus at a dinner in Bethany (just six days before the Passover) is an act laden with theological significance. She approaches Jesus with an alabaster jar of expensive nard worth 300 denarii — roughly a year’s wages for a laborer — and anoints his feet, a gesture of profound devotion and humility. The act is interrupted by Judas’s objection (vv. 4–6), which Jesus overrules, reinterpreting Mary’s action as “preparation for burial” (v. 7). It is a prophetic act, though Mary may not fully grasp what she is prophesying. She acts in pure devotion; Jesus reads in her action the shadow of his impending death.

Carson (Pillar) notes that the timing is significant. This occurs at a dinner in Bethany, the village of Mary, Martha, and Lazarus — the family Jesus has just raised from the dead. The meal is not merely social; it is celebratory. Lazarus sits at the table, a living testimony to Jesus’ power (v. 9). Yet even in the midst of this celebration, the shadow of the cross falls. Mary’s anointing is the first explicit signal that Jesus’ death is imminent. The expensive nard — the kind used for royal anointing — suggests that Mary, at some level, understands that she is anointing a king, though not the kind of king the crowds expect.

Keener (The Gospel of John) emphasizes the cultural significance of the act. In Jewish tradition, anointing with oil was performed on kings, priests, and corpses. The act of washing Jesus’ feet with her hair is even more startling — it was considered culturally inappropriate for a woman to let down her hair in public, especially in front of men. Yet Mary does so as an act of radical humility and devotion. Keener notes that the anointing of the feet, rather than the head (as in the Synoptic accounts), emphasizes servanthood and sacrifice rather than royal coronation.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) reads the episode through the lens of Jewish custom and biblical precedent. Anointing was an act performed on the bodies of the dead to honor them and to mask decomposition. By having Mary anoint Jesus’ feet, John prepares the reader for his death. Stern also notes that the 300-denarius value of the nard echoes the 30 pieces of silver that Judas will receive for his betrayal (Matt 26:15) — the contrast between the price of devotion and the price of betrayal is stark.

Morris (NICNT) observes that Jesus’ defense of Mary against Judas’s criticism is theologically important. Jesus says, “Leave her alone, so that she may keep it for the day of my burial” (v. 7). The verb τηρέω (tēreō) means to keep or preserve, but the syntax and timing of 12:7 are debated. Preserving the memory of Mary’s devotion is a canonical application, not an established lexical meaning here; her degree of prophetic awareness is not stated. Jesus promises that “wherever the gospel is proclaimed in the whole world, what she has done will be told in memory of her” (Matt 26:13). Her act becomes a permanent testimony.

deSilva emphasizes the irony of vv. 9–11. Lazarus’s very presence at the table — the living proof of Jesus’ power — drives the chief priests to plot not only Jesus’ death but Lazarus’s as well (v. 10). “So the chief priests made plans to put Lazarus to death as well, because on account of him many of the Jews were going away and believing in Jesus” (vv. 10–11). The living testimony is more powerful and more dangerous to the authorities than any argument or debate could be. A man raised from the dead cannot be argued away. He must be eliminated.

Greek Note: The word νάρδος (nardos, v. 3) is an expensive aromatic plant, likely spikenard, imported from India. The specificity of the detail — “pure nard” (νάρδος πιστική, nardos pistikē) — emphasizes both the authenticity and the cost of Mary’s gift. This is not cheap perfume but the rarest and most expensive. Similarly, the verb ἀλείφω (aleiphō, “anoint,” v. 3) is distinct from χρίω (chriō), which was used of royal or priestly anointing. John uses the everyday word, emphasizing the personal, intimate nature of the act rather than its formal or institutional significance. It is devotion, not coronation.

OT Connection: The anointing of Jesus’ feet echoes the act of a sinful woman at the house of Simon the Pharisee in Luke 7:36–50, but also connects to the broader tradition of anointing the dead in preparation for burial. In Numbers 19:11–12, touching a corpse renders one unclean; yet Mary touches Jesus’ feet with her hair. She defiles herself to honor him — a profound statement of her willingness to identify with him, even unto death. The reversal of purity rules in favor of love is a distinctly Johannine theme.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has long emphasized the principle of sacrificial giving in response to God’s grace. Mary’s extravagant offering — a year’s wages poured out — stands as a challenge to Christian stewardship. The Restoration tradition would read this passage as a call to radical generosity in response to Jesus’ radical sacrifice. The beauty of her act is not diminished by its impracticality; its impracticality is precisely what makes it beautiful. This is what love looks like when it is allowed to be extravagant.

****Application**** Ask your congregation: What would it look like for you to anoint Jesus’ feet with a year’s wages? Not literally (though perhaps literally for some), but in terms of what you hold most precious — your time, your talents, your treasure. What is the “nard” that you are unwilling to break open? Mary’s act stands as a rebuke to the economic calculus that Judas represents. He looks at the nard and sees 300 denarii. She looks at Jesus and sees worth immeasurable. Which calculation governs your life? And notice: Jesus does not ask Mary to do this. He does not demand it. She chooses it freely, in response to his love. That is what grace produces — not obligation but devotion so profound it seems reckless to everyone else.

2. The Triumphal Entry: Hosanna and Hidden Irony (vv. 12–19)

> The next day the large crowd that had come to the feast heard that Jesus was coming to Jerusalem. So they took branches of palm trees and went out to meet him, crying out, “Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord, even the King of Israel!” — John 12:12–13 (ESV)

Main Point: The triumphal entry is the moment when Jesus receives public acclaim as the messianic king. The crowds, gathered for Passover, come out to greet him with palm branches and shouts of “Hosanna!” — a cry meaning “save us” (from Hebrew hoshi’a na). Yet even as Jesus receives this acclamation, John emphasizes the tragic irony: the crowds hail him as king, but they do not understand what kind of king he is. Their messianic hopes are political and military; Jesus’ kingdom is spiritual and eternal. Within five days, these same crowds (or representatives of them) will call for his crucifixion.

Carson (Pillar) argues that the triumphal entry, far from being a moment of triumph, is actually the beginning of the end. The Pharisees recognize this immediately: “You see that you are gaining nothing. Look, the whole world has gone after him” (v. 19). This is not a statement of pleasure but of desperation. The authorities understand that Jesus’ popularity has reached a critical mass; they can no longer ignore him or marginalize him. The entry does not buy Jesus time; it hastens his death.

Keener (The Gospel of John) provides crucial historical context. The waving of palm branches was associated with the Maccabean celebration of Jewish independence (1 Macc 13:51), and Passover was traditionally a time when Jewish nationalist sentiment ran highest. The Romans, alert to rebellion, would have seen the crowds and the messianic language as potentially dangerous. Pilate would have been even more suspicious of a man whom the crowds called “King of Israel” (v. 13). In this context, the triumphal entry is not a moment of safe popularity but an act that paints a target on Jesus’ back.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) emphasizes the fulfillment of Zechariah 9:9 in vv. 14–15. Jesus’ entry on a donkey’s colt deliberately enacts the prophecy: “Fear not, daughter of Zion; behold, your king is coming, sitting on the colt of a donkey.” But Stern notes that John’s text includes an important interpretive comment: “His disciples did not understand these things at first; but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered that these things had been written about him and had been done to him” (v. 16). The significance of the event is hidden even from the disciples until the resurrection. This is characteristic of John’s Gospel — understanding comes retrospectively, through the lens of faith in the risen Jesus.

Wright (Jesus and the Victory of God) reads the triumphal entry as Jesus’ deliberate claim to be the true king, the one who will accomplish what previous kings failed to do. The donkey, not a warhorse, signals a king of peace. But the entry is also an acted parable, a declaration that the old order (represented by the temple and the political establishment) is coming to an end, and a new order is being born. The cross will be the instrument through which this transformation is accomplished.

Morris (NICNT) notes the Pharisees’ complaint in v. 19: “The whole world has gone after him.” This hyperbolic statement is nonetheless telling — it reveals the Pharisees’ sense of losing control. But notice the irony: the Pharisees say the whole world has gone after Jesus, yet the world is about to reject him. The “world” that followed him into Jerusalem will soon demand his crucifixion. This reveals the nature of worldly allegiance — it is fickle, superficial, and easily overturned.

deSilva emphasizes the structural placement of the triumphal entry. It comes immediately after Lazarus’s raising and the chief priests’ plot to kill Jesus. The entry does not reverse or impede this plot; it accelerates it. The success of Jesus’ ministry in attracting followers is paradoxically what seals his fate. The authorities have moved beyond debate to execution. The triumphal entry is thus a moment of apparent triumph but actual movement toward crucifixion.

Greek Note: The word ὡσαννά (hōsanna, v. 13) is a transliteration of the Hebrew hoshi’a na, literally “save, please” or “save us.” By the first century, it had become a fixed exclamation of praise, particularly associated with Psalm 118 and messianic expectation. The crowds are not merely greeting Jesus; they are calling upon him to save them. But save from what? Political oppression? Roman occupation? They do not yet understand that salvation will come through the cross, not through military conquest.

OT Connection: The triumphal entry fulfills multiple OT prophecies. Psalm 118:25–26 provides the exact words: “O LORD, save us (hosanna)! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the LORD.” Zechariah 9:9 provides the image of a king coming on a donkey. Both texts carry messianic significance in Jewish tradition. Yet the fulfillment is not what was expected. The salvation comes through death, not dominion. The king rides not to battle but to the cross.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has long emphasized the kingdom of Christ as spiritual rather than political. John 12 offers biblical support for this understanding. The crowds’ misunderstanding of Jesus as a political messiah stands as a warning against any attempt to use the gospel for political purposes or to expect Jesus to deliver the kind of salvation the world desires. True restoration is not a return to national independence but a return to covenant relationship with God, accomplished through the cross and sealed by the resurrection.

****Application**** The triumphal entry forces a question on every congregation: What kind of king do you want Jesus to be? Do you want him to solve your problems, meet your needs, validate your political convictions, and provide you with the kind of success the world measures? Or are you willing to follow a king who calls you to take up a cross? The crowds wanted Jesus to be the kind of king they could control, the kind whose victory would benefit them. But Jesus is a different kind of king — one who conquers through suffering, rules through sacrifice, and extends his kingdom not through force but through the persuasive power of love. Which king will you follow?

3. The Greeks Seek Jesus: The Hour Has Come (vv. 20–26)

> Now there were some Greeks among those who went up to worship at the festival. They came to Philip… saying, “Sir, we wish to see Jesus.” Philip went and told Andrew; Andrew and Philip went and told Jesus. Jesus answered, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. Very truly I tell you, unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a single seed. But if it dies, it produces many seeds.” — John 12:20–24 (ESV, slightly adapted)

Main Point: The arrival of the Greeks seeking Jesus is the trigger that makes Jesus declare, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified” (v. 23). This is the pivotal moment of John 12. The Greeks represent the gentile world, those outside the covenant people of Israel. Their desire to see Jesus signals that the eschatological moment — when God’s salvation would be extended to the nations — has arrived. But this moment brings Jesus not joy but disturbance of soul (v. 27). The hour of glorification will be achieved through suffering and death.

Carson (Pillar) argues that the Greeks’ arrival is the signal that triggers the entire theological reorientation of the chapter. For John, the “hour” is not a moment in time but the complex event of crucifixion-resurrection-exaltation as a unified whole. It is the moment when Jesus’ true identity will be revealed, his true glory manifested. But this revelation comes through apparent defeat, degradation, and death. Carson emphasizes that Jesus does not greet the Greeks; instead, he launches into a discourse about the grain of wheat and the necessity of death. The hour’s arrival is not a time for greeting but for preparation.

Keener (The Gospel of John) notes that the “Greeks” (Ἕλληνες, Hellēnes) could refer either to ethnic Greeks or to hellenized Jews — those who speak Greek as their primary language. But the more important point is that they are outside the immediate circle of Israel. Their desire to see Jesus represents the inclusion of the nations, long promised in the OT and expected as a feature of the messianic age. Keener also notes that Philip (who has a Greek name) is the one they approach, suggesting that cultural bridges are being built.

Wright (The Resurrection of the Son of God) emphasizes the eschatological significance. In Jewish expectation, the coming of the gentiles to the God of Israel was a feature of the age to come (Isa 2:2–4; 66:19–20). The fact that Greeks are seeking Jesus indicates that that age has arrived. But Wright insists that this does not mean political victory or the establishment of an earthly kingdom. Rather, it means that the barrier between Jew and gentile is about to be broken down through the cross (cf. Eph 2:14–16).

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) reads the episode through the lens of Jewish exclusivity and the barrier that separated Israel from the nations. The temple had a court of the gentiles, a space where non-Jews were permitted, but they could not go further. The “middle wall of partition” (Eph 2:14) was not merely figurative but literal — there were warnings in the temple that gentiles who proceeded beyond the court would be executed. The fact that Greeks seek Jesus represents a theological boundary-breaking that would have been controversial. Their seeking is itself a sign that the old order is passing away.

Morris (NICNT) highlights the shift in Jesus’ thinking once the Greeks arrive. Up to this point, Jesus has been directing his ministry primarily toward Israel. His signs have been for Israel; his teaching has been in synagogues and the temple. But the arrival of the Greeks signals a broadening. Jesus’ death will not be only for Israel; it will be for the whole world. The grain of wheat that falls to the ground dies not for one nation but so that it might produce much fruit — fruit from many nations.

deSilva emphasizes the paradox: the moment when Jesus becomes aware that his message will extend to the whole world is the moment he becomes aware that he must die. These are not unrelated. The extension of salvation to the gentiles is achieved through the cross. The death of Jesus is, paradoxically, the life of the world. This is the central paradox of John 12: glorification through humiliation, life through death, universality through particularity.

Lexham Research Commentary notes the connection between the Greeks’ arrival and Jesus’ earlier words: “I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself” (v. 32). The “lifting up” is both crucifixion (lowering to the earth) and exaltation (lifted to heaven). The cross is the event through which all peoples are drawn. This is John’s missionary theology — not the expansion of an earthly kingdom but the extension of redemption through the sacrifice at Calvary.

Greek Note: The word ἡ ὥρα (hē hōra, “the hour,” v. 23) is one of the most important words in John’s Gospel. It refers not to a moment in time but to the appointed time, the kairos moment when God’s purposes are fulfilled. Throughout John, Jesus speaks of his hour as something “not yet come” (2:4; 7:6, 8) until finally, in chapter 12, it arrives. This is the moment of glorification, which John identifies with the crucifixion-resurrection-exaltation. Once the hour comes, nothing can turn it back.

OT Connection: The arrival of the Greeks echoes Isaiah 2:2–4 and Isaiah 49:6 (“I will make you as a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth”). The eschatological vision of Israel’s prophets is being realized, but not as a political or military victory. Rather, it is realized through the sacrifice of the Servant (Isa 53). The cross is the instrument by which the nations are gathered into God’s people.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement was born from a conviction that the gospel’s simplicity had been corrupted by institutional accretions and cultural particularism. John 12 offers biblical grounding for a universal gospel. The Greeks’ desire to see Jesus is answered not with ethnic exclusivity or institutional barriers but with the promise that all people will be drawn to Jesus through the cross. This is the vision that should animate restoration thinking — not a return to a golden age but a return to the gospel’s radical inclusivity and its universal offer of redemption through Christ.

****Application**** When your congregation hears this passage, help them grasp what is at stake. The Greeks’ arrival is good news — it signals that the gospel is for all people, not just the ethnic Israel or the privileged few. But it comes at a cost: the hour of glorification is achieved through death. For us, this means that our own glorification — our transformation into Christ’s image — comes through dying to ourselves. The cross is not merely something Jesus endured two thousand years ago; it is the pattern of Christian discipleship. We die so that much fruit is produced. We lose our lives so that we find them. This is the paradox at the heart of the gospel.

4. “Now My Soul Is Troubled” / The Voice from Heaven (vv. 27–36a)

> Now my soul is troubled. And what should I say — ‘Father, save me from this hour’? No, it is for this very reason I have come to this hour. Father, glorify your name…. Then a voice came from heaven, “I have glorified it, and I will glorify it again.” — John 12:27–28 (ESV, slightly adapted)

Main Point: This passage reveals the most intimate moment of Jesus’ struggle before the Passion. Unlike the Synoptic accounts of Gethsemane, where Jesus prays three times asking that the cup be removed, John presents a more condensed and theologically concentrated version: Jesus acknowledges that his soul is troubled, but immediately rejects the option of requesting escape. Instead, he reaffirms his purpose: “I have come to this hour” (v. 27). The troubled soul is not despair but the moment of choosing — and in that choice, God’s voice thunders affirmation from heaven.

Carson (Pillar) argues that this moment in John is equivalent to the Gethsemane prayer in the Synoptics, but presented differently. Where Matthew, Mark, and Luke show Jesus wrestling with the Father’s will, John presents Jesus as having already come to a resolution. The troubled soul is real — Jesus is not a detached divine being observing human suffering from a distance — but it is the prelude to willing submission, not to doubt or rebellion. Carson notes that Jesus’ prayer is not for deliverance from death but for glorification of the Father’s name (v. 28). The focus is not on Jesus’ suffering but on the Father’s purposes.

Morris (NICNT) observes that the phrase “Now is my soul troubled” echoes Jesus’ words at the tomb of Lazarus: “Now is my spirit troubled” (11:33). In both cases, the disturbance is in response to death. But the nature of the disturbance is different. At Lazarus’s tomb, Jesus is deeply moved by the fact of death in God’s world — by the power of this enemy that has invaded creation. At the hour of his own approaching death, Jesus is troubled because the choice is real and costly. Yet in both cases, the disturbance issues not in paralysis but in action. Jesus acts decisively to overcome death.

Keener (The Gospel of John) notes the peculiar phrasing: “What should I say — ‘Father, save me from this hour’? No, it is for this very reason I have come to this hour.” Jesus poses a hypothetical — escape is theoretically possible — but then rejects it. This is not the language of compulsion or divine determinism but of free choice. Jesus could ask for deliverance, but he chooses not to. He chooses the cross. Keener emphasizes that this is what it looks like for God’s will to be done on earth as it is in heaven — not through force but through the willing cooperation of all involved.

Wright (The Resurrection of the Son of God) reads this moment as the climactic affirmation of Jesus’ vocation. The voice from heaven is God’s word of confirmation: “I have glorified it [my name], and I will glorify it again” (v. 28). The first glorification was in Jesus’ ministry — the signs, the teachings, the revelation of God’s character through his words and deeds. The second glorification will be in the cross itself. What appears to be humiliation, defeat, and death is actually the supreme display of God’s character — God’s love, justice, mercy, and redemptive power all converging in one act.

deSilva emphasizes the theological resolution of the moment. Jesus moves from the troubled soul to the decisive declaration of purpose. This is the moment when he consciously embraces the cross, not as imposed necessity but as the fulfillment of his deepest purpose. The troubled soul gives way to clarity: his purpose has been fulfilled, the hour has come, the glorification of the Father’s name is at hand.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) notes that the voice from heaven (bat kol, בת קול) was a recognized phenomenon in Jewish tradition — a means by which God communicated in the period when prophecy was thought to have ceased. The crowds hear the voice but misinterpret it (vv. 29–30). Some think it is thunder; others, that an angel has spoken. This is characteristic of Johannine irony: the very evidence of God’s presence is misunderstood by those who reject Jesus.

Greek Note: The verb ταράσσω (tarassō, “to trouble,” v. 27) is used of Jesus’ inner disturbance. It appears again in John 13:21 and 14:1, 27. It conveys deep emotional turmoil, not mere abstract awareness. Jesus is not a Stoic sage indifferent to suffering, nor is he a fatalist passively accepting his fate. He is emotionally engaged with the cost of what he is about to do, and yet he chooses it freely. The verb δοξάζω (doxazō, “to glorify,” v. 28) means to reveal the glory of, to manifest the true character. The cross is the ultimate manifestation of God’s character.

OT Connection: Jesus’ prayer echoes the language of the Psalms, particularly the prayers of lament and trust. Psalm 42 captures the pattern: deep distress followed by an affirmation of trust. Jesus’ “Now my soul is troubled” echoes Psalm 42:5–6, but his resolution is absolute: “It is for this very reason I have come to this hour.” The Psalter’s pattern of lament yielding to trust is fulfilled in Jesus’ choice.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has rightly emphasized that the Christian life is lived according to the pattern established by Christ. Jesus’ moment of troubled soul followed by willing submission to the Father’s purposes provides the pattern for discipleship. We too will face moments when the cross seems unbearable and escape seems possible. But faithful discipleship means choosing the Father’s purposes over our own comfort, choosing the mission over safety, choosing death so that much fruit is produced.

****Application**** This passage is for those of your congregation who are struggling with a costly choice. Perhaps it is someone called to ministry in a difficult place, someone facing a diagnosis that will require suffering, someone asked to forgive a grave wrong, someone standing at the threshold of a sacrifice they never expected to make. Jesus’ example teaches us that the troubled soul is not the sign of unfaith but the sign of engaging honestly with cost. What matters is not the absence of struggle but the choice we make in the face of struggle. Do we ask the Father to save us from our hour, or do we affirm that it is precisely for this hour that we came? The voice from heaven will not spare us from suffering, but it will confirm us in it.

5. Light and Darkness, Belief and Unbelief (vv. 36b–50)

> Jesus said, “Whoever believes in me believes not in me but in him who sent me. And whoever sees me sees him who sent me. I have come as light into the world, so that everyone who believes in me should not remain in darkness.”… Though he had done so many signs before them, they did not believe in him. This was to fulfill what the prophet Isaiah said: “Lord, who has believed our message?” — John 12:44–46, 37–38 (ESV)

Main Point: As Jesus moves toward the conclusion of his public ministry, the focus shifts from signs to discourse, from spectacle to theology. He makes four crucial points: (1) belief in him is belief in the Father who sent him (v. 44); (2) seeing him is seeing the Father (v. 45); (3) he has come as light into the world (v. 46); and (4) those who believe in him will not remain in darkness. These statements summarize and concentrate the entire theology of John’s Gospel. But they are immediately followed by a somber assessment: despite all the signs, many did not believe (v. 37), fulfilling Isaiah’s prophecy that the Servant’s message would be rejected.

Carson (Pillar) notes that this final public discourse is positioned as Jesus’ “last word” to the crowds. Verse 36 says, “Jesus said these things, and then he hid himself from them,” suggesting that this is the end of his public ministry. From chapter 13 onward, Jesus speaks only to his disciples. This makes vv. 44–50 crucial — it is Jesus’ final statement to the world. And what is that statement? It is both offer and judgment. The offer: believe in me and you will not remain in darkness. The judgment: to reject the light is to be condemned by the light itself.

Keener (The Gospel of John) emphasizes the quotation of Isaiah 53:1 in v. 38: “Lord, who has believed our message?” (ἀκοή, akohē, literally “report” or “hearing”). This is the Servant passage, the prophetic anticipation of the one who would be rejected by many even as he bore the sins of the many. John reads Jesus’ rejection not as a surprise or failure but as the fulfillment of prophecy. The refusal to believe is not an accident; it is part of the pattern established in Scripture.

Wright (The Resurrection of the Son of God) emphasizes that the problem is not intellectual confusion but spiritual darkness. These are not people struggling with difficult theology; they are people who prefer darkness to light because “they loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God” (v. 43). This is the fundamental diagnosis of unbelief in John: not ignorance but spiritual perversion, preferring human honor to divine truth.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) notes that John quotes both Isaiah 53:1 and Isaiah 6:10 to explain the mystery of unbelief. Isaiah 6:10 speaks of God hardening hearts so that they cannot see or understand. Stern emphasizes that this is not arbitrary divine causation but a pattern of response to revelation: those who persistently reject light eventually become unable to see. “They could not believe,” v. 39 says, using the imperfect tense to suggest an ongoing inability rooted in a prior choice.

Morris (NICNT) observes that v. 43 is one of the most penetrating diagnoses of unbelief in Scripture: “They loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God” (δόξα ἀνθρώπων, doxa anthrōpōn versus δόξα τοῦ θεοῦ, doxa tou theou). The problem with many of Jesus’ contemporaries is not lack of evidence but perverse allegiance. They have chosen to live for human approval — the praise of the Pharisees, the respect of the authorities, the favor of the powerful — and they cannot allow themselves to accept a messiah who threatens that status.

deSilva emphasizes the structural significance of this passage. It marks the end of the Book of Signs. No more public revelation will occur. No more signs will be performed. Jesus has spoken his last word to the crowd. From this point forward, his teaching is for the disciples alone. The division between belief and unbelief, which has been implicit throughout the Gospel, now becomes explicit and irrevocable.

Lexham Research Commentary notes that the theme of light and darkness, which has been woven throughout John’s Gospel from the prologue onward, comes to its climax here. Jesus is “the light of the world” (v. 46; cf. 8:12; 9:5). Those who follow him will not walk in darkness. But to reject the light is to remain in darkness — not because the light condemns you, but because you have refused it.

Greek Note: The word σκοτία (skotia, “darkness,” v. 46) appears frequently in John to represent spiritual blindness and opposition to God. Conversely, φῶς (phōs, “light,” v. 46) represents both the revelation of truth and the presence of God. The language is dualistic — not in the sense of Gnostic metaphysics but in the sense of moral and spiritual choice. You either walk toward the light or remain in darkness; there is no neutral middle ground.

OT Connection: The quotation of Isaiah 53:1 and Isaiah 6:10 frames the mystery of unbelief within the narrative of God’s plan. Isaiah 53 is the Servant passage, describing the one who would be despised and rejected. Isaiah 6:10 is God’s word to Isaiah that the people will hear but not understand, see but not perceive. John reads Jesus’ rejection as the fulfillment of these prophecies. This is not a failure of God’s plan but its execution — redemption comes through the rejected Servant.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has always emphasized that the gospel is simple, that Scripture is clear, that anyone who genuinely seeks truth will find it in the Bible. But John 12 offers a corrective: the problem with unbelief is not intellectual confusion but spiritual preference. A person might reject Jesus not because the gospel is unclear but because it challenges their loves, threatens their status, or demands repentance. This is why the Restoration emphasis on community and mutual accountability is so important — we help each other keep seeking the light even when the flesh prefers darkness.

****Application**** The problem diagnosed in vv. 36b–50 is not distant or theoretical. In your congregation, there may be people who intellectually assent to the truth of the gospel but who are held back by the spiritual gravity of other loves. Perhaps it is someone bound to a sinful relationship who cannot let go because they love the earthly glory more than God’s glory. Perhaps it is someone bound to wealth, or power, or status, or acceptance by others. Jesus’ diagnosis is not harsh condemnation but penetrating clarity: we cannot serve two masters. Light and darkness are not compatible. Love for the world’s glory and love for God’s glory cannot coexist. The pastoral challenge is to help people name their competing loves and to invite them to choose. The light is shining. The offer is made. But the choice must be theirs.

Conclusion

John 12 is the final turning point of the Gospel. The crescendo of revelation — the multiplication of signs, the accumulation of teaching, the mounting opposition — all come to a head in this chapter. And the paradox is complete: the moment when Jesus is most publicly acclaimed is the moment he most clearly moves toward death. The triumphal entry, which looks like vindication, is actually the final push toward the cross. The arrival of the Greeks, which signals universality, comes with the declaration of necessary death.

But the death is not defeat. It is glorification. It is the grain of wheat falling to the ground and dying so that it might produce much fruit. It is the drawing of all peoples to the crucified and risen Christ. It is the manifestation of God’s character — a God who loves so radically that he pours out his own blood rather than demand the blood of others, who gives his life rather than take it.

John 12 is also the chapter where comprehension gives way to faith. Even the disciples do not fully understand (v. 16); they understand retrospectively, after the resurrection. This is the pattern of John’s Gospel: understanding comes not through intellectual mastery but through encounter with the risen Jesus. We believe first; we understand later.

For those of us who preach and teach, John 12 asks us to consider: Are we willing to follow a Jesus whose glory is achieved through the cross? A Jesus whose power is revealed in weakness? A Jesus who draws all peoples to himself by pouring out his blood? Or do we want to domesticate him, to make him safe, to align his kingdom with our political hopes and earthly ambitions? The chapter calls us to a choice. It calls us to be the grain of wheat that falls to the ground and dies, so that much fruit might be produced through us.

“Unless a kernel of wheat falls to the ground and dies, it remains only a single seed. But if it dies, it produces many seeds.” — John 12:24

Restoration Movement Connections

  1. The Gospel’s Radical Inclusivity — The arrival of Greeks seeking Jesus (vv. 20–26) embodies the Restoration Movement’s conviction that the gospel is for all people, not merely an ethnic or institutional elite. The barrier between Jew and gentile is broken down not through political conquest but through the cross. This challenges any tendency toward exclusivity in Restoration churches.

  2. Costly Devotion and Radical Generosity — Mary’s anointing (vv. 1–8) stands as an answer to those who calculate the cost-benefit of discipleship. The Restoration tradition emphasizes radical surrender (cf. Alexander Campbell on the “radical reformation”), and Mary’s year’s wages poured out embodies this. Discipleship is costly, and the cost is precisely the measure of the love.

  3. The Pattern of Grain of Wheat Discipleship — Jesus’ declaration that the grain of wheat must die to produce much fruit (v. 24) is the theological foundation for Restoration understanding of sacrifice and mission. We are called not merely to believe but to die — to our own ambitions, our own rights, our own comfort — so that much fruit might be produced. This applies both individually and communally to the church’s mission.

  4. Choice Between Earthly and Heavenly Glory — The diagnosis in v. 43 — “They loved the glory that comes from man more than the glory that comes from God” — speaks directly to the Restoration Movement’s emphasis on recovering biblical priorities. In every age, churches face the temptation to prefer institutional security and human approval to faithfulness to biblical truth. John 12 calls us to choose.

  5. Understanding Comes Retrospectively — John 12:16 (“His disciples did not understand these things at first; but when Jesus was glorified, then they remembered…”) affirms what many in the Restoration tradition have discovered: we understand Scripture not through abstract study alone but through encounter with the risen Christ, through community, through the Spirit’s illumination. This humbles our approach to exegesis and keeps us from prideful confidence in our interpretations.
