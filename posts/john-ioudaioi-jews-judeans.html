John and the Ioudaioi: Jews, Judeans, or Jewish Leaders?

How should readers understand the Greek term Ioudaioi in John? Compare translations and read each occurrence in context.

Background and Theme Study

Read alongside: John 4:22 · John 7 · John 9

Interpretive precision added in review

The contextual referent and the lexical meaning are not identical. In a scene “the Jews” can designate a particular set of authorities without “authorities” becoming the word’s definition everywhere. The appropriate translation remains passage-specific.

Jesus’ Jewish identity and the narrative’s internal disputes are essential facts. They do not automatically settle every question about the Gospel’s rhetoric, composition setting, or later anti-Jewish reception. Intra-group polemic can still be severe and can be weaponized by later readers. The contrasting scholarly perspectives below should therefore remain part of the study, not be treated as objections eliminated by a slogan.

John 1:11 does not contain the word Ioudaioi; it supplies related narrative context rather than a direct example of that lexeme. The proposal that “other sheep” means scattered Israelites is one option, not a reason to omit the common Gentile-inclusion reading of John 10:16. Preserve that distinction when teaching the book review’s thesis.

Commentary Notes

The Question

The issue from the Kesher article:

In John’s Gospel, does the Greek word Ἰουδαῖοι (Ioudaioi) always mean all Jewish people? Or can it refer more narrowly to Judeans, Jerusalem authorities, or a leadership subgroup?

Short Answer

Yes, the word Ἰουδαῖοι (Ioudaioi) in John does not automatically mean “all Jews everywhere” every time it appears.

Depending on context, it can refer to:

  • Jewish people broadly
  • Judeans, meaning people associated with Judea
  • Jerusalem-centered authorities
  • Religious/political leaders connected to the temple establishment
  • A subgroup within Israel hostile to Jesus
  • Jewish customs, festivals, or burial practices

The best approach is not to replace every occurrence with one fixed English word. The best approach is to translate and teach the term case by case.

Big Idea

John’s Gospel should not be preached as Jesus versus all Jews. It is a deeply Jewish Gospel about Yeshua, Israel’s Messiah, in conflict with particular Jewish/Judean authorities and opponents who reject his identity.

Driving Question

When John says “the Jews,” who exactly does he mean in that passage?

Greek Summary

The Word

Form Greek Transliteration Basic Meaning
Singular masculine Ἰουδαῖος Ioudaios Jew, Judean
Plural masculine Ἰουδαῖοι Ioudaioi Jews, Judeans
With article οἱ Ἰουδαῖοι hoi Ioudaioi the Jews / the Judeans / the Jewish authorities, depending on context

Semantic Range

The word can carry overlapping senses:

  1. Ethnic / people-group: Jewish people as a people.
  2. Geographic: Judeans, people associated with Judea.
  3. Religious-cultural: those who practice Judean/Jewish customs.
  4. Institutional: Jerusalem authorities, temple leadership, official representatives.
  5. Narrative-oppositional: the hostile group in a specific scene.

Key Translation Principle

Do not ask, “What does Ioudaioi always mean?”

Ask, “What does Ioudaioi mean here?”

Kesher Article Summary

The Kesher article reviews Eli Lizorkin-Eyzenberg’s The Jewish Gospel of John: Discovering Jesus, King of all Israel. The central concern is whether John should be read as anti-Jewish or as a Jewish text describing an internal Jewish conflict over Yeshua’s identity.

Lizorkin-Eyzenberg’s Main Claim

Lizorkin-Eyzenberg argues that John’s Ioudaioi are not the whole Jewish people. They are better understood as a subgroup within Israel, often connected to Judea, Jerusalem, and the formal ruling authorities.

In this reading:

  • Yeshua is Jewish.
  • The disciples are Jewish.
  • Many believers in John are Jewish.
  • The conflict is not Christianity versus Judaism.
  • At the narrative level, the conflict is an intra-Israel dispute over whether Yeshua is Israel’s true King and Shepherd.

Important Kesher Framing

The article stresses that John’s Gospel has often been used as a weapon against Jewish people. Lizorkin-Eyzenberg’s project attempts to reclaim John as a thoroughly Jewish Gospel rather than an anti-Jewish document.

Reviewer’s Caution

The reviewer finds the proposal promising but not beyond critique. Some of Lizorkin-Eyzenberg’s claims, especially around Samaritans and Ezekiel’s reunification vision, need further scrutiny. This is important: the Ioudaioi argument is useful, but it should be handled with care rather than treated as a magic key that solves every difficult passage.

Why This Matters

Bad Reading

Avoid this:

John says “the Jews,” so the Jewish people as a whole rejected Jesus and are the villains of the story.

That reading is historically dangerous, exegetically lazy, and pastorally harmful.

Better Reading

Use this:

John often uses Ioudaioi to describe particular Judean/Jerusalem authorities or hostile groups within Israel, while also presenting Jesus, his disciples, many believers, and the scriptural world of the Gospel as deeply Jewish.

Preaching Line

John should not be preached as anti-Jewish. It is a Jewish testimony about Israel’s Messiah confronting particular leaders and opponents while calling Israel, and through Israel the world, to recognize her King.

Key Texts in John

John 1:11

“He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him.”

Lizorkin-Eyzenberg reads this as especially connected to the Ioudaioi as Jesus’ own subgroup or people, not a blanket rejection by every Jew. That reading is useful, but John 1:11 itself should still be handled broadly enough to include the tragedy of rejection among Jesus’ own people without making every Jewish person guilty of that rejection.

Teaching point: The rejection is tragic because it is family conflict, not foreign hostility.

John 1:19

Priests and Levites are sent from Jerusalem to question John the Baptist.

Why it matters: Here “the Jews” likely refers to Jerusalem authorities rather than all Jewish people.

Teaching point: The conflict begins with official inquiry from the center of religious power.

John 2:6

The water jars are for the purification customs of the Ioudaioi.

Why it matters: This is not a hostile use. It refers to Jewish/Judean purification practice.

Teaching point: John can use Ioudaioi neutrally for customs and religious practice.

John 2:13

The Passover is called “the Passover of the Jews.”

Why it matters: This refers broadly to a Jewish festival. It is not anti-Jewish.

Teaching point: Not every use of “the Jews” in John is negative.

John 4:9

The Samaritan woman identifies Jesus as a Ioudaios.

Why it matters: Jesus himself is called a Jew/Judean.

Teaching point: If Jesus is a Ioudaios, John cannot be simplistically anti-Ioudaioi.

John 4:22

Jesus says salvation is “from the Jews.”

Why it matters: This is one of the strongest positive uses in the Gospel.

Teaching point: John’s Jesus affirms Israel’s covenant role in salvation history.

John 5:16-18

“The Jews” persecute Jesus and seek to kill him after the Sabbath controversy.

Why it matters: The referent is not every Jewish person. It is the hostile authorities or leaders in the scene.

Teaching point: The conflict is institutional and theological, not ethnic.

John 7:1, 7:13

Jesus avoids Judea because “the Jews” are seeking to kill him; the crowds speak quietly for fear of “the Jews.”

Why it matters: Jewish crowds fear “the Jews,” so “the Jews” cannot mean all Jewish people in a flat sense.

Teaching point: In these scenes, Ioudaioi likely refers to authorities with coercive power.

John 9:22

The parents of the man born blind fear “the Jews” because they had agreed to put confessors of Jesus out of the synagogue.

Why it matters: This points to community authorities, not the entire Jewish people.

Teaching point: The problem is exclusion by leaders, not Jewish identity itself.

John 11:8

The disciples warn Jesus that “the Jews” were just seeking to stone him.

Why it matters: The disciples themselves are Jewish. Their statement refers to the hostile group in Judea.

Teaching point: Context controls the referent.

John 18-19

In the Passion narrative, “the Jews” often refers to the leaders pressing charges before Pilate, though the language is difficult and has a painful history of misuse.

Teaching point: Preach these texts carefully. Do not let shorthand become blame against Jewish people as a whole.

John 19:40

Jesus is buried according to the burial custom of the Ioudaioi.

Why it matters: This is neutral/positive Jewish practice and includes Jesus’ own burial.

Teaching point: John still situates Jesus inside Jewish custom at the end of his life.

Movement Structure for Teaching

1. John Is a Jewish Gospel, Not a License for Anti-Jewish Preaching

Texts: John 1:11; John 1:45; John 4:22; John 19:40

Jesus is Jewish. His disciples are Jewish. The Scriptures that bear witness to him are Israel’s Scriptures. Salvation is “from the Jews” (John 4:22).

Teaching punch: If your reading of John turns Jesus against Jewishness itself, you are misreading John.

2. Ioudaioi Does Not Always Mean All Jews

Texts: John 1:19; John 5:16-18; John 7:13; John 9:22; John 11:8

Several passages make little sense if “the Jews” means every Jewish person. Jewish crowds fear “the Jews”; Jewish disciples warn Jesus about “the Jews”; Jewish individuals are expelled by “the Jews.” In many passages, the term points to hostile authorities or Jerusalem-centered leadership.

Teaching punch: In John, “the Jews” often means “the authorities,” not “the ethnicity.”

3. The Narrative Conflict Is Intra-Israel Before Later Readers Turn It Into Christian-versus-Jewish

Texts: John 3:1-2; John 7:40-52; John 9:16; John 12:42; John 19:38-39

John shows division among Jewish people over Jesus. Some oppose him, some fear confessing him, some defend him, some believe secretly, some follow him openly.

Teaching punch: John is not describing one monolithic Jewish response. At the narrative level, he is describing a divided Israel wrestling with her Messiah.

4. Yeshua Is King of All Israel

Texts: Ezekiel 34:1-24; Ezekiel 37:15-28; John 10:11-16; John 11:51-52

The Kesher article highlights Lizorkin-Eyzenberg’s reading of Jesus as the shepherd who reunites scattered Israel. John 10’s “other sheep” may be read, in this view, as scattered Israel including Samaritans and diaspora Israelites before the mission extends fully outward.

Teaching punch: Jesus’ mission is not less Jewish than we thought. It may be more Jewish than we realized, and through Israel’s Messiah the blessing reaches the world.

Scholarly Perspectives

Eli Lizorkin-Eyzenberg

Lizorkin-Eyzenberg argues that Ioudaioi in John often refers to Judean/Jerusalem authorities or those aligned with them, not all Jewish people. His broader aim is to show John as a Jewish Gospel about Yeshua, King of all Israel.

Strength: Corrects anti-Jewish flattening and recovers John’s Jewish texture.

Caution: Some readings may need further testing passage by passage.

Rich Nichol / Kesher Review

Nichol appreciates the book’s recovery of John’s Jewishness and its value for Messianic Jewish readers, while also noting places that need further scholarly evaluation.

Strength: Balanced review: appreciative but not uncritical.

Caution: Do not turn Lizorkin-Eyzenberg’s thesis into a one-size-fits-all answer.

Raymond Brown

Brown famously connected some of John’s “the Jews” language with later conflict between Johannine believers and synagogue authorities.

Strength: Helps explain why John’s language can feel sharpened by community conflict.

Caution: Brown’s reconstruction of Johannine community history is debated and should not be treated as settled fact.

Adele Reinhartz

Reinhartz warns that John’s rhetoric has real anti-Jewish force and reception history, even when readers try to soften it.

Strength: Forces Christian interpreters to take the danger of the text’s reception seriously.

Caution: Acknowledging danger does not require concluding John is simply anti-Jewish in intent.

NET Bible Note

The NET note on John 2:18 summarizes the range of Ioudaioi: entire Jewish people, residents of Jerusalem/Judea, Jerusalem authorities, or hostile opponents of Jesus.

Strength: Useful concise tool for teachers.

Caution: Do not let “Jewish leaders” become an automatic replacement either.

Translation Options

Option Strength Weakness
the Jews Traditional and sometimes correct for broader people/custom references Can wrongly imply all Jewish people and fuel anti-Jewish readings
the Judeans Highlights geographic/political setting and Judea-centered conflict Can wrongly strip Jesus and Galileans of Jewish identity if used everywhere
the Jewish leaders Often clarifies hostile authority passages Can be too narrow where customs, festivals, or broader people are in view
the Judean authorities Strong for Jerusalem-centered conflict scenes Too interpretive if applied globally
the Jewish people Useful in broad positive/neutral texts Dangerous in hostile scenes if it universalizes blame

Best Practice

Translate with context-sensitive precision:

  • John 2:6 – Jewish purification customs
  • John 4:22 – the Jews / Jewish people
  • John 5:16-18 – Jewish leaders / Judean authorities
  • John 7:13 – authorities
  • John 9:22 – synagogue authorities / Jewish leaders
  • John 19:40 – Jewish burial customs

Preaching Guardrails

  1. Do not say “the Jews killed Jesus” as a blanket statement.
  2. Do not make Jewishness the problem in John.
  3. Do not treat Jesus as non-Jewish or post-Jewish.
  4. Do not erase the Jewish identity of Jesus, the disciples, the Scriptures, the festivals, and the Gospel’s world.
  5. Do not assume every use of “the Jews” means the same thing.
  6. Do not overcorrect by making Ioudaioi always mean only “Judean leaders.”
  7. Explain the term when preaching John, especially in conflict passages.
  8. When in doubt, specify the scene: “the Jerusalem authorities,” “the Judean leaders,” “some of the Jewish leaders,” or “the hostile authorities in this passage.”

Restoration Movement Connection

The Restoration Movement’s commitment to Scripture authority requires careful reading, not inherited shorthand. If the text says Ioudaioi, the preacher has to ask what John means in context rather than importing centuries of Christian anti-Jewish assumptions.

This matters especially because Restoration preaching often emphasizes returning to the Bible’s original meaning. That instinct is good, but it must include the Jewish world of Jesus and the apostles.

Teaching Line

Restoring New Testament Christianity should never mean forgetting that the New Testament is deeply rooted in Israel’s story.

Preaching Punch

  • John is not a Gentile attack on Jews.
  • John is a Jewish Gospel about Israel’s Messiah.
  • Jesus is not outside Jewish identity; he stands at the center of it.
  • In John, “the Jews” often means a particular group in a particular scene.
  • Sometimes it means Jewish people broadly.
  • Sometimes it means Judean authorities.
  • Sometimes it means customs or festivals.
  • Context decides.
  • The danger is not just bad translation; it is bad imagination.
  • If we imagine Jesus over against all Jews, we have already distorted the Gospel.

Diagnostic Questions

  1. When I read “the Jews” in John, do I automatically imagine all Jewish people?
  2. Have I noticed that Jesus, his disciples, and many believers in John are Jewish?
  3. Does this passage refer to a people, a region, a custom, an authority group, or hostile opponents?
  4. Would “Jewish leaders” make better sense in this scene?
  5. Would “Judeans” make better sense in this scene?
  6. Would that translation create new problems?
  7. Am I preaching John in a way that honors Jesus’ Jewish identity?
  8. Am I alert to the anti-Jewish misuse of John’s Gospel in Christian history?

Gospel Pivot

John’s Gospel does not need to be rescued from its Jewishness. Its Jewishness is part of its gospel witness.

The Word became flesh as a Jewish Messiah. He came into Israel’s story, Israel’s Scriptures, Israel’s festivals, Israel’s hopes, and Israel’s conflicts. The tragedy is not that “the Jews” rejected an outsider. The tragedy is that Israel’s King came to his own, and many of his own did not receive him.

Yet John also says that to all who did receive him, he gave the right to become children of God (John 1:12). The Gospel moves from Israel’s Messiah to the life of the world, but it does not get there by despising Israel. It gets there through Yeshua, King of all Israel.

Conclusion Imperatives

  • Read Ioudaioi contextually.
  • Do not universalize blame.
  • Keep Jesus Jewish.
  • Keep John Jewish.
  • Name the authorities when the passage points to authorities.
  • Let the narrative conflict be intra-Israel before later reception turns it into church-versus-synagogue.
  • Preach John in a way that tells the truth and rejects anti-Jewish distortion.
