Commentary Notes

Introduction

  • 1 John is a pastoral letter written into a church crisis.
  • It is not written like a normal Greco-Roman letter:
    • no opening sender line
    • no named recipients
    • no travel plans
    • no closing greetings
  • It reads more like a sermon, a pastoral circular, or an apostolic family address sent to churches that already know the writer and the situation.
  • The mood is both tender and severe:
    • tender toward shaken believers who need assurance
    • severe toward deceivers whose doctrine and life are pulling people away from the apostolic witness
  • John is not trying to satisfy curiosity about church drama. He is trying to stabilize the faithful.
  • The letter answers three urgent questions:
    • Who is the real Jesus?
    • What does genuine fellowship with God look like?
    • How can the church know the difference between true assurance and spiritual self-deception?

Key Thought

1 John teaches the church how to remain in the real Jesus when false spirituality offers confidence without confession, fellowship without holiness, and love without obedience.

Why 1 John Matters

A church can be wounded by people who sound spiritual

  • The crisis in 1 John is not persecution from the outside first. It is fracture from the inside.
  • Some people had left the community:
    • “They went out from us, but they were not of us” (1 John 2:19).
  • Their departure was not treated as ordinary church movement.
    • Schreiner calls them secessionists because they left and formed a rival group.
    • The issue was not preference, personality, or normal relocation.
    • They departed from the apostolic message about Christ.
  • John is teaching the church how to discern the difference between:
    • a painful but ordinary church transition
    • and a gospel-level break with truth, righteousness, and love

A church can be confused by overconfidence

  • The opponents appear to have made high spiritual claims:
    • claiming fellowship with God while walking in darkness (1:6)
    • claiming to have no sin (1:8)
    • claiming not to have sinned (1:10)
    • claiming knowledge of God without obedience (2:4)
    • claiming spiritual insight while denying the Son (2:22-23; 4:2-3)
  • John does not let confidence become the same thing as faith.
  • Spiritual confidence must be tested by truth, conduct, love, and perseverance.

A church can be shaken by the departure of persuasive people

  • The faithful may have wondered:
    • Did we miss something?
    • Are they the advanced ones?
    • Did they leave because they found deeper truth?
    • Are we behind spiritually?
  • John’s answer is pastoral:
    • You have the apostolic message.
    • You have the anointing from the Holy One.
    • You know the truth.
    • Remain in what you heard from the beginning (2:20-27).
  • The letter is not only a warning against false teachers. It is a repair manual for a rattled church.

Historical and Literary Background

Authorship and Johannine Connection

  • The traditional attribution is to the Apostle John.
  • Blomberg notes that the same authorship questions connected to the Gospel of John also appear with the Johannine Epistles.
  • 2 John and 3 John identify the author as “the elder,” which could mean:
    • John the Apostle speaking as an elder in the Ephesian church region
    • or another early figure known as John the Elder
  • The strongest practical point for study is the clear theological and stylistic connection between 1 John and the Gospel of John:
    • beginning
    • Word
    • life
    • light
    • truth
    • love
    • world
    • abiding
    • witness
    • eternal life
  • 1 John 1:1-4 echoes John 1:1-18, but with a sharper emphasis on eyewitness contact:
    • heard
    • seen
    • looked upon
    • touched
  • Schreiner highlights the movement of those verbs:
    • hearing can happen at a distance
    • seeing brings the witness closer
    • observing implies careful attention
    • touching emphasizes the real bodily presence of Jesus
  • The point is not nostalgia. It is authority.
    • Fellowship with God is mediated through the apostolic witness.
    • Today, that apostolic witness is preserved for the church in Scripture.

Date and Setting

  • Schreiner suggests John is likely writing in the 80s or 90s.
  • That fits the traditional setting of an elderly John connected with churches in and around Ephesus.
  • The exact recipients are not named, but the letter assumes a known community, known conflict, and shared theological vocabulary.
  • 1 John sounds like a shepherd speaking to people he knows:
    • “little children”
    • “beloved”
    • “children”
    • “fathers”
    • “young men”
  • This is family language, not institutional distance.

Literary Style

  • 1 John is not a straight-line argument the way Romans or Hebrews often feel.
  • Schreiner notes that John circles back to the same themes repeatedly.
  • Blomberg connects this to the long-standing observation that 1 John is organized around repeated tests of life.
  • The letter moves more like a spiral:
    • it introduces truth, obedience, and love
    • circles back to them
    • deepens them
    • ties them to assurance
    • applies them to the crisis of false teaching
  • This can frustrate modern outline expectations, but it fits the pastoral purpose.
  • John is not merely giving information. He is forming discernment.

The Crisis Behind 1 John

The secessionists

  • The clearest historical clue is 1 John 2:19:
    • they went out
    • they did not truly belong
    • their departure revealed what was already spiritually true
  • Schreiner describes them as secessionists:
    • people who left the church and formed a rival group
    • people claiming fellowship with God
    • people departing from apostolic truth
    • people giving evidence of moral and relational disorder
  • John’s concern is not that someone left a local congregation.
  • His concern is that they left the apostolic Jesus and tried to take others with them.

The likely shape of the false teaching

  • The opponents seem to have combined several errors:
    • Christological error - denying Jesus as the Christ or refusing to confess Jesus Christ come in the flesh (2:22-23; 4:2-3)
    • Moral error - claiming freedom from sin while walking in darkness (1:6, 8, 10)
    • Relational error - failing to love the brothers and sisters (2:9-11; 3:10-18; 4:20-21)
    • Ecclesial error - abandoning the fellowship and forming a rival witness (2:19)
  • Blomberg connects the background to a mix of early Gnostic-like tendencies, Docetism, and possibly Cerinthian-style teaching.
  • It is best to speak carefully:
    • full second-century Gnosticism may be too developed as a label for the whole crisis
    • but the letter clearly confronts ideas associated with docetic, perfectionist, and antinomian tendencies

Docetism and the real humanity of Jesus

  • Docetism comes from the idea of “seeming” or “appearing.”
  • It taught, in some form, that Jesus only appeared to be human.
  • John will have none of it.
  • The Jesus who gives eternal life is not a spiritual projection:
    • he was heard
    • seen
    • observed
    • touched
    • confessed as having come in the flesh
    • testified to by water and blood
  • Blomberg notes a shift of emphasis:
    • the Gospel of John strongly emphasizes Jesus’ deity
    • 1 John strongly emphasizes Jesus’ humanity
  • This is not contradiction. It is pastoral counterbalance.
    • The Gospel says the Word was God.
    • 1 John insists that this same Word truly came in the flesh.

Perfectionism and sin denial

  • 1 John 1:6, 8, and 10 likely mirror the claims John is rejecting:
    • “we have fellowship with him”
    • “we have no sin”
    • “we have not sinned”
  • John responds bluntly:
    • such claims are lies
    • self-deception
    • making God a liar
  • Schreiner makes an important pastoral distinction:
    • walking in the light does not mean sinless perfection
    • walking in the light means honest, repentant, cleansed life before God
  • The believer’s life is not sinless, but it is no longer surrendered to darkness.

Antinomianism and lawlessness

  • Blomberg notes that some false teaching likely separated spirituality from embodied obedience.
  • John refuses that split.
  • To know God and ignore his commands is a lie (2:3-6).
  • Sin is not merely weakness or woundedness:
    • “sin is lawlessness” (3:4).
  • John does not define love as sentiment.
  • Love for God is visible in obedience:
    • “For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments” (5:3).

Major Themes

1. Apostolic Witness

  • Christianity rests on public revelation, not private spiritual superiority.
  • John begins with what the apostles heard, saw, observed, and touched.
  • The church has fellowship with God by receiving the apostolic testimony about the Son.
  • This matters because the opponents seem to claim a higher or alternative insight.
  • John says the true path is not novelty. It is remaining in what was from the beginning.

2. Incarnation

  • The eternal life was manifested in the real Jesus (1:1-3).
  • The true confession is that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh (4:2).
  • Any spirituality that detaches Christ from his true humanity, bodily life, and saving death is antichrist in spirit.
  • Incarnation matters for church life:
    • love must become embodied
    • confession must become obedience
    • fellowship must become visible
    • sin must be dealt with honestly, not spiritually rebranded

3. God Is Light

  • “God is light” (1:5) means God is holy, pure, true, and self-revealing.
  • No darkness belongs to him.
  • Therefore, fellowship with God cannot coexist with a chosen life of darkness.
  • But light is not only exposure. It is also cleansing:
    • “the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin” (1:7).
  • The church must be a community where truth can be told because mercy is real.

4. God Is Love

  • “God is love” (4:8, 16) is not a slogan for sentimental religion.
  • John defines love by God’s action:
    • God sent his Son
    • God loved first
    • God provided atonement
  • Love begins in God, not in human niceness.
  • The church becomes the visible arena where the invisible God is displayed:
    • “if we love one another, God abides in us” (4:12).
  • Love is tested by concrete action:
    • openhanded care
    • sacrificial service
    • refusal to hate
    • loyalty to the family of God

5. Assurance

  • The stated purpose is assurance:
    • “that you may know that you have eternal life” (5:13).
  • John writes to believers who may have been unsettled by the confidence of the secessionists.
  • Assurance is not built on a memory of a claim.
  • It is strengthened by present evidence:
    • faith in the Son
    • confession of the incarnate Christ
    • walking in the light
    • confession of sin
    • obedience to God’s commands
    • love for the brothers and sisters
    • perseverance in the apostolic message
  • Blomberg warns against using 5:13 as assurance for someone with no continuing faith or life evidence.
  • John gives comfort to the faithful, not cover to the rebellious.

6. Obedience

  • John refuses the split between knowing God and obeying God.
  • “Whoever says ‘I know him’ but does not keep his commandments is a liar” (2:4).
  • Obedience is not presented as a way to earn salvation.
  • Schreiner stresses that righteousness, love, faith, and victory flow from being born of God.
  • The order matters:
    • new birth produces new direction
    • grace produces visible fruit
    • obedience is evidence, not wage

7. Love for the Family

  • John emphasizes love for brothers and sisters as a mark of belonging to God.
  • This love is not abstract.
  • Cain is the negative pattern:
    • hatred matures into murder (3:12-15).
  • Christ is the positive pattern:
    • “he laid down his life for us” (3:16).
  • The church must love “in deed and in truth” (3:18).
  • Church life in 1 John is not primarily event attendance. It is shared life shaped by truth, sacrifice, and responsibility.

8. Discernment

  • “Do not believe every spirit” (4:1).
  • John is not encouraging suspicion as a personality trait.
  • He is calling for tested trust.
  • Discernment asks:
    • What do they confess about Jesus?
    • Do they remain in the apostolic message?
    • Do they practice righteousness?
    • Do they love the family?
    • Do they listen to the truth or to the world?
  • Discernment protects love from becoming gullible and protects truth from becoming harsh.

9. The World

  • “World” in 1 John often means the organized system of desire, pride, deception, and opposition to God.
  • “Do not love the world” (2:15) does not mean despising creation or people.
  • It means refusing to be discipled by:
    • desires of the flesh
    • desires of the eyes
    • pride of life
  • The church overcomes the world by faith in the Son of God (5:4-5).
  • This is not triumphalism. It is resilient loyalty.

10. Abiding

  • To abide is to remain, stay, continue, or make one’s home.
  • John calls believers to abide in:
    • the Son
    • the Father
    • the message heard from the beginning
    • the anointing
    • love
    • God
  • In a fractured church moment, abiding is not passivity.
  • It is faithful resistance against drift.

Church Life in 1 John

1. The church is an apostolic fellowship

  • Fellowship is not merely friendship, warmth, or shared activity.
  • Schreiner notes that in 1 John, fellowship means belonging to God through the apostolic witness.
  • True fellowship is vertical and horizontal:
    • with the Father
    • with the Son
    • with the apostolic testimony
    • with one another
  • This means a church cannot build unity on personality, nostalgia, or vibes alone.
  • The shared center must be the true Jesus proclaimed by the apostles.

2. The church must be honest about sin

  • 1 John creates a community where sin is neither hidden nor normalized.
  • John rejects two opposite errors:
    • denying sin
    • surrendering to sin
  • The church should be a place where confession is normal because cleansing is promised.
  • A healthy church says:
    • sin is real
    • grace is greater
    • repentance is necessary
    • denial is deadly
    • Jesus is Advocate

3. The church must hold holiness and love together

  • John does not let holiness become loveless severity.
  • He does not let love become moral softness.
  • God is light and God is love.
  • Church life must reflect both:
    • truth without cruelty
    • compassion without compromise
    • discipline without contempt
    • assurance without carelessness

4. The church must test spiritual claims

  • The presence of spiritual language does not guarantee truth.
  • John expects the church to examine teaching.
  • This is especially important because the false teachers were likely persuasive, confident, and spiritually impressive.
  • Testing is not cynicism.
  • Testing is pastoral protection.

5. The church must love tangibly

  • John gives no room for love that never opens the hand.
  • 1 John 3:17 asks how God’s love abides in someone who has resources, sees a brother in need, and closes his heart.
  • Church love must become:
    • material care
    • patient presence
    • practical service
    • relational loyalty
    • sacrificial action
  • This is where the church becomes visible proof of the message it confesses.

6. The church must learn how to survive division

  • 1 John is realistic about division.
  • Some departures reveal spiritual truth.
  • Some persuasive voices should not be followed.
  • Some claims of deeper spirituality are actually departures from Jesus.
  • John does not tell the faithful to panic.
  • He tells them to remain:
    • remain in the message
    • remain in the Son
    • remain in love
    • remain in confidence
    • remain alert

7. The church must give assurance without cheapening assurance

  • John is deeply pastoral toward believers with tender consciences.
  • Schreiner warns against reading 1 John 1:9 in a rigid way that torments people who cannot remember every sin.
  • John is not teaching that forgotten sins remain unforgiven until individually listed.
  • He is describing a posture of honesty before God.
  • At the same time, John refuses assurance for people who reject the Son, hate the family, or walk in darkness.
  • Good pastoral ministry must know the difference between:
    • comforting the bruised
    • and confronting the self-deceived

The Tests of Life

The three classic tests

  • Many readers summarize 1 John around three tests of genuine life:
    • Doctrinal test - Do you confess the true Jesus, the Son come in the flesh?
    • Moral test - Do you walk in the light and practice righteousness?
    • Relational test - Do you love the brothers and sisters?
  • Blomberg notes that these tests appear in repeated cycles through the letter.
  • They are not three unrelated checkboxes.
  • They interpret one another.

The tests are intertwined

  • 1 John 3:23-24 pulls them together:
    • believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ
    • love one another
    • keep his commandments
    • know by the Spirit he has given
  • Belief without love is exposed as false.
  • Love without obedience loses its biblical definition.
  • Obedience without confession of the Son is not Christian discipleship.
  • The church needs all three because John is not trying to create anxious introspection. He is giving concrete handles for discernment.

The tests are for assurance, not despair

  • John is not asking sensitive believers to panic every time they see weakness.
  • He assumes believers still sin and need confession, forgiveness, and advocacy.
  • The tests are aimed at direction, pattern, allegiance, and visible fruit.
  • Schreiner’s language is helpful:
    • not perfection
    • but a new direction
    • not sinlessness
    • but a life no longer given over to sin

Key Greek Words

  • koinonia - fellowship, sharing, participation (1:3, 6-7)
    • Fellowship means belonging to God and to one another through the apostolic witness, not merely social warmth.
  • phos - light (1:5, 7; 2:8-10)
    • Light carries moral purity, truth, revelation, and exposed reality.
  • skotia - darkness (1:5-6; 2:8-11)
    • Darkness is not only ignorance. It is a moral sphere opposed to God’s light.
  • homologeo - confess, acknowledge (1:9; 4:2-3, 15)
    • Confession includes honest admission of sin and faithful acknowledgment of Jesus.
  • parakletos - advocate, helper, representative (2:1)
    • In the Gospel, the Paraclete language focuses on the Spirit. In 1 John, Jesus is the Advocate with the Father.
  • hilasmos - atoning sacrifice, propitiation, sin-dealing sacrifice (2:2; 4:10)
    • Forgiveness rests on God’s action in Christ, not on denial, minimization, or moral effort.
  • meno - abide, remain, stay (2:6, 10, 24, 27-28; 3:24; 4:13-16)
    • Abiding is the posture of persevering loyalty.
  • antichristos - antichrist, one opposed to Christ (2:18, 22; 4:3)
    • John applies the term to present Christ-denying teachers as well as the expected final opponent.
  • anomia - lawlessness (3:4)
    • Sin is rebellion against God’s order, not merely mistake or immaturity.
  • agape - love (3:1, 16-18; 4:7-21; 5:2-3)
    • Love is defined by God’s self-giving action in Christ and expressed through obedient care.

Scholarly Voices

  • Schreiner
    • Emphasizes the historical nature of revelation in 1 John 1:1-4.
    • Reads the “we” of 1:1-4 as John and the apostles, not the later readers.
    • Argues that fellowship with God comes through the apostolic witness now mediated through Scripture.
    • Describes the opponents as secessionists who left the church and formed a rival group.
    • Stresses that walking in the light does not mean perfection. Believers still sin, confess, and are cleansed.
    • Frames assurance around evidence: confessing sin, keeping commands, loving believers, and confessing the true Christ.
    • Highlights the priority of grace: being born of God produces righteousness, love, faith, and victory.
  • Blomberg
    • Notes the authorship questions connected to John the Apostle and John the Elder, while emphasizing strong Johannine links.
    • Compares the Gospel and 1 John as writings with similar vocabulary but different pastoral emphases.
    • Argues that 1 John stresses Jesus’ humanity because the crisis required that emphasis.
    • Connects the false teaching pressure to a combination of Docetism, perfectionism, antinomianism, and possibly Cerinthian-style teaching.
    • Presents the classic “tests of life” as belief, obedience, and love.
    • Stresses that the tests are mutually defining rather than separate boxes.
    • Warns that 1 John 5:13 gives assurance to those who continue in faith, not to those resting on a past claim with no present evidence.
  • Prior whole-book cheat sheet
    • Summarizes 1 John around truth, obedience, love, and perseverance.
    • Highlights John’s pastoral burden: exposing false assurance while strengthening bruised assurance.
    • Frames the letter as a defense of the real Jesus and a guide for remaining in the light.

Restoration Movement Connections

  • Apostolic authority
    • 1 John grounds fellowship in the apostolic witness.
    • This supports a Scripture-first vision of church life where truth is restored by returning to the apostolic message, not by chasing novelty.
  • Faith and obedience
    • John does not separate believing in Jesus from keeping his commands.
    • This fits the Restoration instinct that saving faith is living, responsive, obedient faith.
  • Confession and repentance
    • 1 John 1:9 gives a pattern of honest confession rather than defensive religion.
    • The church should call people into the light without making confession feel like spiritual defeat.
  • Baptism and embodied faith
    • 1 John is not primarily a baptism text, but its insistence on the embodied Christ matters.
    • Faith is not an invisible mental claim detached from embodied allegiance.
    • John 3, Romans 6, Acts 2:38, and 1 John together keep faith, repentance, embodied response, and new life from being separated.
  • The church as visible family
    • 1 John makes love for brothers and sisters a test of life.
    • Restoration is not only doctrinal correction. It is the recovery of a visible family shaped by truth, holiness, and sacrificial love.
  • Discernment without sectarian arrogance
    • John gives strong categories for false teaching, but he applies them to denial of Christ, darkness, hatred, and departure from apostolic truth.
    • This should make the church serious about doctrine without turning every disagreement into an antichrist label.

Teaching Angles

The real Jesus creates a real church

  • The Word of life was heard, seen, and touched.
  • The church that belongs to him must become equally concrete:
    • real confession
    • real repentance
    • real obedience
    • real love
    • real perseverance

False spirituality often separates what God joined

  • 1 John exposes several dangerous splits:
    • Jesus’ deity from Jesus’ humanity
    • fellowship from holiness
    • knowledge from obedience
    • love from truth
    • confidence from repentance
    • spirituality from church life

Assurance is not denial

  • John gives assurance through truth, not through pretending.
  • He does not say:
    • you have no sin
    • your choices do not matter
    • doctrine is secondary
    • love is optional
  • He says:
    • confess sin
    • trust the Advocate
    • walk in the light
    • remain in the Son
    • love the family
    • know that you have eternal life

The church must become hard to deceive and easy to heal

  • Hard to deceive:
    • because it tests the spirits
    • knows the apostolic message
    • refuses false claims
  • Easy to heal:
    • because confession is welcomed
    • cleansing is preached
    • love is practiced
    • assurance is offered to the bruised

Application Questions

  • Truth: Where am I tempted to reshape Jesus into someone easier to manage?
  • Light: Am I confessing sin honestly, or am I protecting an image?
  • Obedience: What command of God am I currently treating as optional?
  • Love: Where does my love need to become deed and truth, not just words and feelings?
  • Discernment: What spiritual voices am I trusting without testing?
  • Fellowship: Am I building church life around the apostolic Jesus or around preference, personality, and comfort?
  • Assurance: Is my confidence rooted in the Son and visible fruit of grace, or merely in a past claim?
