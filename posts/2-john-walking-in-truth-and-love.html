2 John: Walking in Truth and Love

This brief letter shows why walking in love requires truth, wisdom, and discernment about whom we support.

Bible Study Cheat Sheet

Scripture quotations are from the CSB unless otherwise noted.

Big Idea

Christian love walks within the boundaries of apostolic truth, and Christian truth expresses itself through obedient love.

Second John is a tiny letter with a large pastoral burden. A network of deceivers has denied that Jesus Christ truly came in the flesh. Because traveling teachers depended upon Christian hospitality, welcoming and supporting such teachers could make a congregation a participant in their destructive mission. John therefore calls the church to continue walking in the truth it received from the beginning, to love one another by obeying God’s commands, and to practice hospitality with theological discernment.

John refuses two distortions that churches still face:

  • Truth without love becomes harsh, proud, and unlike Jesus.
  • Love without truth becomes sentimental, undiscerning, and vulnerable to deception.

For John, truth and love are not competing values. They belong together because both are grounded in Jesus Christ.

Sermon Sentence

The church must love everybody, but it must not help anybody lead people away from Jesus.

Memorable Teaching Line

Biblical love is not approval without boundaries; it is obedience seeking another person’s eternal good.

The Letter at a Glance

Movement Verses Emphasis
Greeting in truth and love 1–3 Shared truth creates a community of enduring love
Walk in truth 4 John rejoices that believers are living faithfully
Walk in love 5–6 Love means walking according to God’s commands
Watch for deceivers 7–9 Denial of Christ’s incarnation abandons apostolic teaching
Guard the church’s partnership 10–11 Do not sponsor or authorize false teaching
Seek face-to-face fellowship 12–13 Christian joy reaches fullness in embodied communion

Key Repetitions

  • Truth — vv. 1, 2, 3, 4
  • Love — vv. 1, 3, 5, 6
  • Command/commandment — vv. 4, 5, 6
  • Walk — vv. 4, 6
  • Teaching/doctrine — vv. 9, 10
  • Deceiver — v. 7

The first half establishes the positive life of the church: truth, love, and obedience. The second half names what threatens that life: deceivers who reject the incarnate Christ and seek the church’s support.

Historical and Literary Setting

Author

The writer identifies himself simply as “the elder” (ho presbyteros). Second and Third John share this title, vocabulary, style, and church situation. Their language is also closely related to 1 John and the Gospel of John.

Major proposals include:

  1. John the apostle wrote the letter, using “the elder” as a title reflecting his age and authority.
  2. Another early Christian leader known as John the Elder wrote it, working within the Johannine circle.
  3. A leader of the Johannine community wrote it, preserving apostolic Johannine teaching.

The traditional attribution to the apostle John is historically plausible, but the letter itself does not say “John” or “apostle.” Preaching should rest on the letter’s inspired message rather than overstating what its opening does not explicitly identify.

Date and Place

Many scholars place 2 John near the end of the first century, often around AD 85–100, and associate it with Ephesus or western Asia Minor. This fits early church tradition concerning John’s later ministry and the overlap between the Johannine writings. The precise location and date remain uncertain.

The “Elect Lady and Her Children”

John writes to “the elect lady and her children” (v. 1). Two readings are credible:

A particular Christian woman and her family

  • Kyria can be read as “lady” and may possibly function as a personal name.
  • Household churches made a prominent woman’s home and family a conceivable audience.
  • The language of children can be literal.

A local congregation and its members

  • A church can be personified as a woman or bride (cf. 2 Corinthians 11:2; Ephesians 5:25–27; Revelation 19:7–8).
  • “Her children” naturally represents the congregation’s members.
  • “Your elect sister” and her children in v. 13 then likely describes another congregation sending greetings.
  • The movement between singular and plural pronouns fits an address to a corporate body.
  • The instruction concerning receiving teachers “into the house” may concern a house church rather than merely a private residence.

The corporate interpretation is more likely, though certainty is impossible. John probably addresses a congregation affectionately and discreetly, perhaps also protecting its identity during a vulnerable period.

Occasion

Traveling Christian teachers depended on local hospitality for food, lodging, credibility, and help continuing their journey (cf. 3 John 5–8). Some teachers, however, denied that Jesus Christ had come in the flesh. The church therefore needed to distinguish between:

  • hospitality that advances the gospel; and
  • hospitality that materially assists a counterfeit gospel.

Second John is not a general command to withdraw kindness from unbelievers or people with whom Christians disagree. It addresses active religious teachers seeking Christian sponsorship for a message that overturns the identity of Christ.

Verse-by-Verse Exegesis

Verses 1–3 — A Community Formed by Truth and Love

“The elder: To the elect lady and her children, whom I love in the truth—and not only I, but also all who know the truth—because of the truth that remains in us and will be with us forever.”

“Whom I love in the truth”

This can mean that John loves them:

  • truly or sincerely;
  • within the shared sphere of Christian truth; or
  • because of the truth they jointly confess.

The options overlap. This is not mere affection. Their bond is created by the gospel reality they share in Christ.

“All who know the truth”

Truth creates fellowship beyond one local congregation. Those who truly know the apostolic message recognize and love others who live within it. John does not picture truth as information possessed privately but as a reality that forms a people.

“The truth that remains in us”

The verb menō means remain, abide, continue, dwell. It is one of John’s signature words. The truth is not a temporary insight or novelty. It takes up residence in the church and “will be with us forever.”

This stands in deliberate contrast to the deceivers:

  • truth remains;
  • false teachers move beyond;
  • faithful disciples abide;
  • innovators abandon what was heard from the beginning.

Grace, mercy, and peace

John gives a threefold blessing:

  • Grace — God’s undeserved favor and empowering gift;
  • Mercy — God’s compassion toward the needy and guilty;
  • Peace — the wholeness and reconciliation that flow from God’s saving action.

These come “from God the Father and from Jesus Christ, the Son of the Father.” The parallel construction places the Father and Son together as the source of divine blessing and gives Jesus a strikingly elevated place.

“In truth and love”

Grace, mercy, and peace are experienced within the sphere of truth and love. John will not separate correct confession from Christian character.

Preaching Point

The truth is more than something the church defends. It is something that lives in the church, holds the church together, and shapes how the church loves.

Verse 4 — Walking in Truth

John has found “some of your children walking in truth.” “Some” does not necessarily imply that the rest were unfaithful; it may mean he encountered only some members of the congregation.

“Walking”

In biblical idiom, walking describes a settled way of life. Truth is not merely:

  • something we affirm;
  • something we argue;
  • something we teach; or
  • something we post.

Truth is a path we inhabit. John rejoices not only that they can state the truth but that their conduct corresponds to it.

“Just as we received a command from the Father”

Their obedience is not optional spiritual enrichment. Walking in truth answers the Father’s command. Christian faithfulness joins belief and behavior.

Preaching Question

If someone watched our walk without hearing our words, what would they conclude we believe is true?

Verses 5–6 — Walking in Love

John does not present a new command but the command believers had “from the beginning”: love one another. This echoes Jesus’ command in John 13:34–35 and the sustained teaching of 1 John.

The Johannine circle

John’s logic is beautifully circular:

  1. Love one another.
  2. Love means walking according to God’s commands.
  3. God’s command is that believers walk in love.

This is not empty repetition. It prevents two opposite errors:

  • Defining love as emotion without obedience;
  • Defining obedience as rule-keeping without love.

“This is love”

Love is neither reduced to feelings nor opposed to them. John defines love by a life ordered toward God’s will and another person’s good. Obedience gives love moral content. Love gives obedience relational purpose.

Not a New Message

The deceivers appear to offer advanced insight, but John points the church back to what it heard “from the beginning.” In the Johannine writings, chronological novelty is not automatically spiritual maturity. Genuine growth moves deeper into Jesus, not beyond him.

Key Point

We do not obey God instead of loving people, and we do not love people instead of obeying God. In Christ, faithful obedience is the shape love takes.

Verse 7 — The Deceivers and the Incarnate Christ

“Many deceivers have gone out into the world; they do not confess the coming of Jesus Christ in the flesh.”

The false confession

The Greek phrase can be translated as denying Jesus Christ “coming” in the flesh. The present participle erchomenon may stress:

  • the historical incarnation—Jesus Christ truly came as a human being;
  • the abiding significance of the incarnate Christ; or
  • less likely in this context, his future coming.

The closest parallel is 1 John 4:2–3: every spirit that confesses Jesus Christ having come in the flesh is from God. The central issue is therefore the real incarnation, not merely disagreement about the timing of Christ’s return.

The opponents may reflect an early docetic tendency—the belief that Christ only seemed to be human—or another form of teaching that divided the divine Christ from the human Jesus. Fully developed second-century Gnosticism should not simply be read backward into the letter, but the trajectory is related: matter and genuine embodiment are treated as unworthy of the divine.

Why the incarnation matters

If the Son did not truly become flesh:

  • Jesus did not truly enter human weakness;
  • his suffering and death become unreal or merely apparent;
  • bodily resurrection loses its foundation;
  • creation and embodied life are devalued;
  • the saving work accomplished through Christ’s real embodied life, death, and resurrection is undermined.

Christian truth is irreducibly incarnational: “the Word became flesh and dwelt among us” (John 1:14).

“The deceiver and the antichrist”

John uses antichrist as a theological description of those who oppose and counterfeit Christ, not here as the proper name of one end-times dictator. First John 2:18 says “many antichrists have come,” and 1 John 2:22 identifies the antichrist as one who denies the Father and the Son.

The prefix anti- can carry the sense of against or in place of. False teaching both resists the true Christ and substitutes a different Christ.

Teaching Caution

Do not let popular prophecy speculation silence John’s actual warning. The immediate “antichrist” problem is Christological: teachers within the religious world are denying the incarnate Son.

Verse 8 — Watch Yourselves

“Watch yourselves so that you don’t lose what we have worked for, but that you may receive a full reward.”

Spiritual vigilance

John speaks pastorally, not sensationally. Deception is possible; perseverance is necessary. The church must not presume that a faithful beginning makes watchfulness unnecessary.

Textual manuscripts differ between “what we have worked for” and “what you have worked for.” Either way, the pastoral thrust remains: false teaching can destroy the fruit of genuine gospel labor.

“Full reward”

This need not imply salvation by merit. The New Testament can speak of divine reward while grounding salvation in grace (Matthew 5:12; 1 Corinthians 3:8–15; Ephesians 2:8–10). John urges perseverance so that the intended fruit of faithful ministry reaches completion.

Preaching Point

Grace does not make vigilance unnecessary; grace teaches us what is worth guarding.

Verse 9 — Progress That Leaves Christ Behind

“Anyone who does not remain in Christ’s teaching but goes beyond it does not have God. The one who remains in that teaching, this one has both the Father and the Son.”

“Goes beyond”

The verb proagō means to go ahead, advance, or move beyond. The false teachers may have advertised their doctrine as spiritual progress. John exposes the irony: progress that moves beyond Christ is not progress at all.

This verse does not condemn careful theological development, fresh application, new vocabulary, or deeper understanding. The church must continually articulate apostolic truth in new settings. John condemns innovation that abandons the Christological center of the apostolic witness.

“The teaching of Christ”

The phrase can mean:

  1. teaching given by Christ; or
  2. teaching about Christ.

The immediate concern in v. 7 favors teaching about Christ, though the two belong together. Christ’s teaching and the apostolic teaching concerning Christ cannot finally be separated.

“Does not have God”

John refuses the claim that someone can possess a deeper relationship with God while rejecting the Son as truly incarnate. The Father cannot be honored by bypassing or redefining the Son (cf. John 5:23; 14:6–9; 1 John 2:23).

Key Point

A spirituality that claims to take us beyond Jesus has not taken us deeper into God; it has taken us away from him.

Verses 10–11 — Discernment at the Door

“If anyone comes to you and does not bring this teaching, do not receive him into your home, and don’t greet him, for the one who greets him shares in his evil works.”

These are the letter’s most difficult verses and must be interpreted within their historical and literary context.

What hospitality meant

In the ancient Mediterranean world, receiving a traveling teacher could include:

  • lodging and food;
  • a meeting place;
  • access to the congregation;
  • public recognition;
  • financial or logistical support; and
  • a letter of recommendation for the next church.

Hospitality was therefore not merely private kindness. It could amount to missionary partnership and ecclesial endorsement.

“Do not receive him into the house”

If “the elect lady” is a church and the congregation meets in a house, the instruction likely means: do not give the false teacher a platform, sponsorship, or recognized ministry within the assembly.

“Do not greet him”

The Greek chairein can mean a conventional greeting, but in this ministry setting it may carry the force of welcome, affirmation, or blessing. John is not likely forbidding every civil word spoken to a person with false beliefs. He forbids a greeting that recognizes the teacher as a legitimate Christian emissary.

“Shares in his evil works”

The verb koinōneō means to share, participate, or have fellowship. Materially assisting the deceiver makes the church a participant in the spread of his teaching. This parallels 3 John 8, where supporting faithful workers makes believers “coworkers with the truth.”

The contrast is exact:

  • Support true teachers → become coworkers with the truth.
  • Support false teachers → share in their evil works.

What John is not commanding

This text does not authorize:

  • cruelty toward unbelievers;
  • refusal to help a person in ordinary human need;
  • isolation from everyone who holds an incorrect belief;
  • treating every secondary disagreement as heresy;
  • inquisitions, social hostility, or contempt;
  • avoiding evangelistic conversation with false teachers.

Jesus ate with sinners, taught opponents, and commanded love of enemies. John himself commands love. The issue is not contact but complicity; not conversation but commissioning; not kindness but sponsorship.

Contemporary parallels

The principle applies when a church or believer is asked to:

  • provide a pulpit or teaching platform;
  • fund a ministry;
  • promote or distribute teaching;
  • lend institutional credibility;
  • co-brand an event;
  • recommend a teacher to another congregation; or
  • use media channels to amplify a counterfeit Christ.

The right question is not simply, “Am I being nice?” It is, “Will my action materially advance or authenticate teaching that leads people away from the apostolic Christ?”

Essential Pastoral Balance

We can open our table to a person without opening Christ’s pulpit to their error.

Verses 12–13 — Face to Face and Full Joy

John has much more to say but does not want to communicate it “with paper and ink.” He hopes to come and speak “face to face”—literally “mouth to mouth”—so that “our joy may be complete.”

Embodied fellowship

The ending may also resonate with the letter’s incarnational emphasis. The message can travel through writing, but Christian fellowship seeks embodied presence. Truth is personal and communal, not merely informational.

This should not be turned into a simplistic attack on digital communication; John uses available technology to serve the church. But he knows its limit. A letter can carry truth across distance, while presence allows fuller conversation, relationship, correction, affection, and joy.

“The children of your elect sister”

This likely means members of the congregation where John is presently located send greetings to the recipient church. The family imagery frames local churches as sister communities within God’s chosen family.

Preaching Point

God sends truth through words, but he forms people through presence.

Major Theological Themes

1. Truth is inhabited, not merely asserted

Truth remains in believers, accompanies them forever, and becomes the path on which they walk. Biblical truth is objective, but it is never merely abstract.

2. Love has revealed content

John does not let culture, impulse, or personal preference define love. God’s commands reveal what love seeks and how love behaves.

3. Obedience is relational

Commandment-keeping is not an impersonal ladder by which we earn God’s favor. It is the faithful life of a community already addressed by grace, mercy, and peace.

4. The incarnation is nonnegotiable

Christian faith centers on the eternal Son who genuinely entered embodied human existence. A reconstructed Jesus who did not truly come in the flesh is not the apostolic Christ.

5. Perseverance matters

Believers are told to watch themselves, remain in Christ’s teaching, and guard the fruit of gospel labor. Beginning well does not eliminate the call to continue faithfully.

6. Fellowship includes moral partnership

Support, endorsement, and hospitality are never entirely neutral when they enable a teacher’s mission. Christian generosity requires discernment about what our resources help accomplish.

7. Christian community is embodied

Paper and ink serve the gospel, but face-to-face presence completes joy. The letter’s form mirrors its theology: the incarnate gospel creates an embodied family.

Canonical Connections

Within the Johannine writings

  • John 1:14 — The Word became flesh.
  • John 8:31–32 — Disciples remain in Jesus’ word and know the truth.
  • John 13:34–35 — Jesus gives the command to love one another.
  • John 14:6, 15 — Jesus is the truth; love for him expresses itself in obedience.
  • John 15:4–10 — Abiding, obedience, and love belong together.
  • John 20:30–31 — Apostolic testimony identifies Jesus so that readers may believe and have life.
  • 1 John 2:18–27 — Many antichrists deny the Son; believers must let the original message abide.
  • 1 John 4:1–3 — Spirits are tested by confession of Jesus Christ come in the flesh.
  • 1 John 5:2–3 — Love for God’s children and love for God meet in obeying his commands.
  • 3 John 5–8 — Supporting faithful teachers makes the church coworkers with the truth.

Across the New Testament

  • Matthew 7:15–20 — Beware of false prophets; examine their fruit.
  • Romans 12:13; Hebrews 13:2 — Hospitality remains a Christian duty.
  • Galatians 1:6–9 — A different gospel must not be accepted.
  • Ephesians 4:14–16 — The church resists deception by speaking truth in love.
  • Philippians 1:9–10 — Love should abound with knowledge and discernment.
  • Titus 3:10–11 — Divisive teachers require warning and boundaries.
  • Jude 3–4, 22–23 — Contend for the faith while showing differentiated mercy.

Interpretive and Pastoral Guardrails

Text: what John clearly says

  • Truth and love belong together.
  • Love walks according to God’s commands.
  • Many deceivers deny Jesus Christ’s coming in the flesh.
  • Believers must remain in Christ’s teaching.
  • Churches must not materially support teachers who bring a Christ-denying doctrine.

Strong inference

  • “The elect lady” most likely represents a local congregation.
  • The house probably functions as a meeting place and base for traveling teachers.
  • “Greeting” likely includes public welcome or endorsement, not mere civility.

Uncertain reconstruction

  • Whether the opponents were full docetists, proto-Gnostics, Cerinthians, or another group.
  • Whether John the apostle or another John known as the Elder penned the letter.
  • Whether Kyria was a personal name.

Application requiring wisdom

  • Which modern teachings constitute abandonment of “the teaching of Christ.”
  • When collaboration becomes endorsement.
  • What level of doctrinal disagreement requires limiting fellowship or platform access.

The congregation should distinguish these levels instead of speaking with equal certainty about all of them.

Preaching Framework

Suggested Sermon Title

Truth Has a Walk

Alternative titles:

  • Love With Its Eyes Open
  • No Love Without Truth, No Truth Without Love
  • When Hospitality Becomes Complicity
  • Don’t Progress Past Jesus

Sermon Aim

Call the congregation to embody truth through obedient love, remain centered on the incarnate Christ, and exercise discernment about the voices and missions it supports.

Three-Movement Outline

1. Walk in the truth — vv. 1–4

  • Truth is not only confessed; it abides in us.
  • Truth creates loving fellowship.
  • The Father commands a life that matches the confession.

Diagnostic: Is the truth merely in our vocabulary, or is it visible in our walk?

2. Let love obey — vv. 5–6

  • The command is old but never obsolete.
  • Love is not whatever feels accepting.
  • Obedience without love is hollow; love without obedience is directionless.

Diagnostic: Do our convictions make us more loving, and does our love keep us faithful?

3. Don’t help deception travel — vv. 7–11

  • Test every teaching by the real Jesus who came in the flesh.
  • Do not mistake departure from Christ for spiritual progress.
  • Hospitality becomes complicity when it sponsors a false gospel.

Diagnostic: What voices are we funding, forwarding, platforming, or legitimizing?

Conclusion — vv. 12–13

John does not end with the deceivers. He ends with a promised visit and completed joy. Guarding truth is not about creating a suspicious, joyless church. It protects the face-to-face fellowship in which truth and love flourish together.

Invitation/Response Direction

Jesus Christ came in the flesh, entered our world, died for our sins, and rose bodily from the grave. The proper response is not fascination with secret knowledge but trusting allegiance, repentance, baptism into Christ, and a life that continues to walk in his truth and love.

Expanded Sermon Outline: “Love With Its Eyes Open”

Opening tension

Our culture often says:

  • If you love someone, affirm everything.
  • If you believe something is true, fight everyone who disagrees.

Second John rejects both scripts. John is deeply loving and sharply discerning in the same thirteen verses.

Movement One: Love needs truth

  • John loves “in the truth” (vv. 1–2).
  • Shared truth is the ground of Christian fellowship.
  • Love severed from truth cannot recognize what ultimately harms another person.
  • The incarnation reveals that God’s love does not remain an idea; it enters reality.

Line: Love without truth may feel kind for a moment while helping deception last for years.

Movement Two: Truth must walk

  • John rejoices that believers walk in truth (v. 4).
  • Truth that never reaches conduct is merely a defended proposition.
  • Love means walking according to God’s commands (v. 6).
  • The test of doctrine is not only whether we can explain it, but whether it makes us faithful to Jesus.

Line: Truth does not merely give us a hill to die on; it gives us a road to walk on.

Movement Three: Love sometimes closes a door

  • Many deceivers have entered the world (v. 7).
  • The issue is the identity of Jesus, not a minor preference.
  • Welcoming an emissary authorizes and equips his mission (vv. 10–11).
  • A boundary can protect the deceived, the congregation, and even the deceiver from further harm.

Line: A church door can be open to people while its pulpit remains closed to deception.

Landing

John wants completed joy in face-to-face fellowship (v. 12). The purpose of discernment is not fear. It is to protect a community where the real Jesus is confessed, God’s commands are obeyed, people are genuinely loved, and joy becomes full.

Illustrations

1. The endorsement hidden inside the share button

Forwarding a clip, donating to a platform, or inviting a speaker can feel neutral. But every share gives reach, every dollar gives capacity, and every platform lends credibility. In the ancient world, a guest room and travel provisions did the same thing. John’s question is not merely whether we listened; it is whether we helped the message travel.

2. Guardrails and the mountain road

A guardrail does not hate the driver or oppose the journey. It preserves the journey by marking where the road ends and danger begins. Apostolic truth functions as a guardrail for Christian love—not imprisoning it, but helping it reach its destination.

3. Counterfeit medicine

Kind packaging cannot make counterfeit medicine healing. Giving it to someone because we do not wish to appear judgmental would not be loving. The more serious the illness, the more love cares whether the remedy is true. John treats teaching about Christ as soul-level medicine.

4. A wedding vow

Faithful love is both affection and allegiance. A spouse who says, “My love is so broad that fidelity no longer matters,” has contradicted the nature of covenant love. In the same way, love for Christ is not made larger by treating allegiance to the real Christ as optional.

Discussion and Application Questions

  1. Why does John repeatedly pair truth with love rather than choosing one as primary?
  2. What is the difference between knowing the truth and walking in the truth?
  3. How does obedience protect love from becoming mere sentiment?
  4. How does love protect obedience from becoming cold legalism?
  5. Why is the genuine humanity of Jesus essential to the gospel?
  6. How does John’s use of “antichrist” differ from popular end-times usage?
  7. What kind of “progress” can actually move a church beyond Christ?
  8. What did hospitality provide to a traveling teacher in John’s setting?
  9. Where is the line between showing kindness to a false teacher and supporting false teaching?
  10. What teachers, influencers, ministries, or messages are we currently helping to travel?
  11. How can a church maintain doctrinal boundaries without becoming suspicious or cruel?
  12. Why does John prefer face-to-face communication for the rest of his message?

Personal Inventory

  • Truth: Is there a command of Jesus I affirm but do not presently walk in?
  • Love: Has defending truth made me contemptuous toward someone Jesus calls me to love?
  • Discernment: Am I giving money, reach, or credibility to teaching I have not tested?
  • Perseverance: Where am I tempted to move beyond the simple, demanding way of Christ?
  • Presence: Who needs more than a message from me—who needs my face-to-face presence?

One-Minute Summary

Second John addresses a church threatened by traveling teachers who deny that Jesus Christ truly came in the flesh. John urges believers to remain in the apostolic truth, love one another by obeying God’s commands, and watch themselves so they do not lose the fruit of faithful ministry. They must not provide a platform or material support to teachers whose doctrine abandons Christ, because such support makes them participants in the teachers’ work. Yet the letter is not a manifesto for suspicion: it begins in love and ends in the hope of face-to-face fellowship and complete joy. Its enduring message is that truth and love must walk together.

Thirty-Second Teaching Version

Second John says the church must never choose between truth and love. Truth must become a walk, and love must take the shape of obedience. Because Jesus truly came in the flesh, teaching that replaces the incarnate Christ is not spiritual progress but deception. We should show kindness to all people, but we must not use our homes, money, platforms, or influence to help a false gospel travel.

Stay with the Jesus who came in the flesh, walk the truth you confess, love according to God’s commands, and never lend your strength to a message that leads people away from him.
