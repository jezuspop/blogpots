Revelation 9: When Warnings Meet a Hardened Heart

Trace the fifth and sixth trumpets and consider why even severe warnings do not automatically lead to repentance.

Passage and Book Study

Read alongside: Revelation 9

The chapter in one sentence The fifth and sixth trumpets unleash terrifying but bounded powers, yet the surviving people still refuse to turn from their idols and destructive ways.

Driving question: What good is being shaken awake if I still refuse to listen?

Teaching refrain: Evil destroys. God sets limits. Repentance cannot be postponed forever.

Read first: Revelation 8:13; 9:1-21. Keep 7:1-3, 6:9-11, and 11:14-15 nearby.

Companion: Revelation 8 - Bible Study Cheat Sheet. This is an expansive study resource, not a sermon manuscript. Explanations are paraphrases unless marked as quotations. Sources actually consulted are listed at the end; suggested Logos resources are identified separately.

1. Start with the Ending

The creatures are vivid enough to consume the whole lesson. But the final two verses are where the chapter tells us what the surviving people do with what they have experienced.

  • They do not repent of worshiping demons and handmade idols.
  • They do not repent of murder, sorcery, sexual immorality, or theft.
  • Catastrophe does not automatically produce a changed heart.
  • Fear, pain, and even a longing for escape are not identical to repentance.

Text: This is the response John describes in the vision (9:20-21).

Inference: The repeated attention to nonrepentance makes these judgments function as warnings as well as punishment. It does not imply every death is merely an instructional device for someone else.

Application: Ask not only what the creatures represent, but what I continue defending when God calls me to change.

The shared trumpet setting

  • The seventh seal opens in 8:1.
  • Prayer and altar fire frame the judgments in 8:3-5.
  • The first four trumpets affect land, sea, fresh water, and light.
  • The eagle announces three woes in 8:13.
  • The fifth trumpet is the first woe (9:1-12).
  • The sixth trumpet begins at 9:13; the second woe is not announced as past until 11:14.
  • The seventh trumpet sounds in 11:15, not at the end of chapter 9.

Literary caution: Chapters 10-11:13 are often called an interlude, but that does not make them irrelevant to the trumpet sequence. The vision of prophetic witness belongs to the unfolding response to judgment.

2. The Star and the Key | 9:1-2

What John sees

  • A star that has fallen from heaven to earth.
  • The star is treated as a personal agent and receives a key.
  • It opens the shaft of the abyss.
  • Smoke pours out like smoke from a great furnace, darkening the sun and air.

Observation: The key is given. The figure does not simply possess unlimited authority.

Who is the star?

Reading Supporting considerations Caution
Satan or a fallen angel Fallen-star imagery; comparison with Luke 10:18 and Revelation 12:9 Revelation 9:1 does not name Satan or narrate an original rebellion
An angel carrying out an assigned judgment A key is given; compare the angel with a key in 20:1 Do not assume the different scenes portray the same angel
A symbolic ruler or other historical figure Revelation sometimes uses heavenly imagery for earthly powers A specific identification needs evidence beyond a resemblance

Working reading: An angelic figure acting under permitted authority. Its precise identity is uncertain. Calling it Satan is possible, but more confident than the verse itself.

The IVP treatment cautions against making the fall automatically mean moral rebellion; Guzik canvasses both good-angel and evil-angel possibilities. Their disagreement is useful: neither the personal nature of the star nor the dark scene by itself settles the identification. [S1; S2]

What is the abyss?

  • Here it is a place from which hostile, destructive powers emerge.
  • Luke 8:31: Demons beg not to be sent into the abyss.
  • Revelation 11:7; 17:8: The beast is associated with it.
  • Revelation 20:1-3: Satan can be confined there.
  • Revelation 20:10, 14: The final lake of fire is a distinct image. Do not collapse every biblical term for the realm of the dead or punishment into one undifferentiated place.

Avoid: Treating the vision as a geological map of an opening in the earth or teaching that Satan is the independent ruler of final punishment. Revelation ultimately pictures his defeat.

Question: Do I give evil more authority in my imagination than this passage gives it?

3. Locusts That Do Not Eat Grass | 9:3-6

The reversal that explains the picture

Ordinary locusts devastate vegetation. These creatures are told not to harm grass, plants, or trees. Their target is people without God’s seal.

That is a major clue: John is not giving an ordinary insect report.

Four limits in the vision

  1. Target: People without God’s seal, not the sealed servants.
  2. Action: Torment, not killing.
  3. Duration: Five months.
  4. Authority: Their power is granted; it is not self-created.

Why five months? Some connect it to a locust season or lifespan. That is a proposed background, not an explanation given by John. The secure point is a stated limit. A futurist may expect five calendar months; a symbolic reading emphasizes a bounded period. The passage supplies no rule for turning these months into a modern timetable.

What kind of torment?

  • John compares it to a scorpion’s sting.
  • The imagery conveys severe suffering, not mild uneasiness.
  • People long for death but do not find it in the scene.
  • A symbolic reading need not reduce the suffering to “just in the mind.” Symbols can depict real bodily and spiritual devastation.

Pastoral care: Do not use 9:6 to shame people experiencing despair or suicidal thoughts. It describes this judgment scene; it is not a diagnostic rule that distressed people are demonic or outside God’s care. In teaching, dwell on the horror and need for rescue, not sensational descriptions.

The seal connects chapter 9 to chapter 7

  • Revelation 7:1-3 marks God’s servants before harm is released.
  • Revelation 9:4 shows protection from this particular torment.
  • Revelation 6:9-11 already shows faithful people who were killed for their testimony.
  • Therefore, the seal cannot mean believers never experience illness, injury, persecution, or death.

Working synthesis: God’s people are genuinely distinguished and protected in this vision. Their belonging to him does not make them immune to every form of suffering.

Canonical connection: Ephesians 1:13-14 speaks of being sealed with the Spirit. Revelation 9 does not itself explain the seal as a conversion ritual. Use Acts 2:38 and Romans 6:3-4 when teaching repentance, baptism, and participation in Christ, rather than forcing every conversion detail into this image.

4. Why Do the Locusts Look Like That? | 9:7-10

Read the repeated comparisons

John repeatedly uses “like” language. He communicates a terrifying composite image through things his audience knows.

Feature What it contributes to the picture What not to assume
Horses ready for battle Force and an advancing army A hidden model number for modern equipment
Something resembling gold crowns Appearance of authority or victory Proof that these beings possess God’s final victory
Human-like faces Unsettling intelligence or personal quality A complete identification with an ethnic group
Women’s hair A striking human feature within the monstrous picture That women or femininity are intrinsically evil
Lions’ teeth Ferocity and devouring threat A separate modern weapon for every body part
Iron-like breastplates Apparent resistance and battle readiness A prediction of a particular armor technology
Wings sounding like chariots Overwhelming movement and noise Necessarily helicopter rotors
Scorpion tails and stings The stated power to torment A license to replace the text with speculative engineering

The “contributes” column offers interpretive impressions, not a dictionary John supplies for each detail. Some details resist precise decoding.

Joel supplies the strongest visual background

  • Joel 1:4-7: Locust destruction and lion-like teeth.
  • Joel 2:4-5: Appearance like horses and sound like chariots.
  • Joel 2:1-11: An overwhelming army in the setting of the Day of the Lord.
  • Exodus 10:12-15: The locust plague in Egypt.

Important difference: Revelation transforms the agricultural plague into a human-targeting, abyssal assault. Similarity does not mean the passages describe an identical event.

Working reading: The locusts picture a demonic invasion with military imagery. Some interpretations emphasize corrupt teaching, oppressive power, or inner torment; others expect a future visible supernatural assault. These may capture aspects of the scene, but no one detail authorizes a confident identification of a modern army or machine.

Teaching line: The point is not that John lacked the word for a helicopter. The point is that evil is more destructive than it first appears.

5. Their King Is Called Destroyer | 9:11-12

Names that interpret the scene

  • Abaddon: A Hebrew term associated with destruction; compare Job 26:6 and Proverbs 15:11.
  • Apollyon: A Greek name meaning Destroyer.
  • The king is identified as the angel of the abyss.
  • John gives the name in two languages, making the destructive character of the ruler unmistakable.

The IVP discussion calls this power “personal but far from human.” [S3] That succinct observation helps resist reducing the scene to an ordinary insect outbreak.

Is Abaddon Satan?

  • The passage does not explicitly say so.
  • The king may be understood as Satan, a subordinate destructive angel, or another agent functioning within the judgment scene.
  • It is also uncertain whether the key-bearing figure in 9:1 and the king in 9:11 are the same figure.
  • Some scholars propose wordplay with Apollo and imperial religious associations. It is a possible additional resonance, not the plain meaning needed to understand the verse.

Compare Proverbs 30:27

Ordinary locusts have no king, yet advance together. These locusts do have a king. The contrast reinforces how unlike ordinary insects they are, though Revelation does not explicitly introduce Proverbs as a quotation.

Application: Sin often markets itself as freedom, relief, or power. The vision names the destructive end of the powers behind rebellion. Do not let this become a claim that every temptation involves a visible supernatural creature.

Transition: The first woe has passed; two remain. The limited torment gives way to lethal judgment.

6. A Voice from the Altar | 9:13-15

The scene

  • The sixth angel sounds the trumpet.
  • A voice comes from the four horns of the golden altar before God.
  • The command releases four angels bound at the great river Euphrates.
  • They have been prepared for the appointed hour, day, month, and year.
  • Their assigned purpose is to kill a third of humanity.

Why return to the altar?

  • The golden altar recalls the prayer scene in 8:3-5.
  • Judgment is not disconnected from God’s presence or the cry for justice.
  • The verse does not identify the speaker, so do not automatically assign the voice to a particular person.

What about the four angels?

  • They are bound and then released for a destructive mission.
  • Many interpreters regard them as hostile powers under restraint; the text does not explicitly label them fallen angels.
  • They are not explicitly identified with the four angels of 7:1-3.
  • Similar numbers and settings do not prove identical characters.

The appointed time

The list of hour, day, month, and year emphasizes readiness for a specified moment. It is not naturally a sum of separate durations, and it does not provide a date for modern readers to calculate.

Theological claim: Evil is not an equal god who has seized control of the story. The scene depicts permission, restraint, and appointed release. That does not answer every philosophical question about suffering or make destruction morally good in itself.

7. Why the Euphrates? | 9:14-16

The river carries a layered set of associations:

  • Genesis 15:18: A boundary in the land promise.
  • Isaiah 8:7-8: Assyrian invasion pictured as the overflowing waters of the River.
  • Jeremiah 46:10: Judgment at the Euphrates.
  • First-century setting: The Euphrates marked a major eastern frontier in Rome’s world; Parthian military power beyond it was a serious source of anxiety. The IVP discussion foregrounds this background. [S4]

Working interpretation: The location evokes threatening power from beyond the familiar boundary. The scene draws on both Israel’s prophetic memory and the Roman world’s fears.

Do not overclaim: Revelation 9 does not say the river dries up. That occurs in 16:12. Neither passage by itself identifies a modern nation as the army of 9:16.

Two hundred million

  • John hears the number of the mounted troops: twice ten thousand times ten thousand, conventionally rendered two hundred million.
  • The number overwhelms ordinary military imagination.
  • A symbolic reading takes it as a vast, terrifying host.
  • A numerical-literal reading expects that number of troops in a future event.
  • Neither reading justifies selecting a country by a contemporary population statistic.

8. The Army That Brings Death | 9:17-19

Text before speculation

  • John explicitly locates the description in his vision.
  • Riders have breastplates with fiery red, dark blue or hyacinth, and sulfur-yellow coloring; wording varies by translation.
  • Horses have lion-like heads.
  • Fire, smoke, and sulfur come from their mouths.
  • These three plagues kill a third of humanity.
  • The horses’ tails resemble snakes with heads and also cause harm.

Best starting point

Working reading: A supernatural, destructive host portrayed through military imagery. This fits the composite creatures and the connection to the abyssal assault, without requiring every figure in the two scenes to be identical.

Serious alternative: A future earthly army, possibly empowered by demonic forces, described through visionary images. Such a view still must explain the mouths, tails, and named plagues rather than simply substituting tanks and artillery.

Historical resonance: Parthian cavalry may help explain why an eastern mounted force was frightening to the first audience. It does not exhaust the monstrous features or establish a specific campaign as the complete fulfillment.

The limit remains, but severity grows

  • The fifth trumpet forbids killing.
  • The sixth permits widespread death.
  • The one-third limit means the judgment is not yet universal annihilation.
  • Do not add fractions across visions to calculate how many people must remain today. That assumes a continuous population model the text never supplies.

Pastoral implication: Read slowly enough to feel the cost. People dying are not merely clues in a prophetic puzzle.

9. They Still Did Not Repent | 9:20-21

The chapter’s moral center

The IVP commentary observes:

“these terrible judgments did not bring about repentance or a change of heart”

IVP New Testament Commentary, “The Response to the Invasion (9:20-21),” excerpt. [S5]

Interpretation: The disaster has exposed the problem, but it has not automatically healed it. The text describes refusal, not merely ignorance.

Worship and conduct belong together

What remains unchanged Textual concern Contemporary application, not a replacement meaning
Demonic and idol worship Allegiance directed away from God Ask what receives the trust and obedience owed to God
Murder Destruction of human life Reject violence and the dehumanization that encourages it
Sorcery or magic arts Practices seeking occult power Do not seek control through occult practices
Sexual immorality Sexual conduct contrary to faithfulness to God Examine secrecy, exploitation, and disobedience rather than excusing them
Theft Taking what belongs to others Examine fraud, dishonest gain, and exploitation

These are not merely defects of outsiders. Revelation 2:14, 20 has already confronted idolatrous compromise and sexual immorality within congregations.

The Old Testament exposes the irony

“They that make them are like unto them; so is every one that trusteth in them.”

Psalm 115:8, KJV. Read 115:4-8 for idols that cannot speak, see, hear, or walk. [P2]

  • Revelation 9:20 similarly describes powerless objects made by human hands.
  • Daniel 5:23: Gods of material substances cannot see, hear, or know.
  • Deuteronomy 32:17: Sacrifice to demons rather than God.
  • 1 Corinthians 10:19-22: Paul connects idolatrous participation with demonic fellowship without treating the idol as a genuine rival deity.

The irony: People suffer under destructive powers but remain loyal to what cannot save them.

The Exodus pattern returns

  • The plagues challenge Pharaoh; he repeatedly resists (Exodus 8:15, 32; 9:34-35).
  • Revelation’s surviving people likewise refuse to turn.
  • Do not add the sentence “God hardened all their hearts” to Revelation 9. The chapter emphasizes their nonrepentance without making that particular statement.

A word about sorcery

The Greek term in 9:21 belongs to the word family often transliterated pharmakeia and refers here to sorcery or magic arts. An English word’s historical relationship to that root does not make modern medicine or pharmacy the target of the verse. Context determines the meaning, not word resemblance.

What repentance would look like

  • Not merely feeling shaken, ashamed, or afraid.
  • Turning away from false worship and the practices named here.
  • Turning toward the living God in faithful obedience.
  • For a fuller account of responding to Jesus, connect Acts 2:36-41 and Romans 6:1-4: faith in the risen Lord, repentance, baptism, and a new life. Revelation 9 diagnoses refusal; it is not a complete conversion-study outline.

Teaching line: Relief asks for the pain to stop. Repentance asks what must change in me.

10. Reading the Chapter Responsibly

What is clear

  • The forces are terrifying and destructive.
  • Their activity is repeatedly bounded by commands and permissions.
  • God’s seal distinguishes his people in the fifth trumpet.
  • Judgment escalates from torment to death.
  • Survivors remain committed to idolatry and evil practices.

What remains debated

  • The identity of the fallen star and its relation to Abaddon.
  • The exact nature and historical realization of the locusts and army.
  • Whether numbers and durations describe future literal quantities or primarily symbolic realities.
  • How these scenes relate chronologically to the seals and bowls.

Working approach for this sheet

Read the images in their Old Testament and first-century setting, recognize their portrayal of demonic destruction and divine judgment, and see their warning as relevant across generations while awaiting God’s final victory. Futurist readings take the warnings seriously too, but typically locate their chief realization in a concentrated future crisis. Historical and symbolic readings must also avoid turning the scene into whatever event or idea they prefer.

Do not make these shortcuts

  • “These creatures are obviously helicopters.”
  • “The army must be a specific modern nation.”
  • “Anyone who suffers must be unsealed.”
  • “God’s people never experience physical harm.”
  • “The word sorcery condemns prescribed medicine.”
  • “If people experience enough pain, they will automatically repent.”
  • “Every modern tragedy is a judgment I can confidently explain.” Compare Jesus’ warning against ranking sufferers as worse sinners in Luke 13:1-5.

11. Discussion Questions

  1. Why is it important to read verses 20-21 before getting lost in the creatures?
  2. Where does the chapter show limits on destructive powers?
  3. What is the difference between fear of consequences and repentance?
  4. Is there something I keep defending because changing would cost comfort, money, approval, or control?
  5. What does my use of money, sexuality, influence, or truth reveal about whom I worship?
  6. How can we preach judgment without treating suffering people as objects of curiosity?
  7. What specific step of obedience should follow this study rather than another round of speculation?

12. A Short Teaching Route

  • Opening, 5 minutes: Read 9:20-21 first. Ask why a warning might fail to change someone.
  • Evil Has a Name, 10 minutes: Read 9:1-12. Explain the locusts through Joel and stress the limits. Do not spend the entire session guessing the star’s identity.
  • Evil Does Not Have the Last Word, 10 minutes: Read 9:13-19. Explain the altar, appointed time, Euphrates, and vast army. Acknowledge the human cost.
  • A Shaken Life Is Not Yet a Changed Life, 12 minutes: Return to 9:20-21. Connect Psalm 115 and the sins named in the passage.
  • Closing, 3 minutes: Invite one honest act of repentance, not a public performance of fear.

If teaching chapters 8 and 9 together: Use three movements: God Hears Our Prayers (8:1-5); God Shakes False Security (8:6-9:19); God Calls for a Changed Heart (9:20-21). Explain representative images instead of giving equal time to every feature. The chapter 8 overview supplies the six-trumpet map.

Closing question: What am I asking God to remove from my circumstances while refusing to let him change in my heart?

Five lasting thoughts:

  • Evil promises more than it can deliver and destroys more than it admits.
  • Terrifying powers are not unlimited powers.
  • Belonging to God is deeper than being spared every hardship.
  • A crisis can expose my loyalties without changing them.
  • The faithful response is repentance and allegiance to the Lamb, not fascination with destruction.
