John 13: The Basin and the Betrayal

Jesus washes his disciples’ feet, exposes betrayal, and gives love a new center as his hour arrives.

Context and source precision

The conventional seven predicate “I am” sayings extend beyond chapter 12: the way/truth/life is in 14:6, and the true vine in 15:1, 5. “Book of Signs” and “Book of Glory” are scholarly organizational labels, not headings supplied by John. Counting signs also involves interpretive choices.

Chapters 13-17 primarily address the disciples, but chapters 18-21 include exchanges with other people. Judas leaves in 13:30, and chapter 17 is prayer to the Father. Do not describe every later word as spoken only to the Twelve.

Source status: The named commentary summaries below have not been checked against their editions and page locations in this review. Treat them as inherited research leads, not verified quotations or publication-ready attributions. The form “b. Mekhilta” conflates rabbinic source conventions: Mekhilta is not a tractate of the Babylonian Talmud. The underlying foot-washing claim needs an exact source, date, and qualification before use as universal first-century practice. The textual point is secure without it: Jesus models humble service, Peter objects, and Jesus instructs his disciples to follow his example (13:1-17).

Primary check: John 14:6 and 15:1, 5.

Commentary Notes

Introduction & Context

John 13 is the great turning point of the Fourth Gospel. The public ministry is over. The “Book of Signs” (chs. 1-12) has closed, and with it the crowds, the debates in the temple courts, and the escalating confrontations with the religious establishment. Now begins the “Book of Glory” (chs. 13-21) – the intimate, final preparation of the disciples for Jesus’ departure. The focus now shifts to Jesus’ intimate instruction of his disciples, though chapters 18-21 again include other audiences. The audience has shrunk, but the stakes have never been higher.

deSilva (An Introduction to the New Testament: Contexts, Methods & Ministry Formation) situates John 13 as the opening act of the Farewell Discourse (chs. 13-17), the longest sustained block of Jesus’ teaching in any Gospel. deSilva notes that the literary shift is dramatic: the public controversies of chs. 7-12 give way to private instruction, and the tone moves from confrontation to consolation. The foot washing (vv. 1-17) functions as an enacted parable – a physical demonstration of the cross before the cross happens. deSilva emphasizes that John’s theological genius is on full display: the same Jesus who “knew that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God” (v. 3) is the one who strips off his outer garment and kneels with a basin. Sovereignty and servanthood are not in tension; they are fused.

Carson (The Gospel According to John, Pillar New Testament Commentary) argues that John 13 is structured around two dramatic revelations: who Jesus truly is (the servant-King who washes feet) and who Judas truly is (the disciple who betrays). Carson notes that the chapter is saturated with irony. Jesus knows everything – who will betray him, where he came from, where he is going – and his response to total knowledge is total service. Meanwhile, Judas, who has walked with Jesus for three years, remains opaque to the other disciples even as Satan enters him. The light and the darkness are in the same room.

IVP Bible Background Commentary (Keener) provides essential cultural context for the foot washing. In the ancient Near East, foot washing was the task of the lowest household slave – not even a Jewish slave could be compelled to wash feet under rabbinic law (b. Mekhilta on Exod 21:2). Guests were typically offered water to wash their own feet, or a Gentile slave performed the task. For a rabbi – let alone the Messiah – to wash his disciples’ feet was a shocking inversion of every social norm. Keener emphasizes that the disciples would have been scandalized, which explains Peter’s violent protest.

Stern (Jewish New Testament Commentary) reads John 13 in the context of Passover. The meal is taking place “before the Feast of Passover” (v. 1), and Stern argues that the timing is theologically loaded. Just as the original Passover lamb was selected before it was sacrificed (Exod 12:3-6), Jesus is here preparing himself and his disciples for the sacrifice to come. The foot washing is priestly preparation – the lamb is being readied for slaughter, and the disciples are being cleansed for the new covenant community that will emerge from the cross.

Morris (The Gospel According to John, NICNT) draws attention to the narrative’s emotional register. The opening verse – “Having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end” (v. 1) – is, Morris argues, one of the most important sentences in the Gospel. The phrase εἰς τέλος (eis telos) can mean both “to the end” (temporal) and “to the uttermost” (qualitative). Morris insists John intends both: Jesus loved them all the way to the end of his life, and he loved them to the absolute limit of love’s capacity. Everything that follows in chs. 13-21 is an exposition of this love.

Keener (The Gospel of John: A Commentary) adds that the foot washing must be read as a prophetic sign-act in the tradition of the Old Testament prophets. Just as Ezekiel lay on his side (Ezek 4), Jeremiah smashed a pot (Jer 19), and Isaiah walked naked (Isa 20), Jesus performs an embodied parable. The basin and towel are his prophetic props. But unlike the prophets who pointed forward to God’s action, Jesus is God’s action. The foot washing does not merely symbolize the cross – it anticipates it, interprets it, and commands its replication.

Literary Placement in John’s Narrative Arc

John 13 occupies the pivotal hinge between the two halves of the Gospel. The structure is deliberate:

  • Before John 13: The “Book of Signs” (chs. 1-12) has presented Jesus to the world through signs and revelatory sayings. The conventional seven predicate “I AM” sayings extend through chapters 14-15. The world’s response has been divided: some believe, many reject, and the authorities have formally decided to kill him (11:53). Chapter 12 closes with a summary judgment: “Though he had done so many signs before them, they still did not believe in him” (12:37).
  • John 13 itself: The scene shifts from public to private, from signs to service, from the world to “his own” (τοὺς ἰδίους, tous idious). The foot washing inaugurates the Farewell Discourse (chs. 13-17), the most intimate and theologically dense block of teaching in the Gospels. The departure of Judas (v. 30) creates a purified community – the eleven who will receive the new commandment, the promise of the Spirit, and the High Priestly Prayer.
  • After John 13: The Farewell Discourse continues through chs. 14-16 (comfort, the vine, the Spirit, persecution), culminates in the High Priestly Prayer (ch. 17), and gives way to the Passion narrative (chs. 18-19) and the Resurrection (chs. 20-21).

The transition is marked by one of the most haunting sentences in Scripture: “And it was night” (v. 30). Judas departs into literal and metaphorical darkness. From this point forward, every word Jesus speaks is bathed in the knowledge that the cross is hours away.

Key Themes

  1. Sovereign Servanthood – Jesus washes feet not in spite of his divine authority but because of it. Verse 3 is the theological engine: “knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God.” Full awareness of his cosmic identity produces not domination but a basin and a towel. This redefines power.

  2. The Cross as Enacted Parable – The foot washing is not merely a lesson in humility; it is a preview of Calvary. Jesus removes his outer garment (v. 4), just as he will lay down his life. He takes it up again (v. 12), just as he will take up his life in resurrection. Carson calls the foot washing “the cross in miniature.”

  3. Love “to the End” (εἰς τέλος) – The opening verse sets the theological frame for everything that follows in chs. 13-21. Jesus’ love is both temporal (to the very end of his life) and qualitative (to the uttermost limit). The foot washing, the new commandment, and the cross are all expressions of this single, inexhaustible love.

  4. Light and Darkness – John’s cosmic dualism reaches a dramatic climax in v. 30: “And it was night.” The departure of Judas into darkness is not mere narrative detail; it is theological statement. The betrayer leaves the light and enters the realm of Satan. Meanwhile, Jesus speaks of glorification (vv. 31-32). The darkness deepens, but glory begins.

  5. The New Commandment and the Identity of the Church – “A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you” (v. 34). The newness is not in the command to love (Lev 19:18) but in the standard: “as I have loved you” – sacrificial, cross-shaped, foot-washing love. The church’s distinguishing mark is not its doctrine alone but its love (v. 35).

  6. Betrayal and Sovereign Knowledge – Jesus knows who will betray him from the beginning (v. 11, 18). He is not a victim of circumstances but a sovereign Lord who walks into his own death with open eyes. The morsel given to Judas (v. 26) is not entrapment; it is a final act of hospitality to the one who will destroy him.

  7. Peter’s Overconfidence and Human Fragility – Peter’s protest at the foot washing (vv. 6-8) and his boast of loyalty (v. 37) reveal a man who loves Jesus fiercely but understands him poorly. His failure is predicted (v. 38) but not final. Peter is a portrait of every disciple who overestimates their own strength and underestimates the power of the night.

  8. Glory Through the Cross – “Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him” (v. 31). This is triggered not by a miracle or a triumph but by the departure of the betrayer. The darkest moment – the point of no return toward the cross – is when Jesus declares that glory has arrived. John’s theology is relentless: glory comes through the cross, not despite it.

  9. Cleansing and Participation – Jesus tells Peter, “Unless I wash you, you have no share with me” (v. 8). The foot washing is not optional hospitality; it is the means of participation in Jesus’ community. There is no share in the Master’s life without submission to the Master’s service. The language echoes priestly washing (Exod 30:19-21) and covenantal belonging.

  10. Example and Imitation – “I have given you an example, that you also should do just as I have done to you” (v. 15). Jesus does not merely teach servanthood; he enacts it and commands its replication. The Greek ὑπόδειγμα (hypodeigma) means more than “illustration” – it means a pattern to be followed, a template for life.

RS’s Notes: John 13 – “The Basin and the Betrayal”: The King Who Kneels

Introduction

John 13 is the chapter where Jesus closes the door on the world and opens a door to his own. The crowds are gone. The temple debates are over. The signs have been performed and the verdict is in – “they still did not believe” (12:37). Now, in a rented room in Jerusalem, on the eve of his execution, Jesus gathers the Twelve around a table and does something no one expects. He strips off his outer garment, wraps a towel around his waist, and begins to wash feet. The King kneels. The Master becomes the slave. And in that act, he interprets everything that is about to happen on the cross.

The chapter moves in three dramatic acts: the enacted parable (vv. 1-17), where Jesus washes feet and redefines greatness; the unmasking (vv. 18-30), where Jesus identifies his betrayer and Judas departs into the night; and the new marching orders (vv. 31-38), where Jesus gives the new commandment, announces his departure, and predicts Peter’s denial. Each act builds on the one before. The foot washing shows what love looks like; the betrayal shows what its rejection looks like; the new commandment commissions the disciples to replicate it.

Carson frames the chapter as the hinge of the entire Gospel – the transition from revelation to preparation, from signs to sacrifice. Wright reads the foot washing as a deliberate symbolic action in which Jesus reinterprets the coming cross as an act of service rather than defeat. deSilva emphasizes the literary architecture: John 13 opens the Farewell Discourse, which runs through chapter 17 and constitutes the longest sustained block of Jesus’ private teaching in any Gospel. Morris calls v. 1 – “having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end” – the “heading over everything that follows.”

One-sentence summary: In the upper room, Jesus washes feet to preview the cross, identifies his betrayer to demonstrate his sovereignty, and gives a new commandment to define the community that will carry his mission after he is gone.

****Key Thought**** The one who held all authority in heaven and earth picked up a towel. The one who spoke galaxies into existence knelt on a dirty floor. This is not weakness dressed up as theology – it is the purest expression of divine power the world has ever seen. God’s glory does not look like a throne. It looks like a basin.

1. The Foot Washing: When God Kneels (vv. 1-17)

> Now before the Feast of the Passover, when Jesus knew that his hour had come to depart out of this world to the Father, having loved his own who were in the world, he loved them to the end. During supper… Jesus, knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God, rose from supper. He laid aside his outer garments, and taking a towel, tied it around his waist. Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash the disciples’ feet. – John 13:1-5 (ESV)

Main Point: The foot washing is not a lesson in good manners. It is a prophetic sign-act – an enacted parable of the cross. Jesus does not merely teach servanthood; he performs it, interprets it, and commands its replication. And the theological engine of the entire scene is v. 3: Jesus washes feet not in spite of knowing who he is but because he knows who he is. Full awareness of his divine origin, divine destiny, and divine authority produces not a throne but a basin.

Carson (Pillar) provides essential analysis of the relationship between vv. 3 and 4-5. The sequence is deliberate: “knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands, and that he had come from God and was going back to God, [he] rose from supper… and began to wash the disciples’ feet.” Carson argues that the participial clause in v. 3 is causal: because Jesus knew his identity, he served. This is the Johannine version of the Christ Hymn (Phil 2:5-11) in narrative form. The one who is equal with God does not consider equality something to be grasped but empties himself, taking the form of a servant. Carson writes: “The greatness of the act is measured by the greatness of the one who performs it.”

Keener (The Gospel of John) documents the cultural shock of the scene. In the ancient Mediterranean world, foot washing was performed by the lowest household slave, and even then, rabbinic tradition exempted Jewish slaves from the task (Mekhilta on Exod 21:2; b. Kiddushin 22b). Wives might wash their husbands’ feet; disciples might wash their master’s feet. But for a master to wash his disciples’ feet – that had no precedent and no parallel. Keener notes that the disciples’ silence (only Peter speaks) likely reflects their total bewilderment. They did not know what to say because nothing in their cultural framework could make sense of what they were seeing.

Morris (NICNT) highlights Peter’s protest in vv. 6-8 as a pivotal moment. Peter’s “Lord, do you wash my feet?” (v. 6) uses the emphatic pronoun σύ (sy, “you” – as in, “You – you – are washing my feet?”). The word order in Greek places the pronouns in emphatic contrast: σύ μου νίπτεις τοὺς πόδας – “You, of me, wash the feet?” Peter understands the social impossibility of what is happening. But Jesus’ response is devastating: “If I do not wash you, you have no share with me” (v. 8). Morris argues this is not about foot washing per se but about the cross. Unless Peter accepts Jesus’ self-humbling service – which will reach its climax at Calvary – he cannot participate in what Jesus is doing. The foot washing is the cross in advance.

Wright reads the removal and resumption of Jesus’ garment (vv. 4, 12) as a deliberate echo of John 10:17-18: “I lay down my life that I may take it up again. No one takes it from me, but I lay it down of my own accord, and I have authority to take it up again.” The outer garment (τὰ ἱμάτια, ta himatia) is removed willingly and put back on – just as Jesus’ life will be laid down willingly and taken up again. Wright argues that John wants the reader to see the foot washing not merely as a moral example but as a miniature Passion narrative: stripping, service, death, and rising.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) places the foot washing in the context of Passover preparation. Jewish tradition required ritual purification before participating in the Passover meal. Stern suggests that Jesus is performing a kind of prophetic re-interpretation: the old purification rites point to a new cleansing, accomplished not by ritual baths but by the servant-King’s willingness to kneel. The disciples are being prepared not just for a meal but for a new covenant.

deSilva notes that Jesus’ words in v. 7 – “What I am doing you do not understand now, but afterward you will understand” – are programmatic for the entire Farewell Discourse. The disciples will not grasp the meaning of the foot washing until after the cross and resurrection reveal it. This is John’s characteristic retrospective hermeneutic: events are performed now and understood later (cf. 2:22; 12:16). The foot washing is prophetic theater whose interpretation requires the cross.

Greek Note: The verb νίπτω (nipto) in vv. 5-14 means to wash a part of the body (here, feet), distinct from λούω (louo) in v. 10, which means to bathe the whole body. Jesus’ statement in v. 10 – “The one who has bathed (ὁ λελουμένος) does not need to wash (νίψασθαι), except for his feet” – draws a careful distinction. The one who has been fully cleansed (possibly a reference to the disciples’ prior faith/conversion) needs only the ongoing, daily cleansing of sanctification. The word ὑπόδειγμα (hypodeigma, v. 15, “example/pattern”) appears in the NT also in Hebrews 4:11 and 8:5, always denoting a model to be imitated. Jesus’ service is not merely inspirational – it is prescriptive.

OT Connection: The foot washing evokes the priestly consecration rituals of Exodus 29 and Leviticus 8, where Moses washed Aaron and his sons before they could serve at the altar. Jesus is simultaneously the one who consecrates and the one who serves – priest and servant in one act. The connection to Isaiah 52:13-53:12 is also unmistakable: the Servant who is “high and lifted up” (52:13) is the same one who is “despised and rejected” (53:3). John 13 shows us the Servant’s exaltation expressed through humiliation.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Churches of Christ and Christian Churches have a long tradition of practicing foot washing, though its frequency and form vary widely. Olbricht and other Restoration scholars have argued that John 13:14-15 – “If I then, your Lord and Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another’s feet” – establishes at minimum a principle and arguably a practice. Whether practiced literally or metaphorically, the Restoration Movement’s emphasis on following the pattern of Jesus’ teaching finds clear warrant here. The question is not whether Jesus commanded service but whether we take the command seriously enough to let it reshape our congregational life.

****Application**** Most churches celebrate communion but skip the basin. We remember the cross but forget the towel. Jesus did not say, “Admire what I have done.” He said, “Do as I have done to you” (v. 15). The application is concrete: Where in your congregation’s life is there literal, uncomfortable, hands-and-knees service? Not the service that gets applause – the service that gets ignored. Cleaning toilets after the potluck. Visiting the shut-in nobody else visits. Sitting with the grieving family when there are no words left. The basin is not a metaphor for people who can afford metaphors. It is a command for people willing to kneel.

2. The Betrayal: Darkness at the Table (vv. 18-30)

> “I am not speaking of all of you; I know whom I have chosen. But the Scripture will be fulfilled, ‘He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.’… After receiving the morsel of bread, [Judas] immediately went out. And it was night.” – John 13:18, 30 (ESV)

Main Point: The betrayal of Judas is not a disruption of God’s plan; it is anticipated by Scripture, identified by Jesus, and permitted under divine sovereignty. Jesus is not caught off guard. He chose Judas knowing what Judas would do (6:70-71). He gives Judas the morsel – a sign of honor at a Jewish meal – even as Satan enters him. And then he releases Judas into the night with words of devastating calm: “What you are going to do, do quickly” (v. 27). This is sovereign control exercised at the point of deepest betrayal.

Carson (Pillar) emphasizes that Jesus’ foreknowledge of the betrayal does not reduce Judas to a puppet. The interplay between divine sovereignty and human responsibility is one of John’s deepest theological tensions, and Carson argues that John refuses to resolve it. Jesus “knew from the beginning who those were who did not believe, and who it was who would betray him” (6:64). Yet Judas acts freely – he chooses greed (12:6), he opens the door to Satan (13:27), he departs of his own will. Carson writes: “The mystery of Judas is the mystery of evil itself – freely chosen yet falling within the sweep of God’s redemptive plan.”

Keener (The Gospel of John) provides cultural background on the morsel (ψωμίον, psomion, v. 26). At a formal Jewish meal, the host would dip bread into a common dish (often a mixture of dates, nuts, and wine called charoset at Passover) and offer it to a guest as a mark of special favor. By giving the morsel to Judas, Jesus is performing a final act of hospitality – even intimacy – toward the man who will betray him. Keener notes the devastating irony: the gesture meant to honor becomes the trigger for Satan’s entry. Grace refused becomes the occasion of judgment.

Morris (NICNT) draws attention to the phrase “Satan entered into him” (v. 27). Morris notes that this is one of only two places in John where Satan is named directly (the other being 13:2, where the devil “had already put it into the heart of Judas Iscariot, Simon’s son, to betray him”). John’s Gospel generally avoids the language of demonic possession common in the Synoptics; that he uses it here underscores the cosmic dimension of what is happening. This is not merely a human failure. It is a spiritual invasion – the prince of darkness making his move through a willing vessel.

Wright reads “And it was night” (v. 30) as one of the most theologically charged sentences in the Fourth Gospel. John has been developing the light-darkness motif since the Prologue: “The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it” (1:5). Throughout the Gospel, darkness represents unbelief, spiritual blindness, and alienation from God (3:19-21; 8:12; 9:4; 12:35-36, 46). When Judas walks into the night, he is walking out of the light and into the dominion of the one who rules the darkness. The sentence is three words in Greek (ἦν δὲ νύξ, en de nyx) and they carry the weight of the entire Gospel’s theology.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) highlights Jesus’ quotation of Psalm 41:9 in v. 18: “He who ate my bread has lifted his heel against me.” Stern notes that in Jewish culture, to eat someone’s bread was to enter into a covenant of trust and friendship. To then “lift the heel” – a gesture of contempt or aggression, like a horse kicking – was the deepest possible violation of relational loyalty. Judas’s betrayal is not merely a strategic defection; it is a covenant violation, a shattering of table fellowship, a sin against hospitality itself.

deSilva observes that the other disciples’ confusion about who the betrayer is (vv. 22, 28-29) is itself significant. They have eaten with Judas, traveled with Judas, and ministered alongside Judas for three years – and they cannot identify him as the traitor. deSilva argues that John is making a point about the hiddenness of evil. Hypocrisy, by definition, is invisible from the outside. The church has always contained those who appear faithful but are not. The disciples’ inability to spot Judas is a warning against naive assumptions about the spiritual state of those around us.

Lexham Research Commentary connects the departure of Judas to the literary structure of the chapter. Once Judas leaves (v. 30), the remaining eleven become the audience for the new commandment (vv. 31-35) and the prediction of Peter’s denial (vv. 36-38). The purification of the community – the removal of the traitor – precedes the commissioning of the community. This pattern echoes the Old Testament: Israel had to be separated from Egypt before receiving the Law at Sinai.

Greek Note: The word ψωμίον (psomion, vv. 26-27, 30) is a diminutive of ψωμός (psomos, “morsel, piece of bread”). It appears only in John 13 in the entire New Testament. The word νύξ (nyx, “night,” v. 30) carries the full weight of John’s light-darkness theology. It is not a clock reading; it is a theological verdict. Judas enters the realm where Satan rules and the light of Christ does not reach.

OT Connection: Psalm 41:9 (quoted in v. 18) belongs to a psalm of David about betrayal by a trusted friend during a time of illness. David’s experience prefigures Jesus’ experience: the trusted companion becomes the mortal enemy. The broader pattern of betrayal-within-the-covenant runs through Scripture – from Cain and Abel, through Absalom’s rebellion against David, through the treachery of Israel’s kings – and reaches its climax at this table in Jerusalem.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has historically emphasized the purity of the church and the integrity of its members, particularly at the Lord’s Table. John 13:18-30 provides both a warning and a realistic portrait: even among Jesus’ handpicked disciples, there was a betrayer. The church will never be perfectly pure this side of the eschaton. The Restoration Movement’s concern for faithful membership must be balanced with the humility of knowing that Jesus himself ate with his betrayer – and washed his feet.

****Application**** Two pastoral realities emerge from this passage. First, betrayal happens inside the church, not just outside it. The deepest wounds come from the people closest to us – the elder who embezzles, the friend who gossips, the spouse who walks away. Jesus was not surprised by Judas. We should not be destroyed by the Judases in our own experience. Second, notice what Jesus did not do. He did not expose Judas publicly. He did not retaliate. He gave him bread. He gave him a chance. And then he let him go. There is a time for confrontation (Matt 18) and a time for release. Wisdom is knowing the difference.

3. The New Commandment: Love Redefined (vv. 31-35)

> “A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another. By this all people will know that you are my disciples, if you have love for one another.” – John 13:34-35 (ESV)

Main Point: Jesus calls this commandment “new” (καινήν, kainen) – but love was already commanded in Leviticus 19:18. What makes it new? Not the verb but the standard. The old commandment said, “Love your neighbor as yourself.” The new commandment says, “Love one another as I have loved you.” The standard is no longer self-love but Christ-love – sacrificial, foot-washing, cross-shaped, enemy-embracing love. And this love is not optional enrichment for the spiritually advanced. It is the identifying mark of the Christian community. The world will know we belong to Jesus not by our worship style, our theological precision, or our political positions – but by how we love each other.

Carson (Pillar) argues that the “newness” of the commandment operates on multiple levels. First, it is new in standard: the measure of love is no longer “as yourself” but “as I have loved you” – which means love that washes feet, endures betrayal, and goes to the cross. Second, it is new in context: it belongs to the new covenant inaugurated by Jesus’ death and resurrection (cf. Jer 31:31-34). Third, it is new in scope: the love command is given specifically to the community of disciples and will become the internal ethic of the church. Carson emphasizes that this love is not a vague disposition but a concrete practice – it looks like the foot washing that just happened.

Morris (NICNT) notes the placement of the new commandment immediately after Judas’s departure and Jesus’ declaration of glorification (vv. 31-32). The sequence is significant: the traitor leaves, glory is proclaimed, and love is commanded. Morris argues that the new commandment is the community’s response to the cross. Because Jesus loved us to the end, we are to love one another to the end. The cross creates the community; the commandment defines it.

Wright reads the new commandment in the context of Jesus’ impending departure. Jesus is about to leave, and the disciples will need a way to make him visible in the world. That way is love. Wright argues that the love of the Christian community is meant to be an “epistemological sign” – evidence that Jesus is real and that his mission continues. “By this all people will know” (v. 35) is not a wish; it is a declaration of how the gospel spreads. The church’s love is its most powerful apologetic.

Keener (The Gospel of John) provides background on the Jewish love commands. Leviticus 19:18 (“love your neighbor as yourself”) was already considered by many rabbis to be the summary of the Torah (cf. Rabbi Akiva, Sifra Kedoshim 4:12). The Qumran community (1QS 1:9-10) commanded love for the “sons of light” and hatred for the “sons of darkness.” Jesus’ command surpasses both: the standard is not self-love or tribal love but cruciform love. Keener notes that the early church’s reputation for mutual love was one of its most powerful evangelistic tools – even the pagan Lucian of Samosata observed, “Their first lawgiver persuaded them that they are all brothers.”

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) connects the new commandment to the Jewish concept of imitatio Dei – the imitation of God. In Deuteronomy 10:18-19, Israel is told to love the stranger because God loves the stranger. In John 13:34, the pattern is identical but intensified: love one another because Jesus loved you. Stern argues that for Jewish believers, the new commandment does not abolish the Torah’s love ethic but brings it to its fullest expression through the person and work of Messiah.

deSilva emphasizes that v. 35 – “By this all people will know that you are my disciples” – is the Johannine equivalent of Matthew 28:19-20 (the Great Commission). Both passages describe how the church makes Jesus known to the world. In Matthew, it is through going and teaching. In John, it is through loving. The two are not contradictory but complementary: the church’s mission includes both proclamation and demonstration.

Greek Note: The phrase ἐντολὴν καινήν (entolen kainen, “new commandment”) uses καινός (kainos), which in Greek means “new in quality or nature” (as opposed to νέος, neos, “new in time”). The commandment is not merely recent; it is qualitatively different. The verb ἀγαπάω (agapao) appears here and throughout John’s writings as the characteristic word for divine love – love that is volitional, sacrificial, and unconditional. It is not φιλέω (phileo, friendship love) or ἔρως (eros, romantic love) but the kind of love that goes to a cross.

OT Connection: Jeremiah 31:31-34 prophesied a “new covenant” (בְּרִית חֲדָשָׁה, berit chadashah) in which God would write his law on his people’s hearts. Jesus’ new commandment is the content of that new covenant law. The old covenant was inscribed on stone tablets; the new covenant is enacted through a basin and a cross and written on the hearts of those who witness both. Ezekiel 36:26-27 promised a “new heart” and a “new spirit” that would enable obedience. The new commandment presupposes the new heart.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has always held that the church should be identifiable by its fidelity to the New Testament pattern. John 13:35 adds a dimension that can be neglected: the church is identifiable not only by correct doctrine and practice but by its love. Olbricht and others have argued that the Restoration Movement’s emphasis on “speaking where the Bible speaks” must include speaking the language of love as loudly as the language of doctrine. A church that is doctrinally precise but relationally cold has missed the new commandment. The pattern includes the basin.

****Application**** Here is the uncomfortable truth: if an outsider visited your congregation for a month and never heard a sermon, would they be able to identify your community as belonging to Jesus? Not by your sign out front or your order of worship – but by the way members treat each other? By the way conflict is handled? By the way the lonely are included, the grieving are accompanied, the difficult are loved? Jesus did not say, “By this all people will know you are my disciples – if you have correct theology.” He said love. The new commandment is the church’s public witness. When we fail at love, we fail at mission.

4. Glory in the Darkness: The Paradox of the Cross (vv. 31-32)

> When he had gone out, Jesus said, “Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him. If God is glorified in him, God will also glorify him in himself, and glorify him at once.” – John 13:31-32 (ESV)

Main Point: The timing of this declaration is everything. Jesus announces glorification not after a miracle, not after a triumph, not at the transfiguration – but immediately after Judas walks out the door to set the crucifixion in motion. The darkest moment – the point of no return toward the cross – is when Jesus says, “Now is the Son of Man glorified.” This is John’s theology distilled to its essence: glory comes through the cross, not despite it. The departure of the betrayer is the trigger for the glorification sequence. What the world calls defeat, God calls glory.

Carson (Pillar) notes the remarkable density of the word “glorified” (δοξάζω, doxazo) in vv. 31-32 – it appears five times in two verses. Carson argues that this concentration is deliberate. John is hammering the point: the cross is glory. Not will be glory, not leads to glory, but is glory. The aorist tense (ἐδοξάσθη, edoxasthe, “was glorified”) treats the crucifixion as already accomplished, even though it is still hours away. In John’s theology, the “hour” that Jesus has been anticipating throughout the Gospel (2:4; 7:30; 8:20; 12:23, 27) has now decisively arrived.

Morris (NICNT) observes that the glorification language in vv. 31-32 picks up the theme introduced in 12:23-28, where Jesus said, “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified. Truly, truly, I say to you, unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.” Morris argues that John’s concept of glory (δόξα, doxa) is a radical reinterpretation of the Hebrew kavod (כָּבוֹד). In the Old Testament, God’s glory was associated with fire, cloud, thunder, and overwhelming power. In John, God’s glory is revealed in a basin of water and a cross of wood. The glory has not diminished; our understanding of power has been transformed.

Wright reads vv. 31-32 as the theological headline for the entire Passion narrative. Everything that follows – the arrest, the trials, the flogging, the crucifixion – is to be read under this banner: “Now is the Son of Man glorified.” Wright argues that John is deliberately subverting the Roman imperial ideology of glory (military victory, public spectacle, domination) with a counter-narrative of glory through self-giving love. The cross is not Rome’s triumph over a failed messiah; it is God’s triumph over the powers of darkness through the weapon they least expect – sacrifice.

Keener (The Gospel of John) situates the glorification theme within Jewish apocalyptic expectations. In Daniel 7:13-14, “one like a son of man” is given glory, dominion, and an everlasting kingdom. Jesus identifies himself as this Son of Man but redefines the path to dominion. It does not come through military conquest (as the Maccabees attempted) or political negotiation (as the Sadducees practiced) but through death and resurrection. Keener notes that the early church’s proclamation of a crucified Messiah was scandalous in both Jewish and Greco-Roman contexts (1 Cor 1:23) precisely because it inverted every expectation of how glory works.

Greek Note: The word δόξα (doxa, “glory”) appears over 40 times in the Gospel of John – more than in any other Gospel. Its root meaning in classical Greek was “opinion” or “reputation,” but in the LXX it translates the Hebrew כָּבוֹד (kavod), meaning the weighty, manifest presence of God. John fuses both senses: the cross reveals who God truly is (his “reputation”) and makes his presence tangibly manifest (his “weight”). The verb δοξάζω (doxazo) in vv. 31-32 is in a chain of mutual glorification: the Son glorifies the Father; the Father glorifies the Son. The cross is the event where this mutual glorification reaches its apex.

OT Connection: Isaiah 52:13 – “Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted” – uses the language of exaltation for the Suffering Servant. John applies this same paradox to Jesus: he is “lifted up” on the cross (3:14; 8:28; 12:32-34) and this lifting up is his exaltation. The language of glory-through-suffering runs from Isaiah through the Psalms of lament through Daniel’s Son of Man and reaches its fulfillment in John 13:31-32.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has historically been more comfortable with the language of obedience, faithfulness, and right practice than with the language of glory and mystery. John 13:31-32 challenges that tendency. The cross is not merely a mechanism for salvation (though it is that); it is the revelation of God’s character. To preach the cross rightly is to preach glory – not as triumphalism but as the beautiful, terrible self-giving of God. The church needs both doctrinal precision and doxological wonder.

****Application**** If glory comes through the cross, then it also comes through the crosses we bear. The congregation member battling cancer with fierce faith – that is glory. The single mother raising children in faithfulness when no one notices – that is glory. The church that stays in a declining neighborhood instead of relocating to the suburbs – that is glory. We must stop equating glory with success, growth, and public recognition. John’s theology insists that glory looks like sacrifice. Ask your congregation: Where do you see God’s glory? Then point them to the cross.

5. Peter’s Overconfidence: The Night That Is Coming (vv. 36-38)

> Simon Peter said to him, “Lord, where are you going?” Jesus answered him, “Where I am going you cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterward.” Peter said to him, “Lord, why can I not follow you now? I will lay down my life for you.” Jesus answered, “Will you lay down your life for me? Truly, truly, I say to you, the rooster will not crow till you have denied me three times.” – John 13:36-38 (ESV)

Main Point: Peter is the most sympathetic failure in the New Testament. His problem is not a lack of love – he genuinely means it when he says he will die for Jesus. His problem is a lack of self-knowledge. He overestimates his own courage and underestimates the power of the night that is coming. Jesus’ response is not angry but sorrowful: not “How dare you?” but “Will you really?” The prediction of Peter’s denial is simultaneously a warning, a prophecy, and a hidden promise – because the word “afterward” (v. 36) implies that Peter’s failure will not be the end of his story.

Carson (Pillar) notes that Peter’s question – “Lord, where are you going?” (v. 36) – is answered more fully in 14:1-6 (the Way, the Truth, and the Life). Here, Jesus gives only a partial answer: “Where I am going you cannot follow me now, but you will follow afterward.” Carson argues that “afterward” (ὕστερον, hysteron) refers both to Peter’s restoration after the resurrection and to Peter’s eventual martyrdom (cf. 21:18-19, where Jesus predicts that Peter will indeed “stretch out his hands” in death). Peter’s boast will be fulfilled – but not yet, and not by his own strength.

Keener (The Gospel of John) provides cultural context for Peter’s pledge. In the ancient Mediterranean honor-shame culture, a public declaration of willingness to die for one’s master was the highest expression of loyalty. Peter is not posturing; he is making a culturally intelligible claim that he is the most devoted disciple. But Keener notes the irony: the very confidence that leads Peter to pledge his life is the same overconfidence that will lead him to deny he ever knew Jesus. Self-reliance is the enemy of faithfulness.

Morris (NICNT) observes that Jesus’ response – “The rooster will not crow till you have denied me three times” (v. 38) – is stated with absolute certainty. There is no “might” or “could.” Jesus knows Peter better than Peter knows himself. Morris argues that this is both a judgment and a mercy. The prediction strips Peter of his illusions, but it also gives him a marker: when the rooster crows, he will know that Jesus saw it coming. And if Jesus saw the failure coming and still loved him (v. 1), then the failure is not the end.

Wright reads Peter’s denial in the broader context of John’s “following” language. Throughout the Gospel, Jesus calls people to “follow” him (1:43; 8:12; 10:27; 12:26). Peter wants to follow now, but he is not yet ready. The cross must happen first. Peter cannot follow Jesus to the cross because Peter does not yet understand what the cross means. After the resurrection, Jesus will reinstate Peter with a threefold “Do you love me?” (21:15-17) that mirrors the threefold denial – and then he will say, “Follow me” (21:19). Peter’s story is not one of permanent failure but of failure redeemed.

Stern (Jewish NT Commentary) connects Peter’s boast to the broader biblical pattern of overconfident servants. David’s declaration “The LORD who delivered me from the paw of the lion and from the paw of the bear will deliver me from the hand of this Philistine” (1 Sam 17:37) worked because David relied on God’s power, not his own. Peter’s declaration fails because it is grounded in self-reliance: “I will lay down my life for you.” The pronouns are the problem. Faithfulness comes not from our strength but from God’s.

deSilva notes that Peter’s trajectory in John 13 – from protest at the foot washing (vv. 6-8), to over-eagerness (“not my feet only but also my hands and my head!” v. 9), to the pledge of loyalty (v. 37), to the prediction of denial (v. 38) – reveals a man who is consistently a half-step off. Peter is not wicked; he is simply not yet transformed. That transformation will come, but it will require the cross, the resurrection, the Spirit, and a breakfast on the beach (21:1-19).

Greek Note: The word σκάνδαλον (skandalon, “stumbling block/cause of falling”) does not appear directly in John 13, but the concept permeates the passage. Peter’s overconfidence is itself a σκάνδαλον – a trap set by his own self-assessment. The word ἀρνέομαι (arneomai, “to deny,” v. 38) means not just “to say no” but “to disown, to repudiate a relationship.” Peter will not merely fail to speak up; he will actively deny any connection to Jesus. The depth of the denial makes the depth of the restoration (ch. 21) all the more astonishing.

OT Connection: Peter’s overconfidence echoes Proverbs 16:18 – “Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall.” More specifically, it recalls the pattern of the patriarchs: Abraham, who would lie about Sarah twice despite God’s promises; Moses, who struck the rock in self-reliance despite God’s instruction; David, who took Bathsheba despite being the man after God’s own heart. The biblical pattern is consistent: the most faithful servants are capable of the most devastating failures when they rely on themselves instead of God.

Restoration Movement Connection: The Restoration Movement has always valued bold faith and courageous action – the willingness to leave denominational structures, plant churches, cross oceans with the gospel. Peter’s story is a cautionary tale for this tradition: zeal without self-knowledge is a recipe for failure. Olbricht has written about the danger of confusing confidence in the gospel with confidence in ourselves. The Restoration Movement needs Peters – bold, passionate, willing to jump out of the boat. But it also needs the humility to admit that apart from Christ’s sustaining grace, even the boldest disciple will fail before dawn.

****Application**** Every congregation has Peters – people who love Jesus fiercely, volunteer for everything, and would genuinely mean it if they said they would die for the faith. These are often the people who crash the hardest. The pastoral word from John 13:36-38 is twofold: (1) To the Peters: your courage is real, but it is not sufficient. You will need more than willpower to survive the night that is coming. You will need the sustaining grace of the one who washed your feet. (2) To the congregation watching the Peters fall: the rooster is not the end of the story. Peter denied Jesus three times and was restored three times (21:15-17). Failure does not disqualify. What disqualifies is refusing to come back.

Conclusion

John 13 is the chapter where the door closes and the room gets small. The crowds are gone, the miracles have been performed, the public case has been made. What remains is a room, a basin, a towel, a loaf of bread, and a handful of confused disciples who do not yet understand that they are being prepared for the most important days in the history of the world.

In this room, the King of the universe kneels on a dirty floor and washes feet. The trusted disciple walks into the night with a morsel of bread and a heart full of Satan. The most loyal follower pledges to die and is told he will deny. And through it all, Jesus speaks of glory – not the glory of thrones and armies but the glory of a God who loves to the end, to the uttermost, to the cross.

The new commandment is not an addendum. It is the mission statement. Love one another as I have loved you. Not as you love yourself – that standard was good enough for Sinai. Now the standard is Calvary. And if the watching world cannot see that love in the church, they will never believe the gospel we preach.

John 13 asks every disciple the same question Peter was asked at the basin: Will you let Jesus serve you? Will you accept the scandalous reversal of a God who kneels? And then – having been washed, having been loved, having been commissioned – will you pick up the towel yourself?

A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another: just as I have loved you, you also are to love one another. – John 13:34

Restoration Movement Connections

  1. Foot Washing as Pattern and Practice – John 13:14-15 (“you also ought to wash one another’s feet”) has been understood in the Restoration Movement both as a binding ordinance and as a governing principle. Whether practiced literally or applied as a paradigm for servant leadership, the passage demands that churches embody the downward mobility of Jesus. Congregations that argue about authority structures should start by arguing about who gets to serve.

  2. The New Commandment as the Church’s Identity – The Restoration Movement has rightly emphasized doctrinal fidelity, but John 13:35 insists that the church’s identity is visible through its love. A church can have the correct name on the sign, the correct frequency of communion, and the correct mode of baptism – and still fail the test of discipleship if it does not love. The new commandment is not an elective; it is the final exam.

  3. Baptism and the Foot Washing – The foot washing’s imagery of cleansing and new identity parallels the Restoration Movement’s emphasis on baptism as the moment of dying and rising with Christ (Rom 6:3-4). Just as Jesus said, “Unless I wash you, you have no share with me” (v. 8), so baptism is the moment of participation in Christ’s death and resurrection. The foot washing and baptism both insist: cleansing is received, not achieved.

  4. The Purity of the Lord’s Table – Judas’s presence at the table and departure into the night has long informed Restoration Movement discussions about who should partake of the Lord’s Supper. John 13 provides realism: Jesus himself shared the table with his betrayer. The table is for the broken, the struggling, and the failing – not only for the perfected. The church must hold together the call to purity (1 Cor 11:27-29) and the reality that Jesus washed Judas’s feet too.

  5. Zeal Tempered by Self-Knowledge – Peter’s boast and subsequent failure is a cautionary tale for a movement built on bold conviction. The Restoration Movement’s founding figures (Campbell, Stone, Lipscomb) were courageous reformers, but courage without self-awareness leads to the kind of denominational arrogance the movement was founded to oppose. Peter’s story teaches that the most passionate disciple still needs the rooster’s crow – and the Savior’s restoration.
