Word Study, Story Context, Misconceptions, and Jesus' Vision for Marriage

Purpose: An expansive reference for careful Bible study, not a sermon outline or a collection of arguments to use against people. Scripture is the authority; Gagnon is the requested principal scholarly guide, not an additional source of revelation.

Source transparency: I consulted the publisher's authorized excerpt and contents of The Bible and Homosexual Practice: Texts and Hermeneutics (Abingdon, 2001), together with Gagnon's own accessible companion argument. I did not access the complete book or a Logos copy. Book-chapter pointers below identify where to study further, not a claim that every chapter was read. Some supplementary author-site material was available only as indexed excerpts; that is identified in the sources. No unverified book-page quotations are supplied.1

1

The central question and a working conclusion

Central question: How do Scripture's account of creation, Jesus' teaching, and the relevant prohibitions fit together, and how should Christians apply them truthfully and lovingly today?

Working interpretation: The traditional reading, developed strongly by Gagnon, understands male-female marriage as the biblical setting for sexual union and reads the relevant prohibitions as broader than rape, prostitution, or adult exploitation of boys. On this reading, Christians are called to fidelity within that marriage covenant and chastity outside it. This is a theological conclusion from a cumulative reading, not the dictionary definition of one Greek word.

The principal disagreement: Affirming interpreters argue that the condemned practices and their ancient assumptions do not settle the moral status of faithful, mutual same-sex marriages today. Their strongest case is about historical context and theological application, not simply that the Bible contains no negative references to same-sex acts.

What must remain clear: A judgment about sexual conduct is not a judgment that gay or lesbian people are less human, less worthy of protection, or beyond God's love. Genesis 1:26–27, the love of neighbor, and the gospel's call to humility apply throughout this discussion, not merely in a disclaimer at the end.

Distinguish four questions before beginning

  1. Attraction: What desires does a person experience, including desires not deliberately chosen?
  2. Orientation: How does a person describe an enduring pattern of attraction?
  3. Conduct and relationships: What does a person do, and what commitments shape that conduct?
  4. Identity: How does a person understand and name their life, belonging, and discipleship?

The biblical writers discuss desires, acts, persons, and relationships, but do not organize their teaching around today's entire vocabulary of orientation and identity. Do not read a modern identity category automatically into every ancient vice-list term. Conversely, the absence of the modern term does not prove the absence of a moral judgment about acts.

Teaching rule: Ask what a passage actually addresses before deciding how it applies to a person sitting in front of you.

How Gagnon's framework helps, and where to test it

His basic approach

Gagnon's book moves through the Old Testament, early Jewish interpretation, Jesus, Paul, and the contemporary relevance of those witnesses. That arrangement is important: he does not treat this as a dispute that can be settled by Sodom alone or by a single English translation.2

Gagnon argues that a study of the Old Testament must move beyond the Sodom account.

Robert A. J. Gagnon, The Bible and Homosexual Practice, chapter 1 opening, authorized publisher excerpt, PDF-viewer page 5, not verified printed-book pagination.3

His companion essay places the discussion within Christ's authority and the transformation of the whole person. Desire is not, for him, its own final moral authority. This is a claim about every disciple's life, not permission to scrutinize one group while excusing the rest of the church.4

Use this framework critically

  • Separate the text's wording, Gagnon's interpretation, and our contemporary application.
  • Do not assume every argument in a substantial scholarly book is equally strong.
  • His cumulative creation argument is not identical to proving that every individual passage independently condemns every conceivable same-sex relationship.
  • Historical reconstruction can illuminate a passage without becoming an additional verse of Scripture.
  • The book appeared in 2001. This resource does not adopt its era's psychological, genetic, or treatment claims as current medical evidence. The requested task is biblical and theological.

Word study: Hebrew

A. יָדַע (yādaʿ): “know”

Relevant texts: Genesis 19:5, 8; compare Genesis 4:1.

  • The verb commonly means to know; in some contexts it is a euphemism for sexual relations.
  • In Genesis 19, the threatening crowd, Lot's response, and his reference to his daughters' sexual inexperience make the sexual sense compelling.
  • A word's most frequent dictionary sense does not override the sentence and story in which it appears.

Avoid: “They only wanted to get acquainted with the visitors.” That fails to account for the narrative's coercion and threat.

Also avoid: Inferring the enduring sexual orientation of every member of the mob. The text depicts what they demand and attempt, not a psychological profile of each man.

B. זָכָר (zākār): “male”

Relevant texts: Leviticus 18:22; 20:13; Genesis 1:27.

  • This is a sex-category term, not a word that inherently means “boy,” “prostitute,” or “abuser.”
  • Age or social status must come from context, not be imported into the noun.
  • Therefore, the claim that the Hebrew simply says “do not sleep with a little boy” is not a straightforward translation of the word present.5

C. מִשְׁכְּבֵי אִשָּׁה (miškĕvē ʾiššâ): “the lyings of a woman”

Relevant texts: Leviticus 18:22; 20:13.

  • This unusual Hebrew construction is commonly rendered idiomatically as lying with a male “as with a woman.”
  • The standard reading concerns male-male sexual intercourse, not merely sharing furniture or sleeping quarters.
  • The grammatical construction and its precise scope have generated scholarly disagreement. A wooden word-for-word gloss is not automatically a superior translation.
  • Some narrower proposals appeal to kinship restrictions or particular forms of sexual transgression. Those require arguments from the whole clause and context; they are not established just by pointing out that the Hebrew is unusual.

Working reading: The prohibition is not explicitly restricted to a shrine, a payment, a child, or a violent encounter. That weighs against claiming that any one of those limitations is plainly stated.67

D. תּוֹעֵבָה (tôʿēbâ): “abomination,” “detestable thing”

Relevant texts: Leviticus 18:22; 20:13; compare Deuteronomy 14:3; 25:13–16; Proverbs 6:16–19.

  • This is strong language of rejection, but its range spans different kinds of prohibited objects, practices, and behavior.
  • Brown–Driver–Briggs distinguishes ritual and ethical uses. The word alone cannot decide whether a particular law is enduring, ritual, civil, or some combination.8
  • Dishonesty and other wrongs are also described with this language. It is not a special vocabulary for identifying one class of people as uniquely disgusting.

Two opposite errors:

  • “It means only a temporary ceremonial taboo, so nothing moral is involved.”
  • “It proves this is the worst imaginable sin and gives us permission to speak contemptuously of people.”

Neither follows from the word itself. In Leviticus 18:22, explain the act being prohibited rather than turning the verse into an insult directed at a person.

E. עֵזֶר כְּנֶגְדּוֹ (ʿēzer kĕnegdô) and בָּשָׂר אֶחָד (bāśār ʾeḥād)

Relevant texts: Genesis 2:18, 24.

  • ʿĒzer kĕnegdô: a helper corresponding to him, or a suitable counterpart. “Helper” does not inherently mean an inferior being; God is also called a helper elsewhere (e.g., Psalm 121:1–2).
  • Bāśār ʾeḥād: one flesh. In Genesis 2, the man and woman become a new bonded kinship unit with bodily union, not simply two people who share an interest.
  • These expressions support a relational and embodied account of marriage. A dictionary gloss by itself does not settle the entire modern debate over complementarity.

Word study: Greek

A. μαλακός / μαλακοί (malakos / malakoi): literally “soft”

Relevant texts: 1 Corinthians 6:9; compare Matthew 11:8 and Luke 7:25, where the word describes clothing.

  • In wider usage, the term could carry judgments about luxury, lack of self-control, or perceived effeminacy; a sexual use is also attested.
  • In 1 Corinthians 6:9, many interpreters understand it as the receptive male participant in male-male intercourse, especially because of its placement beside arsenokoitai.
  • Gagnon argues for that sexual reading in this context; other interpreters emphasize a broader moral insult or a narrower reference to prostitution.910
  • Do not take an old English rendering such as “effeminate” and turn it into a ban on a man's voice, gentleness, artistic interests, or failure to conform to local masculine stereotypes.

Confidence: Literal “soft” is clear; its precise referent in this vice list requires contextual interpretation.

B. ἀρσενοκοίτης / ἀρσενοκοῖται (arsenokoitēs / arsenokoitai)

Relevant texts: 1 Corinthians 6:9; 1 Timothy 1:10.

  • The compound combines the language of male (arsēn) and bed/intercourse (koitē).
  • A plausible and important background is the Greek Old Testament, the Septuagint. Leviticus 20:13 places ἄρσενος κοίτην (arsenos koitēn) together in its prohibition.11
  • Gagnon takes that connection, Paul's Jewish scriptural setting, and the surrounding sexual ethics as evidence that male-male intercourse generally is intended, not only child exploitation or paid sex.12
  • The term is rare, and its exact extension is disputed. Some scholars argue for exploitative sexual practices rather than a comprehensive category of same-sex relations.[^lexham]

Method matters: Etymology provides evidence, not an automatic verdict. Usage, textual setting, and likely scriptural echoes must work together. Neither “the roots prove everything” nor “a rare word means we know nothing” is adequate.

Working assessment: The male-male sexual reference has substantial support; restricting it with certainty to “child molester” is not warranted by the word alone. The further question of its application to contemporary same-sex marriage remains part of the interpretive dispute.

C. Translation comparison: 1 Corinthians 6:9

Version Wording for the two terms What to notice
KJV “effeminate”; “abusers of themselves with mankind” Older English requires explanation, not automatic transfer into current categories.
NIV “men who have sex with men” Combines the two words; its note explains them through the two sexual roles.
NRSVue “male prostitutes”; “men who engage in illicit sex” Keeps separate renderings and marks the Greek meaning uncertain in footnotes.

These differences show translation decisions. The broad NIV rendering does not prove the NRSVue committee dishonest; the NRSVue footnotes do not prove the NIV impossible. Examine the evidence behind each.13

D. παρὰ φύσιν (para physin): “contrary to nature”

Relevant texts: Romans 1:26; compare Romans 11:24.

  • Physis concerns nature, constitution, or an understood order; context determines its force.
  • In Romans 1, the expression appears in a negative account of exchanged worship and exchanged sexual relations.
  • In Romans 11:24, Paul uses the same phrase positively for God's extraordinary grafting of Gentiles. Therefore the phrase does not mean “sinful” in every sentence where it occurs.
  • That positive use does not make the acts in Romans 1 morally positive: their immediate context remains negative.

Brownson argues that ancient understandings of nature included bodily realities, social expectations, and individual dispositions. His interpretive challenge is whether all of Paul's ancient assumptions map onto present relationships.14

Working assessment: The Creator/creation context makes a creation-order reading of Romans 1 substantial. Reducing the expression to either “what feels natural to me” or “whatever society happens to prefer” does not adequately explain Paul's argument.

E. πόρνεια (porneia): “sexual immorality”

Relevant texts: Matthew 15:19; 19:9; Mark 7:21; Acts 15:20, 29; 1 Corinthians 6:18.

  • A broad term for illicit sexual activity, not a synonym for every kind of sexual desire.
  • What counts as illicit must be established from a writer's ethical setting and teaching.
  • Traditional interpreters understand Jesus' Jewish sexual framework to include the Levitical restrictions. Affirming interpreters dispute the proposed scope and contemporary application.

Avoid circular reasoning: “It is porneia because it is wrong, and we know it is wrong because it is porneia.” Explain the supporting passages and moral reasoning.

F. σαρκὸς ἑτέρας (sarkos heteras): “other flesh”

Relevant text: Jude 7.

  • The phrase is commonly translated with language such as strange flesh or unnatural desire.
  • In context, the proximity of rebellious angels in verse 6 gives an angel-human boundary reading real force.
  • Other interpreters emphasize forbidden sexual relations more broadly, including the same-sex aspect of the Sodom episode.
  • It is not the Greek expression for a modern sexual orientation. See the Sodom section below.15

Story context: Sodom is not a shortcut

Genesis 18–19: the narrative movement

  1. Hospitality: Abraham receives visitors generously in chapter 18; Lot receives the visitors in chapter 19.
  2. Prior wickedness: The outcry against the cities is already serious before the mob arrives (18:20–21).
  3. Intercession: Abraham asks whether God will judge the righteous with the wicked (18:23–33).
  4. Threatened assault: A mob demands access to Lot's male visitors and threatens further violence (19:4–9).
  5. Intervention and judgment: The visitors protect Lot, and the family is directed to flee.

The episode depicts attempted group sexual assault, domination, and a violation of the protection owed to vulnerable guests. Calling it “inhospitality” is accurate only if we remember this is violent abuse of strangers, not a failure to offer refreshments.

Lot's daughters must not disappear from the discussion

Lot offers his daughters in place of the guests. The narrative reports this; it does not make it a righteous model. Do not imply that assaulting women would have solved the moral problem. Hospitality cannot be defended by sacrificing other vulnerable people.

The canonical cross-check

  • Ezekiel 16:49–50: Sodom's pride, abundance, comfortable indifference, failure to help the poor, and abominations appear in a rebuke aimed at Jerusalem.
  • Isaiah 1:10–17: Sodom language is applied to a worshiping society that must confront injustice and bloodshed.
  • Matthew 10:14–15: Jesus invokes Sodom when speaking of rejection of his messengers.
  • Jude 6–7: Sodom also serves as an example of sexual transgression and judgment; the visitors' angelic identity matters to the interpretive debate.
  • Judges 19: A parallel threat against a male guest culminates in the horrific abuse of a woman. The narrative exposes Israel's collapse, not a permissible alternative sexual ethic.

Gagnon and the alternative reading

Gagnon's position: He argues that the same-sex dimension belongs to the narrative's moral indictment as well as its violence. In his own response to critics, he points readers to his book's Sodom discussion, pp. 71–78. This is an author-supplied locator, not a claim that those book pages were directly consulted here.16

Alternative emphasis: Matthew Vines argues that the episode's only depicted same-sex act is attempted rape; it cannot by itself establish a judgment about mutual same-sex relationships. He also reads Jude through its angelic context.17

Study assessment: State the assault clearly and acknowledge the broader canonical picture. Do not equate a loving same-sex couple with Sodom's mob. Even if one accepts Gagnon's broader moral inference, the case for a comprehensive sexual ethic must be established from other texts as well.

Question: If we use Sodom to discuss sexual sin but ignore arrogance, cruelty, and neglect of the poor in our own congregation, have we actually listened to Scripture?

Leviticus 18:22 and 20:13: holiness and covenant

Read the chapters, not only the clauses

Leviticus 18 introduces its prohibitions by contrasting Israel's calling with Egypt and Canaan. Its boundaries include incest, adultery, and other prohibited practices. The closing verses describe defilement of the land and judgment on the nations. Leviticus 20 includes sanctions within Israel's covenant order.

Textual observations:

  • The male-male prohibition itself contains no explicit qualifier restricting it to prostitution, idolatrous ceremonies, rape, or boys.
  • The neighboring mention of Molech does not grammatically make every nearby sexual prohibition a temple ritual.
  • The text speaks directly about males; it does not explicitly address female-female relations in these verses.
  • Leviticus 20:13 assigns responsibility to both participants. This complicates a reading that assumes only an adult perpetrator and a blameless child are intended.

Interpretive question: What accounts for the prohibition? Possibilities discussed include creation order, sex-role boundaries, holiness distinctions, and more restricted sexual contexts. The traditional argument locates it in God's ordering of sexual union; some alternative readings emphasize ancient social assumptions. Naming a cultural context does not by itself prove either permanent validity or obsolescence.18

How does Torah apply to Christians?

Neither of these shortcuts is sufficient:

  • “Leviticus says it, so every sanction applies to the church unchanged.”
  • “Christians do not keep Israel's food laws, so no sexual teaching in Leviticus can matter.”

Consider Acts 15; Romans 7:4–6; Galatians 3:23–29; 1 Corinthians 5–6; and Hebrews 8. The New Testament distinguishes the church's covenant situation while continuing to teach sexual holiness. The traditional case therefore depends on continuity through Jesus and the apostles, not merely on importing the whole civil code.

Crucial pastoral boundary: Leviticus' capital sanction is not a church instruction to threaten, injure, or kill people. The church's New Testament practices are teaching, repentance, accountable fellowship, and restoration, not carrying out Israel's civil penalties. See 1 Corinthians 5:12–13 and Galatians 6:1.

Question: Can we explain why we carry a moral teaching forward without claiming that every feature of Israel's legal administration is ours to impose?

8

Jesus' vision for marriage and the kingdom

Matthew 19:3–12 and Mark 10:2–12

Immediate question: Jesus is being challenged about divorce, not asked directly about same-sex marriage.

What Jesus does: He brings together Genesis 1:27 and Genesis 2:24, speaking of male and female, the man and his wife, their one-flesh union, and God's joining. He contrasts creation's intention with concessions made because of hard hearts.

Gagnon's inference: Male-female correspondence is foundational, not incidental, to Jesus' reasoning about marital union. His later writing explicitly connects that foundation to marital exclusivity and permanence.19

Affirming challenge: Does Jesus' use of the male-female marriage in the question establish an exhaustive rule about every possible marriage, or does it primarily establish fidelity within the marriages under discussion? That challenge must be answered through the combined scriptural argument, not by claiming Jesus uttered a sentence he did not.

Working judgment: Jesus gives strong positive support to the male-female creation pattern. This is a better starting point for the traditional ethic than pretending that Jesus explicitly discussed contemporary same-sex marriages. Neither his silence on that exact question nor his welcome of marginalized people proves endorsement of every form of sexual relationship.20

Jesus also resists making marriage everyone's destiny

  • Matthew 19:10–12 recognizes lives outside ordinary marriage expectations, including renunciation for the kingdom.
  • Do not identify all ancient eunuchs with modern gay people, or treat the passage as an instruction to mutilate the body.
  • First Corinthians 7 honors unmarried service to the Lord and marriage alike.
  • Resurrection life is not organized around marriage in the same way as the present age (Matthew 22:30).

Theological implication: Marriage is good, but marriage and sexual experience are not prerequisites for full humanity or belonging to Christ. A church cannot teach celibacy responsibly while treating unmarried people as incomplete adults.

Creation must not be reduced to reproductive capacity

Genesis connects male-female humanity with fruitfulness, but Scripture does not make each couple's ability to conceive the test of whether they are married. Infertile couples are not failed marriages. The traditional argument concerns the kind of union, not a requirement that every act or every marriage produce children.

Love and holiness belong in the same account

  • Love of God and neighbor: Matthew 22:34–40.
  • Heart-level sexual integrity: Matthew 5:27–30.
  • Mercy rather than self-righteous exclusion: Luke 18:9–14.
  • Faithfulness and costly discipleship: Luke 9:23.

Gagnon argues that grace and demanding moral teaching belong together, including in sexual ethics.

Gagnon, “The Bible and Homosexual Practice: Theology, Analogies, and Genes,” section “Light from Paul.” This summary is from the companion essay, not the book.21

Application: If we invoke Jesus to challenge another person's sexual life, we must also receive his challenge to our lust, marital unfaithfulness, contempt, and pride.

Romans 1:24–27: the strongest sustained NT text

Follow Paul's argument into chapter 2

  • 1:18–23: Humanity suppresses truth and exchanges the Creator's glory for created images.
  • 1:24–27: God gives people over; bodily and sexual dishonor illustrate disorder associated with that exchange.
  • 1:28–32: The indictment expands to many forms of wrongdoing, including greed, envy, deceit, and slander.
  • 2:1–5: The judging reader is confronted with personal guilt and the call to repentance.
  • 3:9–26: Jews and Gentiles alike need the righteousness God provides through Christ.

Do not stop reading at the point where someone else seems condemned. Paul's rhetorical movement does not cancel his moral judgments; it removes the reader's grounds for superiority.

What is explicit?

  1. Paul speaks negatively about the sexual behavior he describes.
  2. Male-male sexual activity is explicit in verse 27; the men desire one another.
  3. Verse 26 refers to women exchanging natural relations for those contrary to nature. In parallel with verse 27, it is widely understood as female-female activity. The partner is less explicitly identified than in verse 27; some interpreters propose other non-procreative practices.
  4. Neither verse names a commercial transaction, an age gap, a temple ceremony, or coercion as the limiting condition.

Why the traditional reading is substantial

The passage's Creator/creation framework, male-female terminology, and parallel descriptions of women and men support a reading broader than adult male exploitation of boys. Mutual desire also makes “only rape” an inadequate description of what Paul actually says. These observations do not depend on how arsenokoitai is translated, since that word is not used here.22

The strongest affirming challenge

Brownson asks whether Paul's portrayal of excessive passion, dishonor, impurity, and ancient understandings of nature necessarily describes every faithful same-sex relationship today. His case concerns the moral reasoning beneath the words, not simply removing the words from the Bible.23

Brownson argues that ancient references to nature brought together several forms of perceived order; the study distinguishes his approach from its own conclusion.

James V. Brownson, “Romans 1:26–27, ‘Nature,’ and the Blindfolded Students of the Elephant.” This is a summary of his contextual approach, not the conclusion of this study.24

Assessment: The text clearly supplies a negative evaluation, but moving from an ancient description to a modern relationship requires argument. This study finds the traditional creation-based reading stronger than an exclusively exploitation-based reading: Paul's wording does not identify exploitation as the sole reason for his judgment. A faithful study should nevertheless address the affirming question seriously rather than dismiss it as mere unwillingness to read.

Claims Romans 1 does not justify

  • A biography of every gay person: Paul is giving a theological indictment of humanity, not explaining how each individual's attractions developed.
  • A claim that every gay person consciously worshiped idols before experiencing attraction: The argument is corporate and theological; it is not that individual causal story.
  • A diagnosis of a particular disease or calamity: The penalty language in verse 27 does not name HIV/AIDS or authorize identifying someone's illness as a special divine punishment.
  • A morally superior heterosexual audience: Chapter 2 dismantles self-exemption, and chapter 3 announces the need of all for grace.

Question: Can I explain Paul's moral reasoning without using the passage to diagnose a stranger's inner life or distance myself from the need for mercy?

First Corinthians 6 and First Timothy 1: conduct, cleansing, and mercy

1 Corinthians 6:9–11 in chapters 5–7

  • The church is confronting sexual wrongdoing, litigation, injustice, exploitation, bodily conduct, and questions about marriage and singleness.
  • The list in 6:9–10 is not exclusively sexual. Greed, theft, drunkenness, and verbal abuse belong to the same call to repentance.
  • The discussion continues into the believer's bodily union with Christ and the misuse of the body in 6:12–20.
  • Verse 11 celebrates washing, sanctification, and justification in Christ and the Spirit.

Baptismal connection: Washing naturally invites comparison with Acts 22:16 and Titus 3:5. In a Restoration Movement teaching setting, connect cleansing, baptism, new allegiance, and the Spirit, but do not turn this verse into a detailed description of a psychological treatment process.

What verse 11 establishes: God's grace changes people's standing, allegiance, and life. No listed past conduct makes a person beyond redemption.

What verse 11 does not establish: That everyone described became heterosexually attracted, married someone of the opposite sex, or no longer experienced temptation. The verse does not report those outcomes.

Pastoral implication: Do not make a particular change in attraction the proof that someone's baptism, repentance, or faith was genuine. Discipleship is not measured by inventing an outcome the passage never promises.

1 Timothy 1:8–17

  • The law is used properly to expose conduct contrary to sound teaching.
  • Arsenokoitai occurs alongside other categories of wrongdoing; the list alone does not independently define its precise scope.
  • Nearby reference to enslavers does not prove the sexual term means only trafficking; proximity is evidence to consider, not identity of meaning.
  • Continue into verses 12–17: the writer identifies himself as a recipient of Christ's patience and mercy, not merely an evaluator of other people's sins.

Question: Does my teaching preserve both the ethical warning and the invitation to grace, or do I preach only the half that exposes someone else?

The disagreement: strongest versions, not caricatures

Question Gagnon-guided traditional reading Affirming challenge What needs to be established
Is male-female union essential to marriage? Genesis and Jesus supply a normative embodied pattern. A depicted pattern may not exhaust the faithful forms a covenant can take. Whether the sex distinction is constitutive or incidental to the moral reasoning.
Are the prohibitions only about abuse? Their wording is broader than force, payment, or age difference. Ancient relations and assumptions differ from contemporary mutual marriages. Whether historical differences change the scope or application of the prohibition.
Does a disputed word settle everything? The Levitical echo supports a male-male sexual reference. Rare vocabulary and social conventions complicate precise translation. How word usage, intertext, and wider argument work together.
Is love enough to establish moral legitimacy? Love must be ordered by God's teaching, not merely defined by sincerity. Faithful same-sex commitments can display sacrificial love and covenant virtues. Whether those virtues satisfy all relevant biblical criteria for sexual union.
What about celibacy? Sexual faithfulness outside marriage is part of discipleship; the church must support it concretely. Requiring lifelong celibacy of an entire group raises questions about 1 Corinthians 7 and vocation. How calling, gift, moral boundaries, companionship, and pastoral responsibility fit together.

These are descriptions of arguments, not judgments about the motives of everyone holding them. Neither side should assume that disagreement proves hatred or that an appeal to love removes the need for exegesis. Brownson's own interview explicitly argues for sacrificial, other-directed love rather than mere permission to do whatever one wants.25

Misconceptions and misuse

“Sodom proves that every gay person is like a violent predator.”

No. The narrative describes threatened violence. Sexual orientation does not make a person a rapist, and consensual adult relationships must not be equated with abuse. A traditional sexual ethic needs no such equation.

“Sodom was only about bad hospitality, with no sexual dimension.”

Also inadequate. The sexual threat is central to the scene, and Jude includes sexual transgression. Read violence, injustice, hospitality, and the contested boundary question together.

“The Bible originally said ‘boy,’ but translators changed it to ‘man.’”

The Hebrew zākār means male, not specifically boy. Arguments about pederasty depend on context and history; they are not a simple correction of that Hebrew noun. The Greek compound likewise does not contain the word for boy.

“The term ‘homosexual’ appeared in modern translations, so the interpretation was invented recently.”

Modern terminology deserves scrutiny, but the underlying debate and negative readings of male-male acts long predate that English label. The KJV's older phrasing itself demonstrates this. A history of translation can expose anachronism without proving that the ancient authors approved the acts in question.

“The Greek is perfectly clear, so there is nothing to discuss.”

Some aspects are clearer than others. Male-male activity in Romans 1:27 is explicit; the precise force of malakoi is less straightforward. Calibrate confidence passage by passage.

“The Greek is disputed, so Scripture says nothing meaningful.”

Uncertainty about one term does not erase the surrounding argument or the other passages. It calls for more careful reasoning.

“Jesus never mentioned homosexuality, so he approved it.”

No recorded direct statement is not proof of approval. His teaching on creation, marriage, sexual integrity, and discipleship remains relevant. Equally, do not fabricate a direct saying of Jesus on the modern question.

“Jesus' reference to eunuchs is an explicit blessing of same-sex marriage.”

The passage does not say that. It does challenge an assumption that everyone must marry or fit ordinary expectations of family life to belong in the kingdom.

“David and Jonathan, Ruth and Naomi, or the centurion and his servant settle the question.”

These texts can invite discussions of affection, loyalty, power, and intimacy, but they do not explicitly narrate same-sex intercourse or establish same-sex marriage. Neither deep same-sex friendship nor the word pais in the centurion story automatically proves an erotic partnership. Avoid assigning sexual identities the narratives do not supply. See 1 Samuel 18:1–4; 2 Samuel 1:26; Ruth 1:16–17; Matthew 8:5–13; Luke 7:1–10.

“Repentance guarantees heterosexual attraction.”

The relevant texts do not promise that. Do not pressure someone to marry as a cure, require public disclosure of private attractions, or treat enduring attraction as proof of deceit. A voluntary commitment to celibacy is different from a program promising to change orientation.

“Defending biblical marriage means gay people deserve mistreatment.”

No. Insults, threats, outing, humiliation, and coercion are not fruits of faithful exegesis. Christian conviction must not become an excuse to deny another person's dignity or protection from violence.

“Welcoming someone requires pretending we have no convictions.”

Hospitality, friendship, listening, and practical care are not identical to agreement about every moral question. Be transparent about congregational teaching and expectations; do not offer friendship as bait and withdraw ordinary human care when a person disagrees.

“Only this sexual question deserves serious church discipline.”

First Corinthians confronts greed, exploitation, drunkenness, and reviling as well as sexual wrongdoing. A congregation that ignores adultery, abuse, financial misconduct, or humiliating speech has not earned credibility by being vocal on this one subject.

13

Pastoral and theological synthesis

A. Begin with persons, not a category

Ask what the person means by their terms, what they believe, and what they are seeking. Do not assume that a gay identity label tells you someone's conduct, theology, or willingness to follow Jesus. Let them describe their own experience without demanding unnecessary intimate details.

B. Teach one demanding ethic, not one demanding exception

Under the working traditional reading, fidelity, chastity, truthfulness, and repentance apply to straight and gay disciples. That does not mean every person's cost is identical. Listen honestly to the particular losses and difficulties a teaching may entail.

C. Make church family tangible

If a church commends celibacy, it must cultivate enduring friendship, shared meals, mutual care, meaningful responsibility, and inclusion across life stages. “Do not marry” accompanied by “now manage alone” is not an adequate account of the body of Christ. Consider Mark 3:31–35; 1 Corinthians 12:12–27; Galatians 6:2.

D. Keep accountability restorative

A person seeking help should not be made into a sermon illustration without consent. Address conduct with the same seriousness and fairness used elsewhere. Galatians 6:1 joins restoration, gentleness, and self-watchfulness.

E. Distinguish the book's argument from the church's tone

You can learn from Gagnon's textual and cumulative arguments without reproducing every polemical phrase or adopting unsupported claims about an individual's psychology. Let biblical neighbor-love govern how the argument is taught.

F. Do not make agreement with a scholar the gospel

The center is Jesus Christ, his death and resurrection, repentance, faith, baptism, the Spirit, and a life of discipleship. Sexual ethics belongs within that life; neither political allegiance nor mastery of a debate replaces it.

Original teaching summary: Be clear about the text, honest about the interpretive steps, and accountable for the way you treat the person.

14

A practical 50–60 minute teaching route

  1. Dignity and definitions (5 minutes): Genesis 1:26–27; distinguish attraction, orientation, conduct, and identity. Establish that no participant is a case study for the room.
  2. Jesus' positive vision (10 minutes): Genesis 2:18–25; Matthew 19:3–12. Include singleness, not marriage alone.
  3. Sodom in context (10 minutes): Genesis 19:4–9; Ezekiel 16:49–50; Jude 6–7. Distinguish narrative observation from broader moral inference.
  4. Words and law (10 minutes): Leviticus 18:22; 20:13; malakoi and arsenokoitai. Use the comparison table and explain why covenant context matters.
  5. Romans and grace (10 minutes): Romans 1:24–27; 2:1–4; 3:23–24; 1 Corinthians 6:9–11. Never end with condemnation detached from mercy.
  6. Response and care (5–15 minutes): What does faithful, truthful, non-coercive discipleship look like for this congregation?

Questions for the group

  • Which conclusion comes directly from the wording, and which requires an additional theological argument?
  • Have we used Sodom to avoid confronting our own pride or treatment of vulnerable people?
  • How does Jesus challenge both casual marriage and the assumption that everyone must marry?
  • What is the strongest argument from someone who disagrees with our interpretation?
  • Where do we need repentance in our speech and treatment of people, regardless of our sexual-ethics position?
  • Would an unmarried person find actual family in our congregation?
